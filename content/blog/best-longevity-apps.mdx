Every longevity app sells you the same thing: a number that is supposed to be your real age. Whoop calls it WHOOP Age. Oura calls it Cardiovascular Age. Apple will call it Health Age. NOVOS, Elysium, Function Health and Superpower all call it biological age. The pitch is identical, the price ranges from free to 849 dollars, and almost every comparison you will read ranks them on features.

This one ranks them on something more useful: **how much you can trust the number.** Because the uncomfortable finding, published in 2026 and largely absent from the marketing, is that most of these scores are far less stable than they look.

## The problem with every biological age score

Two peer-reviewed papers frame this honestly, and they should shape how you read any result.

The first is **Higgins-Chen et al., *Nature Aging*, 2022**. The researchers ran the same blood sample through standard epigenetic clocks twice. The biological age estimates came back differing by **up to nine years**. Their own framing: it would be misleading for a clock to say a person has aged nine years when the difference is solely technical variation. They proposed a fix — principal-component recalibration, the basis of what vendors now call PC clocks — which brought most replicate pairs within 1 to 1.5 years.<Cite id="higgins-chen-2022-pc-clocks" />

The second is worse, and newer. **Sehgal et al., *Aging Cell*, 2026** separated *technical* reliability from *biological* reliability. The technical side holds up well: most clocks reached excellent technical reliability (ICC above 0.9), and even older ones such as Hannum and PhenoAge sat around ICC 0.7 to 0.8. But across repeat samples taken around ordinary perturbations — a meal, an acute stress test, air pollution — most clocks managed only **ICC 0.4 to 0.7**, and after a single meal many fell into the moderate or poor range. DunedinPACE and GrimAge fell into the poor range under stress. And critically, the two kinds of reliability were **essentially uncorrelated**. A vendor citing an impressive technical reliability figure is not telling you your score will be stable from one test to the next. The authors' own warning: if a meal can dramatically impact a score, a before-and-after study that ignores fasting can mistake that shift for an intervention effect.<Cite id="sehgal-2026-clock-reliability" />

Hold that in mind through everything below. It does not make these products useless. It does mean that **a single reading is an estimate with a wide error bar, and a trend over years is worth far more than any one number** — which is the same thing that is true of a cholesterol panel, and the reason we keep saying that two points is a guess. If you want the underlying idea before spending anything, [biological age versus chronological age](/blog/biological-age-vs-chronological-age) covers what these scores are trying to capture, and you can compute a PhenoAge from a standard blood panel for nothing with the free [PhenoAge calculator](/tools/phenoage-calculator).

## How we picked

We looked for products that produce an aging or healthspan score, are available to a US buyer, and come with an app or dashboard you actually use. For each one we went to the company's own pricing and science pages rather than to affiliate listicles, and we checked every price in **September 2026**, then re-checked each one in October 2026. Where a company's own pages contradicted each other, we say so instead of picking the flattering number. Where we could not verify something, we say that too.

We also asked a question most comparisons skip: **is the algorithm published?** For almost all of them, the answer is no.

## Wearables that estimate an age

These compute an age from movement, heart rate and sleep. They see your habits daily but have no access to your blood chemistry.

### Whoop — the most transparent method

Whoop's **Healthspan** feature contains two numbers: **WHOOP Age**, a physiological age in years, and **Pace of Aging**, a rate on a scale from −1x to 3x. It is built from nine inputs: sleep duration, sleep consistency, steps, time in heart-rate zones 1 to 3, time in zones 4 to 5, strength-activity time, VO2 max, resting heart rate and lean body mass percentage.<Cite id="whoop-healthspan" /> Each is mapped to published hazard ratios for all-cause mortality, then converted into effective years using a Gompertz mortality model.<Cite id="whoop-2025-healthspan-white-paper" />

That is more method disclosure than anyone else in this list offers, and Whoop publishes a white paper. But the algorithm itself has **no peer-reviewed publication**. Its only validation is an internal member survey. A fair objection: hazard ratios come with confidence intervals, and Whoop's white paper reports none, which gives the output a precision it has not earned.<Cite id="whoop-2025-healthspan-white-paper" />

**Price (October 2026):** Whoop One 199 dollars a year, but it does **not** include Healthspan. Whoop Peak 239 dollars a year and Whoop Life 359 dollars a year both do. Hardware is included in the membership price.<Cite id="whoop-membership" /> Healthspan is not available to users under 18.<Cite id="whoop-healthspan" />

### Oura — the closest thing to validation, and an overstated headline

Oura reports **Cardiovascular Age**, not a general biological age. It works by reading the shape of the overnight PPG waveform and using it as a proxy for pulse wave velocity, the standard measure of arterial stiffness.<Cite id="van-bortel-2012-cfpwv" /> The ring does not measure pulse wave velocity directly. You need a Gen3 ring or newer and at least 14 nights of data in the past 30.<Cite id="oura-cardiovascular-age" />

There is a real paper here — Yilmaz et al., *PLOS Digital Health*, March 2026 — and Oura's blog headlines it as validation.<Cite id="oura-2026-nus-vascular-age" /> Read the paper and the claim softens considerably: the researchers built **their own** model on Oura PPG data, with 160 healthy adults at a median age of 31, reaching a mean absolute error of **7.25 years**. They did not test Oura's shipping algorithm.<Cite id="yilmaz-2026-vascular-age" /> So the concept has academic support; the product's own implementation remains unvalidated, and a 7-year error bar is not a small thing when the output is an age.

**Price (October 2026):** Oura Ring 5 from 399 dollars, Ring 4 still sold from 349 dollars, plus membership at 5.99 dollars a month or 69.99 a year.<Cite id="oura-store" /><Cite id="oura-membership" /> Cardiovascular Age requires the membership.<Cite id="oura-cardiovascular-age" />

### Apple Health Age — free, and about to change the pricing of this whole category

Apple announced **Health Age** on 9 September 2026, inside a new Longevity tab in the redesigned Health app. It draws on Apple Watch metrics such as VO2 max, resting heart rate, sleep and HRV, plus **optional lab values such as A1c and LDL cholesterol that you add yourself**, which is a notable acknowledgement that wrist data alone does not capture metabolic aging.<Cite id="apple-2026-health-age" />

Two things to be clear about. It **had not shipped** as of mid-September 2026: Apple says it arrives later in 2026 with iOS 27, and it needs an Apple Watch and an Apple Intelligence-capable iPhone. And Apple cites no studies for the algorithm, only the phrase "informed by the best available science" — so it lands in exactly the same unvalidated bucket as the others, just with no announced fee.<Cite id="apple-2026-health-age" />

That matters strategically. If it stays free, a first-party age score built into the operating system puts real pressure on subscriptions in the 239 to 359 dollar range.

### Garmin Fitness Age — the honest outlier

Garmin's **Fitness Age** is derived from your VO2 max estimate, with newer devices also factoring in activity intensity, resting heart rate, and body fat percentage or BMI.<Cite id="garmin-2025-fitness-age" /> Garmin cites no validation studies either — but Garmin also never claims this is a longevity or biological age. It is a fitness comparison against your age group, it is free, and it does not pretend to be more. Worth including precisely because it is the one product here that does not oversell.

## Epigenetic tests with an app

These read DNA methylation from a blood spot, swab or saliva sample. They measure a different biological layer than a wearable — and they are where the reliability caveats above bite hardest.

### NOVOS Age — the best value, and the most honest about retesting

NOVOS runs an Illumina MethylationEPIC array across roughly 850,000 CpG sites and returns a biological age, **DunedinPACE**, organ ages across 11 systems and telomere length. It is the only product in this group with a genuine mobile app on both iOS and Android.<Cite id="novos-age" />

What earns it the top slot here is a line in its own marketing: NOVOS tells customers to **wait 6 to 12 months** before retesting, because testing sooner "tends to show measurement noise rather than real change."<Cite id="novos-age" /> A company arguing against selling you more tests is a signal worth paying attention to.

**Price (October 2026):** 349 dollars for a single kit, 299 per kit for two or more. No subscription required. Finger-prick blood.<Cite id="novos-age" />

### Elysium Index — built on the reliability fix

Index reports a biological age, a pace of aging and **nine system ages**, from a saliva sample.<Cite id="elysium-index" /> Elysium cites the Higgins-Chen 2022 paper directly as its reliability basis, describing its own computational fix for technical noise and claiming replicate agreement within 0 to 1.5 years against 3 to 9 years for other published clocks.<Cite id="elysium-science-behind-index" />

Whether that claim holds independently is not something we can verify, but it is at least the right paper to be citing, and Elysium carries the most explicit disclaimer of the group: Index is not a medical device and is not intended to screen, diagnose, treat, cure or prevent anything.<Cite id="elysium-index" />

**Price (October 2026):** 299 dollars, single kit, no subscription option. Results in about six weeks.<Cite id="elysium-index" />

### TruDiagnostic TruAge — the deepest panel, the most aggressive retesting

TruAge is the most extensive report here: **OMICmAge** (Harvard), **SYMPHONYAge** (Yale, 11 organ-system ages), **DunedinPACE** (Duke and Columbia), telomere length and 75-plus longevity biomarkers, from a finger-stick blood sample.<Cite id="trudiagnostic-about-truage" /><Cite id="trudiagnostic-truage" />

The reservation is commercial rather than scientific. TruDiagnostic markets retesting **every 3 to 6 months**.<Cite id="trudiagnostic-truage" /> Set that against the *Aging Cell* finding that a meal or a stressful day moves these clocks, and against NOVOS telling its own customers to wait 6 to 12 months, and a three-month retest cycle sits at or below the noise floor.

**Price (October 2026):** 499 dollars for TruAge, 849 for the TruAge plus TruHealth combination.<Cite id="trudiagnostic-truage-truhealth" /> Web portal, no consumer mobile app that we could find.

### Tally Health — cheapest entry, now under the same roof as TruDiagnostic

Tally Health uses a **cheek swab** rather than blood, run against a proprietary in-house clock trained on more than 8,000 adults.<Cite id="shokhirev-2024-cheekage" /> It is the cheapest way into epigenetic testing at 249 dollars a kit, 234 for members.<Cite id="tally-test-kit" />

One fact worth knowing if you are comparing options: Tally Health was **acquired in April 2026 by Infinite Epigenetics, the parent company of TruDiagnostic**.<Cite id="infinite-epigenetics-2026-tally" /> They are no longer independent alternatives to each other.

**Price (October 2026):** 249 dollars per kit, or a 129 dollar monthly membership that includes two tests a year and a daily supplement.<Cite id="tally-membership" />

### A note on GlycanAge

GlycanAge is frequently listed alongside the above, but it is **not an epigenetic test**. It measures IgG glycosylation — the sugar chains on your circulating antibodies — as a marker of inflammatory state, sometimes called inflammaging.<Cite id="cindric-2024-glycans-inflammaging" /><Cite id="glycanage-cost-faq" /> That is a real and interesting signal, but it is a different biological layer and cannot be benchmarked against a methylation clock. Its FAQ quotes an approximate US range of 499 to 549 dollars for a single test.<Cite id="glycanage-cost-faq" />

## Blood panel platforms with a biological age

These run a full lab panel and fold an aging score into it. They see the chemistry a wearable cannot.

### Function Health — 365 dollars a year, the volume leader

Function Health runs **160-plus lab tests a year** across two draws, phlebotomy included at Quest locations, and includes a **Biological Age** score tested twice a year.<Cite id="function-biological-age" /> You can upload past results. HSA and FSA eligible.<Cite id="function-faq" /><Cite id="function-pricing" />

The caveat is disclosure: Function does not publish how its Biological Age is computed. The page renders the methodology as a placeholder.<Cite id="function-biological-age" /> Not available in Rhode Island, Hawaii or Puerto Rico, and testing in New York and New Jersey can cost more.<Cite id="function-faq" />

### Superpower — one membership, 349 dollars a year

Superpower now lists a single **membership at 349 dollars a year** (150-plus biomarkers across a baseline draw and a retest). It returns a biological age plus 17 health scores, accepts uploaded past labs, and syncs Oura, Whoop and Apple Health.<Cite id="superpower-pricing" />

For completeness, since it circulated widely and is usually reported backwards: in January 2026 **Function Health sued Superpower**, not the other way round, alleging false advertising. **No court has found that Superpower did anything wrong.** We mention it only because the rumour usually reaches people inverted.

### InsideTracker — the one built for labs you already have

InsideTracker sells the analysis layer separately from the blood, which makes it unusual. A **149 dollar annual membership** exists specifically so you can upload existing results from another provider — up to 30 result sets a year.<Cite id="insidetracker-membership" /> Its aging score is **InnerAge**, 249 dollars standalone or 99 as an add-on for existing members.<Cite id="insidetracker-innerage" /> A full Ultimate test covers up to 54 biomarkers.<Cite id="insidetracker-store" />

Two things to check before buying: InsideTracker's own pages disagree on the biomarker count (54 on the live store, 48 in an article), and the store labels the standalone membership "International Only" while the product page presents it as generally available.<Cite id="insidetracker-store" /> Worth confirming directly for a US purchase.

### Humanity through Quest — the 29 dollar option nobody mentions

Announced on **25 August 2026**, Humanity's Biological Age analysis is now sold directly through questhealth.com as a **29 dollar add-on** to four of the health panels people already buy there, with no additional blood draw.<Cite id="quest-2026-humanity" /> If your question is "I am getting bloodwork anyway, what does it say about my aging," this is by a wide margin the cheapest honest answer, and it is the incumbent lab infrastructure undercutting the membership startups by an order of magnitude.

Similarly, Amazon One Medical members got **Health Insights** in February 2026 — an analysis of 50-plus biomarkers from labs drawn in the past year, at no extra cost.<Cite id="amazon-2026-health-insights" />

## How to choose

**If you want daily feedback on habits**, a wearable is the right layer, and Apple Health Age may make it free once it ships. Until then Whoop Peak is the most transparent paid option.

**If you want a laboratory measure of aging**, pick one epigenetic test and stay with it — switching vendors makes your history meaningless, because the clocks are not interchangeable. NOVOS at 349 dollars is the best balance of depth, a real app, and honesty about retest intervals. **Retest once a year, not quarterly.**

**If you already get bloodwork**, do not buy a membership before trying the 29 dollar Humanity add-on on your next Quest panel, or InsideTracker's upload-only tier.

**If the goal is to move the number rather than measure it**, the levers are unglamorous and well established — sleep, strength, cardiorespiratory fitness and metabolic health — and [how to lower your biological age](/blog/how-to-lower-biological-age) covers them without a purchase attached.

**And whatever you pick, judge it on the trend, not the reading.** Everything in this article points the same direction: one number from one sample on one day carries an error bar the marketing does not show you. The signal is in the direction of travel across years, and that only exists if you keep the history in one place — which is the part most of these products quietly do not solve, because each keeps its own results inside its own app.
