You get your blood test results back, and there it is — a flag next to **ALT** or **AST** showing elevated. Your first instinct is to panic and Google "liver damage." Let's take a step back.

Elevated liver enzymes are one of the most common findings on routine blood work. In most cases, the cause is benign and reversible. But in some cases, it is the first clue to a serious problem that deserves attention. Knowing the difference is what matters.

## What ALT and AST actually are

ALT and AST are **enzymes** — proteins that catalyze chemical reactions inside cells. They are not toxins or waste products. They exist in healthy cells as part of normal metabolism.

**ALT (alanine aminotransferase)** is found primarily in **liver cells** (hepatocytes). When liver cells are damaged or inflamed, they leak ALT into the bloodstream. This makes ALT the more liver-specific of the two enzymes.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

**AST (aspartate aminotransferase)** is found in the liver, but also in the **heart, skeletal muscle, kidneys, and brain**. Elevated AST can come from any of these sources — not just the liver.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" /> After intense exercise (especially weightlifting or endurance events), AST commonly rises from muscle breakdown, not liver damage.<Cite id="pettersson-2008-exercise-liver-tests" />

## Reference ranges: lab normal vs. optimal

| Enzyme | Standard lab range | Optimal (longevity) |
|---|---|---|
| **ALT (men)** | 7–56 U/L | Below 30 U/L |
| **ALT (women)** | 7–45 U/L | Below 25 U/L |
| **AST (men)** | 10–40 U/L | Below 30 U/L |
| **AST (women)** | 9–32 U/L | Below 25 U/L |

Research increasingly suggests that ALT levels in the upper range of "normal" — say 40–55 U/L — are not truly normal. Lab upper limits were set using populations that included people with silent liver disease, and higher ALT tracks with excess weight, abnormal blood sugar and lipids, and future type 2 diabetes.<Cite id="prati-2002-alt-healthy-ranges" /><Cite id="vozarova-2002-alt-insulin-resistance" /> The American College of Gastroenterology's 2017 guideline on abnormal liver tests links elevated ALT to higher liver-related mortality, puts the true healthy upper limit of ALT at 29–33 U/L for men and 19–25 U/L for women, and says levels above this should be assessed.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

## The AST/ALT ratio (De Ritis ratio)

The ratio between AST and ALT is diagnostically useful:<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

| AST/ALT ratio | Suggests |
|---|---|
| **Below 1** | Fatty liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD), viral hepatitis |
| **About 1** | Non-specific — could be many causes |
| **Above 1 but below 2** | Cirrhosis, progressing liver disease |
| **Above 2** | Strongly suggests alcohol-related liver disease |

This ratio was described by Fernando De Ritis in 1957 and remains one of the simplest and most useful calculations in liver diagnostics.<Cite id="de-ritis-1957-transaminase-ratio" /><Cite id="botros-2013-de-ritis-ratio" />

## Common causes of elevated liver enzymes

### 1. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)

By far the most common cause of elevated ALT — in US national data, most elevations were not explained by alcohol, viral hepatitis or iron overload and tracked with excess weight and metabolic syndrome.<Cite id="clark-2003-elevated-aminotransferases" /> Since 2023 the condition has officially been called metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).<Cite id="rinella-2023-masld-nomenclature" /> An estimated 80–100 million Americans have some degree of fatty liver.<Cite id="estes-2018-nafld-burden" /> It is driven by insulin resistance, excess visceral fat, and metabolic syndrome.<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" /> In early stages, ALT may be only [mildly elevated (40–80 U/L)](/blog/alt-level-40-high). AST is typically lower than ALT (ratio below 1).<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

It exists on a spectrum: simple steatosis (fat accumulation) → MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, formerly NASH, with inflammation) → fibrosis → cirrhosis. Catching it early is key — though liver enzymes can be normal even in advanced disease — because the early stages can be reversed with weight loss.<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" /><Cite id="vilar-gomez-2015-weight-loss-nash" />

### 2. Alcohol

Even moderate drinking can elevate liver enzymes (ALT and GGT), especially in people carrying extra weight.<Cite id="alatalo-2008-alcohol-bmi-liver-enzymes" /> Alcohol-related liver disease classically shows AST/ALT ratio above 2, often with [elevated GGT](/blog/ggt-levels-high-meaning). Heavy drinking raises AST more than ALT, but values rarely exceed about 300 U/L.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

### 3. Medications

Common medications that elevate liver enzymes include acetaminophen (Tylenol) — especially at high doses, statins (usually mild, transient, and more common at higher doses), NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) with chronic use, antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate is notorious), anti-seizure medications, and some herbal supplements (green tea extract, kava).<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" /><Cite id="livertox-atorvastatin" /><Cite id="livertox-amoxicillin-clavulanate" /><Cite id="livertox-green-tea" /><Cite id="livertox-kava" />

If your liver enzymes rose after starting a new medication, that medication is a likely cause. Do not stop the medication without consulting your doctor — the elevation may be benign and expected.

### 4. Intense exercise

Heavy resistance training or endurance events (marathons, CrossFit) can raise AST — and even ALT — for a week or more. CK (creatine kinase) also rises with muscle damage, which helps distinguish exercise-related AST elevation from true liver damage.<Cite id="pettersson-2008-exercise-liver-tests" /><Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" /> If you exercise intensely, avoid hard training for about a week before a blood draw.

### 5. Viral hepatitis

Acute hepatitis A, B, and C can cause dramatic liver enzyme elevations (often 5 to more than 15 times normal). Hepatitis B and C can also cause chronic mild elevations. Screening is recommended for anyone with persistently elevated enzymes without an obvious cause.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

### 6. Autoimmune hepatitis

The immune system attacks the liver. About four times more common in women. ALT and AST can be anywhere from mildly to markedly elevated, often along with elevated immunoglobulins (IgG). Requires specialist evaluation, including a liver biopsy to confirm.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

### 7. Celiac disease

Undiagnosed celiac disease is an underrecognized cause of mildly elevated liver enzymes, found in approximately 9% of patients with unexplained liver enzyme elevations.<Cite id="bardella-1999-celiac-hypertransaminasemia" /> A celiac antibody screen (tTG-IgA) is a simple test that should be considered.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

## When to worry

The bands below are a practical simplification. The American College of Gastroenterology grades elevations as borderline (under 2x the upper limit), mild (2–5x), moderate (5–15x) and severe (above 15x), and calls for immediate evaluation from the moderate range upward.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

### Mild elevation (1–3x upper limit)
Usually not urgent. Recheck in 2–4 weeks. Consider recent medications, exercise, alcohol, and metabolic factors. About one-third of mildly elevated results normalize on retest.<Cite id="lazo-2008-liver-test-variability" />

### Moderate elevation (3–10x upper limit)
Warrants further investigation — hepatitis screening, ultrasound of the liver, autoimmune markers.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" /> Urgent but not emergency.

### Severe elevation (above 10x upper limit)
Requires urgent medical evaluation. Consider acute hepatitis, drug toxicity (especially acetaminophen), ischemic hepatitis, or autoimmune flare.<Cite id="kwo-2017-acg-liver-chemistries" />

### Persistently elevated (any level, over months)
Even mild persistent elevation should not be ignored. Chronic mildly elevated ALT is the hallmark of MASLD/MASH (formerly NAFLD/NASH), which can progress silently to cirrhosis over years.<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" />

## How to lower ALT and AST naturally

If the cause is MASLD or metabolic dysfunction, these interventions are highly effective:

- **Lose visceral fat** — even 5–10% body weight loss reduces liver fat and inflammation, and losing 10% or more can resolve steatohepatitis and improve fibrosis<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" /><Cite id="vilar-gomez-2015-weight-loss-nash" />
- **Exercise regularly** — both aerobic and resistance training reduce liver fat, even with little or no weight loss<Cite id="keating-2012-exercise-nafld" /><Cite id="hashida-2017-aerobic-resistance-nafld" />
- **Reduce refined carbohydrates and fructose** — the liver converts excess fructose to fat (de novo lipogenesis), directly driving fatty liver<Cite id="softic-2016-fructose-lipogenesis" />
- **Limit or eliminate alcohol** — even moderate drinking is linked to less improvement in fatty liver<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" />
- **Coffee** — 3–4 cups of coffee per day is consistently associated with lower ALT, lower fibrosis risk, and better liver health outcomes across multiple studies<Cite id="xiao-2014-coffee-liver-enzymes" /><Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" />
- **Increase [omega-3 intake](/blog/omega-3-index-test)** — EPA and DHA may modestly reduce liver fat, though effects on liver enzymes are inconsistent<Cite id="parker-2012-omega-3-nafld" />
- **Address insulin resistance** — improving metabolic health (lower [fasting insulin](/blog/fasting-insulin-levels-chart), lower [HOMA-IR](/blog/homa-ir-insulin-resistance)) directly improves liver health<Cite id="rinella-2023-aasld-nafld-guidance" />
