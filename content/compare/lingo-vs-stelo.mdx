**If you want the cheapest short trial and a simple daily score, Lingo is the easier start; if you plan to wear a sensor for months, or want a shorter warm-up, longer wear and published accuracy figures, Stelo has the edge.** People on insulin or with a history of problematic low blood sugar should use neither and talk to their clinician about a prescription CGM instead.

Lingo is Abbott's over-the-counter glucose biosensor and Stelo is Dexcom's. Both were cleared by the FDA in 2024 as over-the-counter integrated CGMs (iCGMs), both stick to the back of the upper arm, and both measure glucose in the fluid under the skin, not in blood. Every price and spec below was checked on the companies' own pages or FDA documents on **November 24, 2026** and is linked to its source.

The two points that matter most are not in the price row. First, Lingo's sensor gives a reading every minute, which makes the line look smoother and more "live." Second, Stelo's sensor lasts one day longer, warms up in half the time and is far more water tolerant, which matters if you swim.

## Which is more accurate: Lingo or Stelo?

The honest answer is that nobody has published a head-to-head study of these two consumer products. What exists is each company's FDA paperwork.

- **Stelo.** The FDA review of Stelo's 2024 clearance reports a pivotal study of 130 participants at six US sites. Mean absolute relative difference (MARD, the average gap between sensor and reference blood glucose) was 8.5% in the 70 to 180 mg/dL range, and 93.1% of readings across 40 to 400 mg/dL fell within 20% of the reference ([FDA K234070](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/K234070.pdf)).
- **Lingo.** Abbott's FDA summary says Lingo met the same iCGM accuracy requirements by leveraging clinical data from the FreeStyle Libre 2 study, but it does not print MARD or percentage-within-20% figures ([FDA K233655](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K233655.pdf)).

So Stelo has published numbers and Lingo does not, but that is not proof that one is more accurate in your arm. Two practical details matter more for most buyers. The FDA documents list the range each app displays: 55 to 200 mg/dL for Lingo and 70 to 250 mg/dL for Stelo at clearance. Neither is designed to show the extremes a person with diabetes needs to see. And both sensors read interstitial fluid, which lags blood glucose by several minutes, so a single number is less useful than the shape of the line. Our guide to [wearing a CGM without diabetes](/blog/cgm-without-diabetes) explains what a normal line looks like.

## Which app is better?

This is a question of style more than features.

**Lingo** is built around one number. The Lingo Count turns your glucose exposure into a daily score with a target to stay under, backed by weekly progress reports and challenges ([Lingo](https://www.hellolingo.com/lingo-experience)). Lingo also lists access to registered dietitians through an independent partner, subject to insurance. Stelo plans include one-on-one video calls with a dietitian ([Stelo](https://stelo.com/buy-stelo-one-time)). It suits people who want a coach-like nudge rather than raw charts.

**Stelo** leans on context. It offers personalized tips, flags spikes when glucose rises faster than usual, can pull sleep and activity from Apple Health, Google Health Connect and Oura, and can show data directly on a compatible Apple Watch ([Stelo](https://stelo.com/how-it-works)). Dexcom announced a redesigned app for iPhone and Android in the US, launching in July 2026 ([Dexcom, June 2026](https://investors.dexcom.com/news/news-details/2026/Dexcom-Further-Advances-Vision-of-Glucose-Biosensing-for-All/default.aspx)). It suits people who already wear an Apple Watch or Oura ring and want glucose next to sleep and workouts.

Neither app is a medical alarm. Stelo's provider page says it avoids "bothersome alerts," and its low-pattern detection is a trend notice, not an urgent alarm. If you need real-time warnings of dangerous lows, that is a prescription device.

## Which stays on better, and which is smaller?

Both companies publish nearly identical survival figures: about 77% of sensors last the full wear period (77.1% for Lingo's 14 days, 77.9% for Stelo's 15 days). Put differently, roughly one sensor in five may come off or stop early with either brand. Lingo says it will replace a sensor that stops before 14 days. If adhesion worries you, an overpatch, clean dry skin and avoiding lotion at the site help with either.

On size, neither consumer site publishes dimensions. Lingo describes its sensor as "about the size of a coin," and Stelo calls its sensor "a small wearable." We are not printing millimeter figures we could not confirm on the official pages.

## Can you switch between Lingo and Stelo?

Yes. Neither sensor works with the other company's app, but there is no contract tying you to one, apart from any subscription you need to cancel. The catch is the same one that applies to [switching blood labs](/compare/quest-vs-labcorp): two different sensors, with different algorithms and display ranges, will not give exactly the same numbers. If you compare a meal on Lingo with the same meal on Stelo, part of the difference may be the device. Keep one brand for any experiment you want to compare over time.

## Lingo vs Stelo vs Libre, and Signos

- **FreeStyle Libre and Dexcom G7** are prescription CGMs for people managing diabetes, including people on insulin. They show wider glucose ranges and offer real-time high and low alarms. Insurance coverage depends on your plan and diagnosis.
- **Signos** is not a separate sensor. Its site says it is "powered by Stelo" and pairs the sensor with its own FDA-cleared app and a behavior change program focused on weight, from $136 per month on a 6-month plan as listed on [signos.com](https://www.signos.com/) on November 24, 2026. You pay more than Stelo alone for the program on top.

## Who should not use an over-the-counter sensor

Both Lingo and Stelo are cleared for people not on insulin, and the user is not meant to take medical action from the readings without a qualified clinician ([FDA K233655](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf23/K233655.pdf), [FDA K234070](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/K234070.pdf)). Stelo also states it should not be used by people with problematic hypoglycemia. Neither is intended to diagnose diabetes.

Talk to your clinician instead of buying either if you use insulin, have had serious low blood sugar episodes, are pregnant, or are adjusting diabetes medication. Abbott also suggests checking with a health professional before changing diet or exercise if you have a current or past eating disorder. In our view, watching a live glucose line all day is not a good fit for everyone.

## What a CGM does not tell you: your blood work

A CGM shows how your glucose moves day to day: after a meal, a walk or a bad night of sleep. It does not replace lab tests. Diagnosis and long-term risk still rest on blood work such as hemoglobin A1c, which reflects average glucose over roughly two to three months, and fasting glucose.

The two complement each other. A two-week sensor experiment can suggest which habits push your glucose up; your lab A1c and fasting glucose show whether the longer-term picture is moving. If your A1c is in the 5.7 to 6.4% range, read our guide to [an HbA1c of 5.7](/blog/hba1c-5-7-pre-diabetic) and to a [fasting glucose of 100 mg/dL](/blog/fasting-glucose-100-borderline). Our [A1c calculator](/tools/a1c-calculator) converts an A1c to an estimated average glucose, which is a useful bridge between a lab result and a sensor's average, though the two will rarely match exactly.
