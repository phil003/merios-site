Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. Even at rest, the interval between heartbeats varies — and that variation tells a powerful story about your nervous system, recovery, and overall health.

This variation is called **Heart Rate Variability (HRV)**.

## HRV in 30 seconds

HRV measures the time differences between successive heartbeats, usually in milliseconds. A **higher** HRV generally indicates better [cardiovascular fitness](/blog/apple-watch-vo2-max-accuracy) and a well-balanced autonomic nervous system. A **lower** HRV often signals stress, fatigue, or poor recovery. ## Why HRV matters more than resting heart rate

[Resting heart rate](/blog/resting-heart-rate-suddenly-high) tells you how fast your heart beats. HRV tells you **how well your body adapts** to stress.

Two people can have the same resting heart rate of 62 bpm but wildly different HRV scores. The person with higher HRV is likely:

- Recovering better from exercise
- Managing stress more effectively
- Sleeping more restfully
- At lower risk of cardiovascular events

## What affects your HRV?

Several factors influence your daily HRV readings:

- **[Sleep quality](/blog/deep-sleep-how-to-increase)** — A bad night's sleep can drop your HRV by 20-30%
- **Alcohol** — Even moderate drinking suppresses HRV for 24-48 hours
- **Exercise timing** — Intense workouts temporarily lower HRV; the recovery pattern matters
- **Stress** — Both acute and chronic stress reduce variability
- **Hydration** — Dehydration affects autonomic balance

## Recovery HRV (Whoop, Oura) vs Apple Watch HRV: why the numbers differ

If your Whoop says 78 and your Apple Watch says 34, neither is broken. They are not measuring the same thing, and the gap between them is the single most common source of HRV confusion.

Three things differ at once:

- **The metric.** Recovery-focused wearables like Whoop and Oura typically report **RMSSD**, which captures beat-to-beat differences and tracks parasympathetic (vagal) activity closely. Apple Watch reports **SDNN**, the standard deviation of the intervals across the whole recording, which folds in slower influences as well. RMSSD and SDNN are different statistics of the same signal, so their absolute values are simply not interchangeable.
- **The window.** Whoop and Oura compute recovery from a long, quiet stretch during sleep, when confounders are at their lowest. Apple Watch takes short opportunistic samples across the day — while you are sitting, walking, or running a Breathe session — so its readings carry whatever you were doing at the time.
- **The processing.** Each vendor applies its own artifact rejection and averaging before showing you a number. Two devices on the same wrist will disagree even when both are working correctly.

The practical rule: **compare a device only to itself.** Your Apple Watch HRV trend over six weeks is meaningful. Your Apple Watch number against your friend's Oura number is noise. If you switch devices, treat it as starting a new baseline rather than a change in your physiology.

A related trap is reading a single night. HRV swings substantially day to day with sleep, alcohol, illness, training load and even room temperature, so a weekly average is far more informative than any one morning's figure.

## How to improve your HRV

The good news: HRV is highly trainable.

1. **Prioritize sleep** — Consistent 7-9 hours of quality sleep is the single best HRV booster
2. **Breathwork** — Slow, deep breathing (4-7-8 pattern) activates your parasympathetic nervous system
3. **Avoid alcohol close to bedtime** — Even 1-2 drinks visibly suppress overnight HRV
4. **Regular aerobic exercise** — [Consistent cardio](/blog/zone-2-cardio-heart-rate) improves vagal tone over time
5. **Cold exposure** — Cold showers or face immersion can acutely boost HRV
