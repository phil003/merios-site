MCHC, or mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, is the average concentration of hemoglobin inside your red blood cells, calculated from your hemoglobin and hematocrit on a complete blood count. In adults, a normal MCHC is about 32 to 36 g/dL (320 to 360 g/L); below that is called hypochromia, and a result above it is, more often than not, worth rechecking before anyone interprets it.<Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

That second point is what makes MCHC different from its neighbors. MCV and MCH tend to drift with real changes in the cells. MCHC is held in a tight band by biology, so a low value usually means something specific, and a high value is more often a problem with the sample than with you.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> This guide covers what MCHC measures, the ranges in US and SI units, what a low or high result is commonly associated with, a checklist of the artifacts behind a high MCHC, and a grid for reading MCHC next to MCV and MCH. For the size and content indices on their own, see our guides to the [MCV blood test](/blog/mcv-blood-test) and the [MCH blood test](/blog/mch-blood-test).

## What is MCHC in a blood test?

MCHC in a blood test is the average amount of hemoglobin per unit volume of red cells, reported in g/dL. A typical adult value sits between 32 and 36 g/dL. It is not measured directly: the analyzer derives it from your hemoglobin and hematocrit, or equivalently from MCH and MCV, and prints it with the other red cell indices.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

The calculation looks like this:

**MCHC (g/dL) = hemoglobin (g/dL) × 100 ÷ hematocrit (%)**

So a hemoglobin of 14 g/dL with a hematocrit of 42% gives an MCHC of about 33.3 g/dL. You get the same answer from the other two indices: an MCH of 30 pg divided by an MCV of 90 fL, times 100, is also 33.3. That double derivation is the key to understanding MCHC. It has no measurement of its own, so anything that distorts hemoglobin or hematocrit in the tube distorts MCHC too.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

What it describes is density. MCH tells you how much hemoglobin an average cell holds; MCHC tells you how tightly that hemoglobin is packed for the cell's size. Cleveland Clinic puts the distinction simply: unlike MCH, MCHC factors in both the amount of hemoglobin and the size of the cell.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /> A large cell with plenty of hemoglobin and a small cell with a little can share exactly the same MCHC.

Cells with a normal MCHC are called **normochromic**, and cells with a low MCHC **hypochromic**, literally under-colored, because hemoglobin is what makes red cells red.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> There is no matching word in routine use for the other direction. As one classic hematology text puts it, there are no hyperchromic anemias: normal red cells carry hemoglobin at close to the maximum density they can hold, so a high MCHC usually reflects a change in cell shape or a measurement problem, not cells stuffed with extra hemoglobin.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

## MCHC normal range: US and SI units at a glance

A normal adult MCHC is about 32 to 36 g/dL. MedlinePlus lists 32 to 36 g/dL, or 320 to 360 g/L in SI units, and a classic reference gives 34 plus or minus 2 g/dL, the same band. A result below about 32 g/dL is read as low and one above about 36 g/dL as high, against your own lab's range.<Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

The same number can appear in three different formats depending on where your lab is and which analyzer it uses. US reports almost always use g/dL. Many international reports use g/L, which is simply the g/dL figure multiplied by 10. Some older reports write MCHC as a percentage, which is numerically the same as g/dL.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

| MCHC result | US units (g/dL) | SI units (g/L) | What it describes | Commonly associated with | What is usually checked next |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|-------------------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| Low | Below 32 | Below 320 | Hypochromia: hemoglobin less concentrated | Iron deficiency; thalassemia trait; a surge of young red cells; rarely lead, copper or vitamin B6 deficiency | Ferritin, iron panel, reticulocyte count |
| Normal | 32 to 36 | 320 to 360 | Normochromic | If hemoglobin is low: read MCV and RDW for the cause | Depends on MCV and hemoglobin |
| High | Above 36 | Above 360 | Hemoglobin appears more concentrated | Sample artifact first; then spherocytes, as in hereditary spherocytosis or autoimmune hemolysis | Repeat sample, blood smear, reticulocyte count |

The low row follows StatPearls and the standard review of microcytosis; the high row follows StatPearls and the British guideline on hereditary spherocytosis.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

Two practical points. First, because MCHC lives in a narrow band, a small shift means more than it would for MCV: 31 g/dL is a modest move, while 40 g/dL is far enough out that a lab will usually investigate it. One hospital laboratory, for example, set its automatic recheck for cold agglutinins at an MCHC above 38.5 g/dL (385 g/L).<Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" /> Second, babies are read against their own ranges. In newborns, the hereditary spherocytosis guideline describes an MCHC above 36 g/dL (360 g/L) as a useful pointer toward the condition, with 82% sensitivity and 98% specificity.<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" /> A child's result should always be compared with the range for their age.

## Low MCHC: what does it mean?

A low MCHC, below about 32 g/dL, means hemoglobin is less concentrated in your red cells than usual, called hypochromia. The most common cause is iron deficiency. Less common causes include copper or vitamin B6 deficiency and lead poisoning, and a surge of young red cells can lower it too, because reticulocytes carry hemoglobin at a lower concentration.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

**Iron deficiency** leads the list. Without enough iron, the marrow cannot build hemoglobin fast enough, and it releases cells that are smaller and paler.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> Here is the nuance most pages miss: on modern analyzers, MCHC is often still normal in microcytic anemia, and it tends to fall later than MCV and MCH.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> So a low MCHC tends to show up with a low MCV and a low MCH rather than on its own, and it often suggests the iron shortfall has been building for a while. Ferritin is the usual first test, and in adults an iron deficiency anemia is generally followed up to find its source, most often blood loss.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> If your ferritin is normal but you still suspect low iron, our guides to [low ferritin with normal hemoglobin](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin) and [ferritin vs iron saturation](/blog/ferritin-vs-iron-saturation) cover the gray zones. If your report includes serum iron and TIBC, the [transferrin saturation calculator](/tools/transferrin-saturation-calculator) turns them into a percentage.

**Thalassemia trait** is the other main cause of small, pale cells. It is an inherited difference in hemoglobin production that typically produces a low MCV and MCH with only mild anemia or none. If ferritin is not low, further tests usually include the iron panel and possibly hemoglobin electrophoresis.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> It is one of the reasons to confirm the cause before starting iron on your own.

**A surge of young red cells.** After bleeding, or while the body is replacing cells lost to hemolysis, the marrow releases many reticulocytes at once. These are larger than mature cells and carry hemoglobin at a lower concentration, so MCV can rise while MCHC dips slightly.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> A reticulocyte count separates this from a production problem.

**Less common causes.** StatPearls lists copper deficiency, pyridoxine (vitamin B6) deficiency and lead poisoning, and sideroblastic anemia appears on standard lists of microcytic causes.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

A low MCHC is rarely an artifact. When it appears, it is usually a real signal worth following, and the questions that matter are whether your hemoglobin, MCV and ferritin agree with it.

## High MCHC: what does it mean?

A high MCHC, above about 36 g/dL, is more commonly a laboratory artifact than a real change, according to StatPearls: lipemia, hemolysis in the sample and red cell clumping can all raise it. When it is genuine, it is most often associated with spherocytes, round red cells that have lost membrane, as in hereditary spherocytosis.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

Because of that ordering, artifact first and biology second, the next section gives the artifact checklist on its own. The real causes, in rough order of how often they come up:

**Hereditary spherocytosis.** This is an inherited defect in the red cell membrane. The cells lose surface area, become spherical, are trapped and destroyed in the spleen, and pack the same hemoglobin into a smaller volume, which is why MCHC rises.<Cite id="perrotta-2008-hereditary-spherocytosis" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> It is the most common inherited anemia in people of northern European ancestry, and most people with it have a well-compensated hemolytic anemia; some have no symptoms at all.<Cite id="perrotta-2008-hereditary-spherocytosis" /> The British guideline notes that many affected people have a normal hemoglobin with raised reticulocytes, and that someone with a family history, typical features, spherocytes on the smear, a raised MCHC and raised reticulocytes generally needs no further tests to confirm it.<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" /> When the picture is less clear, a screening test such as the EMA binding test is used.<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

**Autoimmune hemolytic anemia.** Antibodies against red cells can also produce spherocytes. Without a family history, autoimmune hemolytic anemia is the most important alternative to hereditary spherocytosis, and it can usually, though not always, be excluded by a negative direct antiglobulin test (DAT).<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

**Cold agglutinins.** These are antibodies that make red cells clump at below-body temperature. They sit on both sides of the line: they can be part of a real condition, and they also distort the count in the tube, which is covered in the checklist below.<Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

**Other causes.** StatPearls also lists xerocytosis, an inherited condition in which red cells become dehydrated, and some hemoglobinopathies.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> Severe burns can damage red cells directly and produce spherocytes on the smear, one recognized cause of anemia in burn care.<Cite id="siah-2010-burns-haemolysis" /> In one laboratory's series of high MCHC readings, a remaining group of samples came from people on antiretroviral therapy or with severe electrolyte disorders, conditions the authors describe as known to be associated with a raised MCHC.<Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" />

## Is a high MCHC an artifact? A checklist

A high MCHC is often an artifact. StatPearls states that a high MCHC more commonly represents an artifact than true disease, and in one prospective hospital series of 73 samples with an MCHC above 38.5 g/dL, a quick warming step brought the value back below that threshold in 50 of them.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" />

That second figure comes from a single lab, using a specific analyzer and a sample group already flagged for review, so it is not a general rate. But it shows why labs recheck a striking MCHC before reporting it as a finding. These are the usual suspects, and the clues a clinician or lab looks for:

| Possible artifact | How it pushes MCHC up | Typical clue on the report | How it is usually resolved |
|-------------------|-----------------------|----------------------------|----------------------------|
| Lipemia (fatty sample) | Clouds the sample and falsely raises the hemoglobin reading | Hemoglobin and MCH also look high for the hematocrit; often a non-fasting draw | A repeat sample, sometimes fasting, or review by the lab |
| Hemolysis in the tube | Red cells break open, so hemoglobin is high relative to hematocrit | Sample flagged as hemolyzed; difficult or delayed draw | A fresh, carefully drawn sample |
| Cold agglutinins | Cells clump, so the red cell count and hematocrit read falsely low | Low hematocrit and red cell count, normal hemoglobin, high MCV and MCHC | Warming the sample to body temperature and re-running it |

The lipemia, hemolysis and agglutination rows follow StatPearls; the cold agglutinin pattern and the warming step follow Sarma and Falvella.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" />

**Key insight:** the cold agglutinin pattern is distinctive. A low hematocrit, a normal hemoglobin and a high MCV and MCHC on the same report is described as characteristic of cold agglutinins, and the classic fix is to warm the sample to 37 °C before re-running it.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" /> If that combination is on your report, it is worth asking whether the sample was rechecked warm.

In practice, the test for an artifact is internal consistency. Does the hemoglobin fit the hematocrit, roughly three to one? Does the high MCHC fit how you feel and your previous results? A single MCHC of 37.5 on an otherwise unremarkable panel is usually repeated rather than worked up. A persistently high MCHC on clean, repeated samples is the one that leads to a smear and the tests in the next sections.

## Reading MCHC with MCV and MCH: the combination grid

MCHC means most when read next to MCV and MCH. Low MCV and MCH with a low MCHC usually suggests iron deficiency or thalassemia trait; a high MCV with a normal MCHC suggests the large-cell causes; and a high MCHC with a normal or low MCV suggests spherocytes or an artifact.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

The grid below puts the usual combinations in one place. These are tendencies, not rules, and there is plenty of overlap between rows.

| MCV | MCH | MCHC | What the cells are doing | Commonly associated with |
|-----|-----|------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Low | Low | Low | Small, pale cells (microcytic, hypochromic) | Established iron deficiency; thalassemia trait |
| Low | Low | Normal | Small cells, concentration preserved | Earlier iron deficiency; thalassemia trait (MCHC is often normal in microcytosis on analyzers) |
| Normal | Normal | Normal, with low hemoglobin | Normal cells, too few (normocytic anemia) | Chronic disease, kidney disease, recent blood loss |
| High | High | Normal | Large cells, normal concentration (macrocytic) | B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, medications, liver or thyroid disease |
| High | Normal or high | Low or low-normal | Many young, large, less concentrated cells | A reticulocyte surge after bleeding or hemolysis |
| Normal or low | Normal | High | Dense, spherical cells | Hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia |
| High | High | High, with low hematocrit and normal hemoglobin | Cells clumping in the tube | Cold agglutinins, usually rechecked warm |
| Any | High | High, hemoglobin high for the hematocrit | Hemoglobin reading inflated | Lipemia or hemolysis in the sample |

The rows draw on StatPearls and Sarma for MCHC, the standard reviews of microcytosis, macrocytosis and normocytic anemia for the other patterns, and the hereditary spherocytosis guideline for the spherocyte row.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /><Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /><Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

Two rows deserve a comment. The macrocytic row shows why MCHC is the most stable of the three indices: in B12 or folate deficiency the cells are larger and carry more hemoglobin in total, but the concentration stays roughly the same, so MCHC typically stays in range. And the spherocyte row explains why a high MCHC does not always come with abnormal MCV or MCH: spherocytes are dense rather than large. RDW adds one more layer to all of this, which our [MCV guide](/blog/mcv-blood-test) and the hub on [MCV and MCH patterns together](/blog/mcv-mch-blood-test-meaning) cover.

## What is usually checked after an abnormal MCHC?

After a low MCHC, ferritin and an iron panel are usually checked first, often with a reticulocyte count. After a high MCHC, the first step is usually a repeat sample, sometimes warmed, then a peripheral blood smear to look for spherocytes, a reticulocyte count and, where spherocytes are seen, a direct antiglobulin test.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

**For a low MCHC:**

- **Ferritin**, the first test for suspected iron deficiency, followed by serum iron, TIBC and transferrin saturation when ferritin does not settle the question.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />
- **Reticulocyte count**, to see whether a surge of young cells is pulling the average down.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />
- **Hemoglobin electrophoresis**, when small cells persist with a normal ferritin, to look for thalassemia trait.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

**For a high MCHC:**

- **A repeat sample**, ideally drawn cleanly and handled promptly, and warmed if cold agglutinins are suspected.<Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" />
- **A peripheral blood smear**, where a trained eye can see spherocytes, clumping or other shape changes directly.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />
- **Reticulocyte count**, since a raised count alongside spherocytes supports a hemolytic process.<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />
- **Direct antiglobulin test (DAT)**, to separate autoimmune hemolysis from hereditary spherocytosis, and the **EMA binding test** when hereditary spherocytosis is suspected but not clear-cut.<Cite id="bolton-maggs-2012-hereditary-spherocytosis" />

Which of these is ordered, and when, is your clinician's call and depends on the rest of the panel. Starting iron on your own because of a low MCHC, before ferritin and the cause are known, can make the picture harder to read afterward.

## Why the trend matters more than one MCHC value

A single MCHC tells you where your red cells stood on the day of the draw. A series tells you which way they are moving. Because MCHC normally sits in such a narrow band, a slow slide from 34 to 32 g/dL across a few years, alongside a falling MCV and ferritin, can be more informative than any one flagged result, even though each value is technically in range.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> In the other direction, a high MCHC that appears once and vanishes on the next draw is usually a sample story, while one that is high every time is the result worth working up.

A few practical habits when comparing reports: compare results from the same lab where you can, since analyzers differ slightly; read MCHC alongside hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV and MCH rather than alone; and note anything unusual about the draw, such as a big meal beforehand or a difficult collection, because those details can explain an odd MCHC.
