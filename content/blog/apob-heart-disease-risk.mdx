If you could only look at one number on a blood test to assess your cardiovascular risk, it should be **ApoB** — not total cholesterol, not LDL, not HDL.<Cite id="nla-2024-apob" />

Apolipoprotein B is the structural protein on every atherogenic (artery-clogging) lipoprotein particle. One ApoB molecule sits on the surface of every LDL, VLDL, IDL, and Lp(a) particle. That means your ApoB level is essentially a **particle count** — a direct measurement of how many potentially dangerous lipoproteins are circulating in your blood.<Cite id="sniderman-2019-apob" /> If your lab used different units, the free [Lp(a) unit converter](/tools/lpa-unit-converter) shows the equivalent range.

And particle count, not cholesterol mass, is what drives atherosclerosis.<Cite id="marston-2022-apob-mi" />

## Why ApoB beats LDL cholesterol

Standard lipid panels report **LDL-C** — the mass of cholesterol carried inside LDL particles. But LDL particles vary in size. Some people carry their cholesterol in fewer, larger particles (pattern A). Others carry it in many, smaller particles (pattern B). Both can have the same LDL-C, but the person with more particles has more chances for those particles to penetrate the arterial wall and start plaque formation.<Cite id="sniderman-2019-apob" />

This is called **LDL-C / ApoB discordance**, and it is not rare: European guidelines estimate it affects around 20% of patients.<Cite id="esc-eas-2019-dyslipidaemia" /> It is especially common in people with:

- **Metabolic syndrome** — [high triglycerides](/blog/how-to-lower-triglycerides) drive the liver to produce more small, dense LDL particles<Cite id="boren-2020-ldl-pathophysiology" />
- **Type 2 diabetes** — same mechanism
- **Obesity** — similar metabolic pattern
- **Normal LDL-C but high triglycerides** — the classic setup for discordance<Cite id="esc-eas-2019-dyslipidaemia" />

In these populations, LDL-C can read "normal" while ApoB is elevated. If you rely only on LDL-C, you miss the risk.<Cite id="nla-2024-apob" />

## What the research says

The evidence base for ApoB superiority is substantial:

- The **INTERHEART study** (52 countries, 27,000+ participants) found ApoB/ApoA1 ratio to be the strongest lipid predictor of myocardial infarction.<Cite id="mcqueen-2008-interheart" />
- **UK Biobank data** (about 390,000 people without heart disease) showed that when ApoB, non-HDL cholesterol and triglycerides were analyzed together, only ApoB stayed linked to heart attack risk — although adding ApoB to a standard risk model barely improved prediction.<Cite id="marston-2022-apob-mi" /><Cite id="welsh-2019-uk-biobank-lipids" />
- A 2011 meta-analysis of 12 studies (233,455 people) found ApoB was a stronger marker of cardiovascular risk than LDL-C or non-HDL cholesterol.<Cite id="sniderman-2011-apob-meta-analysis" />
- The **2019 ESC/EAS guidelines** now recommend ApoB measurement for risk assessment, particularly when LDL-C and ApoB may be discordant.<Cite id="esc-eas-2019-dyslipidaemia" />

The emerging consensus: **ApoB adds real value to the standard lipid panel.** The National Lipid Association supports its regular clinical use, and the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline endorses testing it once LDL-C goals are met, especially with high triglycerides, diabetes or very low LDL-C.<Cite id="nla-2024-apob" /><Cite id="aha-acc-2026-dyslipidemia" /> It is not yet routine for every adult, but it should be.

## What is a good ApoB level?

Here is where there is genuine disagreement between standard cardiology and longevity medicine:

| Risk category | ApoB target |
|---|---|
| **US risk-enhancer cut-off (2018 AHA/ACC)** | Below 130 mg/dL |
| **Borderline-to-intermediate / moderate risk** | [Below 90 mg/dL](/blog/apob-90-borderline) (NLA) / below 100 mg/dL (ESC/EAS) |
| **High risk** | Below 70 mg/dL (NLA) / below 80 mg/dL (ESC/EAS) |
| **Longevity-focused** | Below 60 mg/dL |
| **Very aggressive prevention** | Below 40 mg/dL |

The NLA figures are treatment thresholds from its 2024 expert consensus (60 mg/dL at very high risk); the ESC/EAS figures are goals from the 2019 European guidelines (65 mg/dL at very high risk). The 2018 AHA/ACC guideline counted an ApoB of 130 mg/dL or more as a risk-enhancing factor.<Cite id="nla-2024-apob" /><Cite id="esc-eas-2019-dyslipidaemia" /><Cite id="aha-acc-2018-cholesterol" />

The longevity argument is straightforward: atherosclerosis is driven by **cumulative lifetime exposure** to atherogenic particles.<Cite id="ference-2017-ldl-causality" /> A 30-year-old with an ApoB of 110 mg/dL will accumulate far more arterial damage over the next 50 years than one at 60 mg/dL. The earlier you lower it, the more years of reduced exposure you buy.

Whether the very aggressive targets are worth the medication side-effect trade-off is an individual decision best made with your physician.

## How to lower ApoB

### Lifestyle interventions

- **Reduce refined carbohydrates and sugar** — higher sugar intakes raise triglycerides and LDL-C<Cite id="te-morenga-2014-sugars" />
- **Moderate saturated fat intake** — swap some saturated fat for monounsaturated (olive oil, avocado) and polyunsaturated fat (fatty fish, nuts); this swap lowers both LDL-C and ApoB<Cite id="koutsos-2024-rissci" />
- **Increase soluble fiber** — 10-25 g per day of soluble fiber (oats, psyllium, beans, lentils); in trials, about 10 g/day of psyllium lowered ApoB by roughly 5 mg/dL<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001" /><Cite id="jovanovski-2018-psyllium" />
- **Regular aerobic exercise** — 150+ minutes per week of moderate-intensity activity<Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity" />
- **Lose excess body fat** — particularly visceral and liver fat, which are linked to VLDL overproduction<Cite id="adiels-2006-liver-fat-vldl" />
- **Limit alcohol** — heavy drinking raises triglycerides by increasing VLDL production<Cite id="klop-2013-alcohol-tg" />

Combining cholesterol-lowering foods (the "Portfolio" diet) lowered LDL-C by about 17% and also reduced ApoB in controlled trials.<Cite id="chiavaroli-2018-portfolio" /> For those starting very high or with genetic predisposition ([familial hypercholesterolemia](/blog/ldl-cholesterol-high-healthy-diet)), lifestyle alone is often not enough.<Cite id="defesche-2017-fh" />

### Medications

- **Statins** — the first-line treatment, though they carry [side effects worth weighing](/blog/statins-side-effects-when-to-stop). Lower ApoB by about a third on average, depending on the statin and dose — somewhat less than they lower LDL-C.<Cite id="sniderman-2008-apob-response" />
- **Ezetimibe** — blocks cholesterol absorption in the gut. Adds a further ApoB reduction on top of a statin.<Cite id="shin-2022-statin-ezetimibe" />
- **PCSK9 inhibitors** (evolocumab, alirocumab) — injectable antibodies that dramatically increase LDL receptor recycling. Add roughly 43–52% ApoB reduction on top of a statin.<Cite id="huang-2022-pcsk9-network-meta" /> Reserved for high-risk patients or those who do not tolerate statins.
- **Bempedoic acid** — newer oral option for statin-intolerant patients.<Cite id="nissen-2023-clear-outcomes" />
- **Inclisiran** — twice-yearly injection targeting PCSK9 synthesis.<Cite id="ray-2020-orion" /> One of the newest options.

## The complete lipid panel you should actually get

The standard lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides) is a starting point, not the full picture. For genuine cardiovascular risk assessment:

| Test | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **ApoB** | Atherogenic particle count (the most important number) |
| **LDL-C** | Cholesterol mass in LDL (useful but incomplete) |
| **Lp(a)** | [Genetically determined, independent risk factor](/blog/lp-a-lipoprotein-a-high) — test once |
| **Triglycerides** | Metabolic health marker; high TG often signals discordance |
| **HDL-C** | Inverse marker (higher generally better, but not always) |
| **hsCRP** | [Inflammatory marker](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation) that adds context to lipid risk |
| **Fasting insulin** | Metabolic context for your lipid pattern |
