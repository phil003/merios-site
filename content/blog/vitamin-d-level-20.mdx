Your lab report shows vitamin D: 20 ng/mL. Your doctor says it's "normal." But you've read that vitamin D deficiency is linked to everything from weak bones to depression, and 20 feels... low. Should you worry?

The answer depends on which expert you ask—and that's the problem.

## What 20 ng/mL Actually Means

Vitamin D circulates in your blood in a form called **25-hydroxyvitamin D** (or 25(OH)D), and that's what's being measured when you get a vitamin D test. The units are nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL), though some labs use nanomoles per liter (nmol/L); to convert, multiply ng/mL by 2.5.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**20 ng/mL = 50 nmol/L**

At this level, you're at a critical threshold. Depending on which health organization you consult, 20 ng/mL is either:
- Just barely acceptable
- Borderline deficient
- Insufficient for optimal health

Here's where the controversy starts.

## The Guideline Wars: Endocrine Society vs. IOM vs. Functional Medicine

**The Endocrine Society** (largest organization of endocrinologists in the US) defined vitamin D status in its 2011 guideline as:
- **Deficient**: &lt;20 ng/mL
- **Insufficient**: 20–29 ng/mL
- **Sufficient**: 30–100 ng/mL<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" />
- **Toxic** (NIH threshold): &gt;150 ng/mL<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

By this 2011 standard, you're **insufficient**—below the 30 ng/mL the guideline called sufficient. In 2024, the Endocrine Society withdrew these "sufficient" and "insufficient" definitions and stopped setting any target level.<Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" />

**The Institute of Medicine (IOM)** sets a lower bar for "sufficient":
- **Deficient**: &lt;12 ng/mL
- **Inadequate**: 12–20 ng/mL
- **Sufficient**: 20 ng/mL or higher<Cite id="ross-2011-iom-dri" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

By this standard, you're technically sufficient, just barely.

**Functional and integrative medicine practitioners** typically recommend:
- **Optimal levels**: 40–60 ng/mL minimum
- Many advocate for 50–70 ng/mL

The reason for these higher targets is mostly observational research linking higher vitamin D levels to better outcomes for bone health, immune function, autoimmune disease prevention, and mood. Large randomized trials in people who were not deficient have mostly not confirmed those benefits.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" /><Cite id="bolland-2018-musculoskeletal" /><Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" />

**The truth:** The IOM set its "sufficient" threshold (20 ng/mL) as the level that covers the bone-health needs of at least 97.5% of people. It also reviewed non-skeletal outcomes but found the evidence too inconsistent to set requirements.<Cite id="ross-2011-iom-dri" /> The Endocrine Society aimed higher, at 30 ng/mL, based largely on where parathyroid hormone levels plateau.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" />

At 20 ng/mL, you're above the range linked to rickets and osteomalacia (below 12 ng/mL) and at the level the IOM considers adequate; whether going higher improves health outcomes has not been established in trials.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /><Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" />

## What Does Low Vitamin D Actually Do?

Vitamin D isn't just about calcium and bone strength, though that's what most people learn. It's actually a hormone that regulates hundreds of genes:<Cite id="carlberg-2022-target-genes" />

**Bone health** — Vitamin D enables calcium absorption in the intestines. Without it, your body can't absorb dietary calcium efficiently, even if you drink milk. Long-term deficiency leads to soft bones (osteomalacia in adults, rickets in children) and fractures.<Cite id="christakos-2011-calcium-absorption" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Immune regulation** — Vitamin D modulates T cells and macrophages that fight infection and regulate inflammation. [Low vitamin D](/blog/vitamin-d-deficiency) is associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory infections and with autoimmunity.<Cite id="aranow-2011-immune" /><Cite id="ginde-2009-urti" />

**Mood and mental health** — Vitamin D receptors are present throughout the brain, with the strongest expression in the hypothalamus and substantia nigra.<Cite id="eyles-2005-vdr-brain" /> Observational studies link low vitamin D to depression.<Cite id="anglin-2013-depression" /> Whether supplementation improves mood is less clear: in VITAL, 2,000 IU daily did not prevent depression or change mood scores in adults over 50.<Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" />

**Muscle function** — Severe deficiency causes muscle weakness. In trials, supplementation modestly improved muscle strength, mostly in people who were very deficient or over 65.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /><Cite id="beaudart-2014-muscle" />

**Cardiovascular health** — Low vitamin D is associated with increased inflammation and worse cardiovascular outcomes in observational studies.<Cite id="wang-2012-cvd" /><Cite id="zhou-2023-vitamin-d-crp" /> But in VITAL, supplementation did not reduce major cardiovascular events.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" />

**Glucose control** — Low levels are associated with higher diabetes risk, and in people with prediabetes, supplementation reduced progression to type 2 diabetes by 15%.<Cite id="song-2013-diabetes" /><Cite id="pittas-2023-prediabetes" />

**Cancer risk** — Observational studies link low vitamin D most consistently to colorectal cancer.<Cite id="mccullough-2019-colorectal" /> Randomized trials have been disappointing: in VITAL, rates of breast, prostate, and colorectal cancer did not differ between vitamin D and placebo.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" />

## Do You Have Symptoms at 20 ng/mL?

At 20 ng/mL, you're unlikely to have overt rickets or severe osteomalacia, which are tied to levels below 12 ng/mL.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> The symptoms below are often attributed to low vitamin D, but they are nonspecific, so treat them as possibilities rather than a diagnosis. You might experience:

- **Fatigue** — A common presenting symptom of vitamin D deficiency
- **Muscle aches or weakness** — Particularly in the legs
- **Bone pain** — A dull ache, especially in the back or pelvis
- **Mood changes** — Depression, anxiety, or seasonal mood patterns
- **Frequent infections** — More colds, flu, or respiratory infections
- **Slow wound healing** — Cuts and injuries take longer to recover
- **Hair loss** — Less common but reported in severe deficiency

If you have any of these, vitamin D deficiency could be contributing. Fatigue and mood symptoms also overlap heavily with [low B12](/blog/vitamin-d-b12-deficiency-together).

## Who Needs More Aggressive Treatment?

If your vitamin D is 20 ng/mL, you should consider supplementation if any of the situations below apply. For context, the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests that healthy adults under 75 stick to the RDA (600 IU a day, 800 IU over 70) rather than taking more to prevent disease.<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Age &gt;50 or &gt;65** — Bone loss accelerates with age. The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests empiric vitamin D for adults 75 and older, because of its potential to lower mortality, but not supplementation above the RDA for adults 50 to 74.<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" />

**Limited sun exposure** — You live at high latitude, work indoors, or have dark skin tone (which needs considerably more sun to make the same vitamin D). Sun exposure is highly variable in its ability to produce vitamin D.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Dietary insufficiency** — You don't eat fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), egg yolks, or fortified dairy. Average US intakes from food are only about 170–200 IU a day, well below the 600 IU RDA.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Malabsorption** — Celiac disease, Crohn's, cystic fibrosis, or other GI conditions impair fat-soluble vitamin absorption, including vitamin D.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Darker skin tone** — Melanin reduces vitamin D synthesis from sunlight. In one small classic experiment, a person with very dark skin needed about six times the UV dose to match the vitamin D response of lighter skin.<Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" />

**Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — The RDA stays at 600 IU, but the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests empiric supplementation during pregnancy, and low levels are linked to gestational diabetes and postpartum depression.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /><Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" /><Cite id="aghajafari-2013-pregnancy" /><Cite id="wang-2018-postpartum-depression" />

**Chronic disease** — Chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes, and autoimmune conditions are all associated with vitamin D deficiency; in chronic kidney disease, guidelines suggest correcting it.<Cite id="kdigo-2017-ckd-mbd" /><Cite id="song-2013-diabetes" /><Cite id="aranow-2011-immune" />

**You simply want to feel better** — If you have fatigue, mood issues, or muscle aches, raising vitamin D is often suggested, but without a diagnosed deficiency the trial evidence is weak: in VITAL, 2,000 IU daily did not improve mood scores in adults over 50.<Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" />

## How to Raise Vitamin D from 20 to Optimal

**Sunlight exposure** is free but inconsistent:
- Sun exposure depends on latitude, season, time of day, skin tone, and cloud cover<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- In winter at Boston's latitude (about 42° North) or farther from the equator, sun exposure produces essentially no vitamin D<Cite id="webb-1988-latitude" />
- At lower latitudes, about 5–30 minutes of midday sun on the face, arms and legs, at least twice a week, is often enough for lighter-skinned individuals<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- Darker-skinned individuals need considerably longer (about six times the UV dose in one small study), making supplementation more practical<Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" />

**Dietary sources** are helpful but limited:
- Salmon (3 oz): ~570 IU
- Mackerel (3 oz): ~450 IU
- Egg yolk: ~40 IU
- Fortified milk (1 cup): ~120 IU
- Fortified orange juice (1 cup): ~100 IU

To reach 1,000–2,000 IU daily from food alone is difficult without supplementation. The NIH lists about 570 IU for 3 oz of cooked sockeye salmon, 44 IU for an egg and 120 IU for a cup of fortified milk.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Supplementation** is the most reliable:

**Vitamin D3 vs. D2:**
- Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) from animal/lanolin sources is more potent and preferred<Cite id="tripkovic-2012-d2-d3" />
- Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol), made from yeast, is less effective at raising and maintaining 25(OH)D<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- Choose D3 unless you're vegan (in which case, vegan D3 from lichen exists)<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Dosing** (for healthy adults under 75, the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests not going above the RDA to prevent disease; the higher doses below are for correcting a low level with your clinician):<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" />
- **Maintenance (to prevent deficiency)**: 1,000–2,000 IU daily
- **Repletion (to raise from 20 to 40)**: 2,000–5,000 IU daily for 8–12 weeks; in one large dataset, an average level of 40 ng/mL took about 2,000 IU daily at normal weight and about 5,500 IU with obesity<Cite id="ekwaru-2014-body-weight" />
- **Obese individuals**: May need higher doses; discuss with a doctor
- **Very deficient (&lt;10 ng/mL)**: Consider about 6,000 IU daily (up to 10,000 IU with obesity or malabsorption) for 8 weeks under medical supervision, the regimen in the Endocrine Society's 2011 guideline<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" />

**Pro tip:** Vitamin D is fat-soluble, so take it with a meal containing fat (not with black coffee) for better absorption.<Cite id="dawson-hughes-2015-dietary-fat" />

**Vitamin K2:** Some practitioners recommend pairing vitamin D3 with [vitamin K2](/blog/vitamin-k2-d3-together) (menaquinone-7 or MK-7), which activates a protein that helps keep calcium out of soft tissue.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" /> Outcome evidence is still limited: a randomized trial of K2 plus D did not slow aortic valve or coronary calcification.<Cite id="diederichsen-2022-avadec" /> For reference, the adequate intake for vitamin K from all sources is 90 mcg daily for women and 120 mcg daily for men.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" />

Magnesium is also required to activate vitamin D, so [low magnesium can blunt your response](/blog/magnesium-deficiency-symptoms-women) to supplementation.<Cite id="uwitonze-2018-magnesium-vitamin-d" />

## Retesting Protocol

After 8–12 weeks of supplementation, retest vitamin D. The goal:
- **Functional medicine standard**: 40–60 ng/mL (100–150 nmol/L)
- **2011 Endocrine Society target**: [30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L)](/blog/vitamin-d-level-30), withdrawn in 2024; the IOM considers 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) adequate<Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" /><Cite id="ross-2011-iom-dri" />

Once you reach your target, you can often drop to a maintenance dose (1,000–2,000 IU daily) to keep levels stable.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" /> Retest annually or when symptoms return.

## The Bottom Line

A vitamin D level of 20 ng/mL is technically sufficient by the IOM's standard and was "insufficient" by the Endocrine Society's 2011 definition, which the Society withdrew in 2024.<Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" /> If you're fatigued, have mood issues, weak bones, or limited sun exposure, raising your vitamin D with supplementation is a low-risk step to discuss with your clinician, though trials in people who weren't deficient have mostly not shown benefits.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" /><Cite id="bolland-2018-musculoskeletal" />

Vitamin D is inexpensive and safe at reasonable doses, but outside of correcting a true deficiency, the evidence for mood, bone, and immune benefits is weaker than its reputation.<Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" /><Cite id="bolland-2018-musculoskeletal" /><Cite id="jolliffe-2021-respiratory-infections" /> If you're at 20 ng/mL, it's worth asking: why not optimize it?
