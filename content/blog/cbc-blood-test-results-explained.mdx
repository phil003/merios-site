  - q: "What is a CBC blood test?"
    a: "A CBC (complete blood count) is one of the most commonly ordered blood tests in medicine. It measures three main categories: red blood cells (which carry oxygen), white blood cells (which fight infection), and platelets (which help blood clot). A CBC with differential further breaks down white blood cells into five subtypes: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils."
  - q: "What are normal CBC values for adults?"
    a: "Normal ranges for adults are approximately: WBC 4,500–11,000/µL, RBC 4.6–6.2 million/µL (men) or 4.2–5.4 million/µL (women), hemoglobin 13–18 g/dL (men) or 12–16 g/dL (women), hematocrit 40–54% (men) or 36–48% (women), and platelets 150,000–400,000/µL. Ranges vary slightly by lab."
  - q: "Should I be worried about abnormal CBC results?"
    a: "Not necessarily. Many factors cause temporary CBC changes: dehydration, recent exercise, stress, menstruation, minor infections, and medications. A single slightly abnormal value usually requires a recheck, not panic. However, significantly abnormal values — very high or very low white cells, hemoglobin below 10, or platelets below 100,000 — should be evaluated promptly by your physician."
  - q: "How often should I get a CBC?"
    a: "For healthy adults with no symptoms, there is no standard schedule: many doctors include a CBC in a routine check-up, but no major screening guideline calls for one every year. If you have a chronic condition, take medications that affect blood counts, or are monitoring a known abnormality, your doctor may order it more frequently, on a schedule set by that condition or medication."

The **complete blood count** — or CBC — is one of the most commonly ordered blood tests in medicine: in a comparison of hospitals in five countries, it was the most common test by volume in four of them and the second most common in the fifth.<Cite id="horton-2019-top-lab-tests" /> It is a routine part of many emergency room visits, annual physicals, and pre-surgical workups.

Yet most people get their results back and have no idea what the numbers mean. RBC, WBC, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, MPV — it reads like alphabet soup. This guide translates every line on your CBC into plain language.

## CBC normal ranges at a glance

Here is the full adult reference table.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> Labs vary slightly, so always read your own report's range alongside these. Normal values also shift with sex, age, altitude and ancestry.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" /><Cite id="who-2024-hemoglobin-cutoffs" />

| Value | Normal range (men) | Normal range (women) |
|---|---|---|
| **WBC** (white blood cells) | 4,500–11,000/µL | 4,500–11,000/µL |
| **RBC** (red blood cells) | 4.6–6.2 million/µL | 4.2–5.4 million/µL |
| **Hemoglobin (Hgb)** | 13–18 g/dL | 12–16 g/dL |
| **Hematocrit (Hct)** | 40–54% | 36–48% |
| **MCV** | 80–100 fL | 80–100 fL |
| **MCH** | 27–32 pg | 27–32 pg |
| **MCHC** | 32–36 g/dL | 32–36 g/dL<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> |
| **RDW** | 11.5–14.5% | 11.5–14.5%<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> |
| **Platelets** | 150,000–400,000/µL | 150,000–400,000/µL |

Each of these is explained line by line below — including what a high or low value usually means.

## What a CBC measures

A CBC evaluates three populations of cells that circulate in your blood:

| Cell type | What it does | Key measurements |
|---|---|---|
| **Red blood cells (RBC)** | Carry oxygen from lungs to tissues | RBC count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW |
| **White blood cells (WBC)** | Fight infections and disease | WBC count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils |
| **Platelets** | Form clots to stop bleeding | Platelet count, MPV |

A "CBC with differential" includes the white blood cell breakdown into subtypes.

## Red blood cell values

### RBC count

The number of red blood cells per microliter of blood.

| Reference range | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | 4.6–6.2 million/µL | 4.2–5.4 million/µL |

**High RBC** (polycythemia): can indicate dehydration, chronic hypoxia (lung disease, sleep apnea, high altitude), smoking, or rarely polycythemia vera (a bone marrow disorder).<Cite id="medlineplus-cbc" /><Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" /> **Low RBC** (anemia): can indicate iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, chronic disease, blood loss, or bone marrow problems.<Cite id="medlineplus-cbc" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> The red-cell indices tell you which kind of anemia it is — see [what MCV and MCH mean](/blog/mcv-mch-blood-test-meaning).

### Hemoglobin (Hgb)

The protein inside red blood cells that actually carries oxygen. This is often the single most important number for assessing anemia.<Cite id="who-2024-hemoglobin-cutoffs" />

| Reference range | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | 13–18 g/dL | 12–16 g/dL |

**Low hemoglobin** is the definition of anemia: the WHO draws the line at 13.0 g/dL for men and 12.0 g/dL for non-pregnant women, and grades 8.0–10.9 g/dL as moderate anemia and below 8.0 g/dL as severe.<Cite id="who-2024-hemoglobin-cutoffs" /> Below 7 g/dL is the usual threshold for considering a transfusion in stable hospitalized adults.<Cite id="carson-2023-aabb-transfusion" /> **High hemoglobin** mirrors high RBC causes — dehydration, chronic hypoxia, or polycythemia vera.<Cite id="medlineplus-cbc" />

### Hematocrit (Hct)

The percentage of your blood volume occupied by red blood cells.

| Reference range | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | 40–54% | 36–48% |

Hematocrit moves in parallel with hemoglobin. It is particularly affected by hydration status — dehydration concentrates the blood and raises hematocrit artificially.<Cite id="billett-1990-hemoglobin-hematocrit" />

### MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)

The average size of your red blood cells, measured in femtoliters (fL). This is the key to classifying the type of anemia.<Cite id="tefferi-2005-abnormal-cbc" />

| MCV value | Classification | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Below 80 fL** | Microcytic (small cells) | Iron deficiency, thalassemia, chronic disease<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> |
| **80–100 fL** | Normocytic (normal size) | Chronic disease, acute blood loss, kidney disease<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> |
| **Above 100 fL** | Macrocytic (large cells) | B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> |

MCV is one of the most diagnostically useful numbers on the entire CBC. If your hemoglobin is low, MCV tells your doctor where to look next.

### MCH and MCHC

**MCH** (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin): the average amount of hemoglobin per red blood cell. Normal: 27–32 picograms (pg).<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

**MCHC** (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration): the average concentration of hemoglobin within red blood cells. Normal: 32–36 g/dL.

These correlate with MCV and help confirm the type of anemia. Low MCH with low MCV points to iron deficiency (MCHC often stays normal on modern analyzers). High MCH with high MCV points to B12 or folate deficiency.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

### RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width)

Measures the variation in red blood cell size. Normal: 11.5–14.5%.

A high RDW means your red blood cells vary a lot in size — some are big, some are small. This can be the earliest sign of a developing nutritional deficiency (such as iron or folate) before other values change.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" /> RDW has also been identified as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in multiple studies — making it an underappreciated longevity marker.<Cite id="patel-2009-rdw-mortality" /><Cite id="perlstein-2009-rdw-mortality" />

## White blood cell values

### Total WBC count

The total number of white blood cells per microliter. Normal: 4,500–11,000/µL.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

**High WBC** (leukocytosis): most commonly caused by infection, inflammation, stress, or medication (corticosteroids). Extreme elevations (above 30,000) warrant urgent evaluation.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

**Low WBC** (leukopenia): can indicate viral infection, autoimmune conditions, bone marrow problems, or medication effects (chemotherapy, some antibiotics).<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" /><Cite id="andersohn-2007-agranulocytosis" />

### The differential: five white blood cell subtypes

This is where the real diagnostic information lives. Typical adult values for each subtype are below.<Cite id="medlineplus-differential" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

| Cell type | Normal % | Normal absolute | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophils** | 40–60% | 1,500–8,000/µL | First responders to bacterial infection |
| **Lymphocytes** | 20–40% | 1,000–4,000/µL | Adaptive immunity (T and B cells), plus natural killer (NK) cells |
| **Monocytes** | 2–8% | 200–1,000/µL | Clean-up crew; become macrophages in tissue |
| **Eosinophils** | 1–4% | 0–500/µL | Fight parasites; involved in allergies and asthma |
| **Basophils** | 0.5–1% | 0–200/µL | Involved in allergic and inflammatory responses |

**Key patterns:**

- **High neutrophils** (neutrophilia): bacterial infection, acute stress, corticosteroid use, smoking<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />
- **High lymphocytes** (lymphocytosis): viral infection (such as mono), chronic lymphocytic leukemia<Cite id="medlineplus-differential" />
- **High eosinophils** (eosinophilia): allergies, asthma, parasitic infection, drug reactions<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />
- **High monocytes** (monocytosis): chronic infection, autoimmune disease<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />
- **Low neutrophils** (neutropenia): can be medication-induced, viral, autoimmune, or ethnic (benign ethnic neutropenia, now often called Duffy-null associated neutrophil count, is common in people of African descent)<Cite id="kim-2025-neutropenia" /><Cite id="merz-2023-duffy-null-anc" />

### NLR (Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio)

Not always printed on the report, but easily calculated: divide absolute neutrophils by absolute lymphocytes. Normal: roughly 0.8–3.5 in healthy adults.<Cite id="forget-2017-nlr-normal" /> An NLR above that range suggests systemic inflammation.<Cite id="adamstein-2021-nlr" /> Higher values are associated with poor outcomes in cardiovascular disease, cancer, and critical illness.<Cite id="adamstein-2021-nlr" /><Cite id="templeton-2014-nlr" /><Cite id="wu-2024-nlr-sepsis" /> This ratio is increasingly used in research as a simple, cheap marker of chronic inflammation.

## Platelet values

### Platelet count

Normal: 150,000–400,000/µL.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

**High platelets** (thrombocytosis): often reactive — caused by infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, or post-surgery. Less often, a myeloproliferative disorder.<Cite id="griesshammer-1999-thrombocytosis" /><Cite id="medlineplus-cbc" />

**Low platelets** (thrombocytopenia): can indicate immune destruction (ITP), viral infection (dengue, HIV, hepatitis C), medication effects, liver disease with portal hypertension, or bone marrow issues.<Cite id="gauer-2022-thrombocytopenia" /><Cite id="gauer-2012-thrombocytopenia" /> Below 50,000 increases bleeding risk; below 10,000 the risk of serious bleeding is high and a platelet transfusion is recommended.<Cite id="gauer-2022-thrombocytopenia" />

### MPV (Mean Platelet Volume)

The average size of your platelets. In US national survey data, 95% of adults fell between about 6.8 and 10.7 fL.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" />

Young, newly produced platelets are larger and more active. A high MPV with low platelet count can indicate that the body is producing platelets rapidly to compensate for destruction.<Cite id="gauer-2012-thrombocytopenia" /> MPV has also been studied as a cardiovascular risk marker — higher MPV has been associated with heart attack and with worse outcomes after one.<Cite id="chu-2010-mpv" />

## Common scenarios explained

**"My doctor said I'm slightly anemic"**: Usually means hemoglobin is just under the line — 11.0–11.9 g/dL in women or 11.0–12.9 g/dL in men is what the WHO calls mild anemia.<Cite id="who-2024-hemoglobin-cutoffs" /> Check MCV — if low, likely iron deficiency (most common). If high, check B12 and folate.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

**"My white count is a little high"**: If WBC is 11,000–14,000 with high neutrophils, it is usually a recent infection, stress, or inflammation. One elevated reading is rarely concerning; a persistently elevated count deserves investigation.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

**"My platelets are borderline low"**: If 130,000–150,000 with no symptoms, it is often normal variation or very mild reduction. Recheck in 4–6 weeks. If dropping on serial tests, investigate.<Cite id="gauer-2022-thrombocytopenia" />
