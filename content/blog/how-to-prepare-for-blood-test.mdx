You booked a blood test, the lab handed you a slip that says "fasting required," and now you have a dozen small questions. Can you drink water? Does black coffee count? Should you take your morning pills? Is it a problem that you did leg day yesterday?

These details matter more than most people realize. A few hours of the wrong preparation can push a result outside its reference range, trigger a false alarm, or force you back for a repeat draw. The good news is that preparing well is simple once you understand the logic. This guide walks through every factor that changes a blood test, connects each one to the specific markers it affects, and ends with a night-before and morning-of checklist you can follow without thinking.

## Do You Actually Need to Fast?

Fasting is the single most misunderstood part of blood test prep. Most tests do not require it at all. Fasting means nothing to eat or drink except water for 8 to 12 hours before your draw, and it exists for one reason: to capture your true metabolic baseline before food scrambles it.

Only a short list of tests genuinely need it. A **lipid panel** (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and especially triglycerides) is best drawn after 9 to 12 hours fasting, because a recent meal can raise triglycerides for hours. **Fasting glucose** needs at least 8 hours, since any carbohydrate spikes blood sugar and erases your baseline. **Fasting insulin** follows the same rule, because eating triggers insulin secretion within minutes. If your slip mentions glucose, insulin, or lipids, plan to fast.

Almost everything else does not require fasting: a complete blood count, thyroid panel, HbA1c, liver enzymes, kidney function, vitamin levels, and most hormone tests are unaffected by your last meal. For the full breakdown of what does and does not need an empty stomach, see this guide on [fasting rules before blood work](/blog/fasting-before-blood-test). One important caveat: do not over-fast. Going past 14 hours does not sharpen your results and can paradoxically raise glucose through a mild stress response. If your fasting glucose lands in the gray zone, understanding what a reading like [a fasting glucose of 100](/blog/fasting-glucose-100-borderline) means depends on having fasted correctly in the first place.

## Water: Drink It Freely

Water is the one thing you can always have, fasting or not, and you should have plenty of it. Hydration does two useful things. First, it makes the draw itself easier: well-filled veins are larger and easier for a phlebotomist to find, which means fewer failed sticks and less bruising. Second, it protects the accuracy of concentration-dependent markers.

When you are dehydrated, the liquid portion of your blood shrinks, so anything measured as a concentration reads artificially high. **Hematocrit** and **hemoglobin** can look elevated, **sodium** can drift up, and even **BUN** (a kidney marker) can rise simply because you were short on fluids. A tall glass or two of water in the hour before your appointment keeps these values honest and your veins cooperative. There is no downside — water contains no calories, sugar, or fat, so it never breaks a fast.

## Coffee and Tea: The Fasting Gray Zone

Coffee is where good intentions go wrong. Black coffee has almost no calories, so people assume it is harmless before a fasting test. The reality is more nuanced. Caffeine gives cortisol a small nudge, and cortisol can modestly raise **fasting glucose**. Coffee also stimulates the release of free fatty acids into the bloodstream, which can affect a **lipid panel**. The effect is not huge, but if glucose, insulin, or lipids are the whole point of your test, why introduce a variable?

Tea carries the same caffeine caveat in a milder form. The safe rule: if you are fasting, drink water and save the coffee for immediately after your draw. If your test does not require fasting, coffee is generally fine. The deeper question of whether coffee technically breaks a metabolic fast is covered in this breakdown of [whether coffee breaks a fast](/blog/does-coffee-break-a-fast). And obviously, anything you add — milk, cream, sugar, a splash of oat milk — carries calories and fat and breaks the fast outright.

## Alcohol: Stop 24 to 48 Hours Before

Alcohol is easy to forget about because you are not drinking it the morning of your test, but its effects linger. A night of drinking can elevate **triglycerides** and **blood glucose**, and it puts direct stress on the liver. That shows up in **GGT**, which is a sensitive marker of alcohol exposure, and can also bump **ALT** and **AST**. If you want a clean read on your liver and metabolic markers, avoid alcohol for 24 to 48 hours before the draw.

This matters most if liver enzymes or lipids are being evaluated. A single heavy night can push GGT or triglycerides up enough to look like a chronic problem when the real cause was last weekend. Giving your body a two-day buffer removes that confounder entirely.

## Medications and Supplements: Don't Stop, but Do Disclose

This is the most important safety point in the entire checklist: **do not stop a prescription medication on your own to prepare for a blood test.** Blood pressure pills, heart medications, thyroid medication, antidepressants, and most other routine prescriptions should be taken as usual, typically with a sip of water even during a fast. Skipping doses to "clean up" your results can be genuinely dangerous and is almost never what your doctor wants. If you are unsure whether to take something the morning of a fasting test, ask the lab or your physician rather than guessing.

Supplements are a different story, because a few of them actively distort specific assays. **Biotin** (vitamin B7, common in hair and nail supplements) is the notorious one: at high doses it interferes with the immunoassay technology used for many **thyroid** tests (TSH, Free T4, Free T3), some **hormone** panels, and even cardiac troponin, producing falsely high or falsely low numbers depending on the test. Many labs recommend pausing high-dose biotin for two to three days before a draw — confirm the timing with your provider. Similarly, a **vitamin D** supplement taken that morning will not corrupt the assay, but it does mean the level you measure reflects your pill as much as your baseline, and the same logic applies to **iron** and **B12** supplements raising those exact readings. The rule is simple: keep your prescriptions, but tell your doctor every supplement you take, because context changes interpretation.

## Exercise: Skip the Hard Workout

A tough workout the day before a blood test can make a perfectly healthy person look sick on paper. Intense or unfamiliar exercise breaks down muscle tissue, which floods the blood with **creatine kinase (CK)** — a marker that can read many times above normal after heavy lifting or a long run. The same muscle activity releases **potassium** and can raise **AST** and **creatinine**, mimicking liver, kidney, or cardiac stress that is not really there.

Strenuous exercise also shifts **glucose** and can transiently raise **inflammation** markers. None of this means you should stop exercising in general, but for the 24 hours before a draw, keep it light. A walk is fine. A max-effort session, a marathon, or your first day back in the gym is not. If your results come back with an oddly high CK or potassium and you trained hard the day before, that is almost always the explanation — and a good reason to note recent workouts when you [read your blood test results](/blog/how-to-read-blood-test-results).

## Timing: Why the Hour of Your Draw Matters

Several markers are not stable across the day — they follow a circadian rhythm, so the clock time of your draw is part of the measurement. **Cortisol** is the clearest example: it peaks in the early morning and falls through the day, so a cortisol test is almost always scheduled for the morning, and comparing a 7 a.m. value to a 4 p.m. value is meaningless. **Serum iron** also tends to run higher in the morning and can swing by a meaningful margin from morning to evening.

**Testosterone** is the one men most often get wrong. It peaks in the morning and declines through the afternoon, which is exactly why lab guidelines call for morning draws — a low afternoon reading can look like a deficiency that a morning test would not show. If you are interpreting a result against a chart of [testosterone levels by age](/blog/testosterone-levels-by-age), the number is only comparable if it was drawn in the morning. The practical takeaway: book your appointment early, and if you are tracking a marker over time, always draw it at roughly the same hour so your trend reflects biology, not the clock.

## Sleep and Stress: The Overlooked Variables

The night before your test is part of your preparation, even though nothing is going into a needle yet. Poor sleep and acute stress both raise **cortisol**, and elevated cortisol raises **fasting glucose** and can nudge blood pressure and inflammation markers. Rushing to the lab, running late, or arriving anxious can measurably lift these values in the minutes before your draw.

You cannot engineer a perfect night, but you can stack the odds. Aim for a normal night of sleep, avoid a stressful late night, and give yourself enough time in the morning that you are not sprinting through a parking lot. Sit quietly for a few minutes in the waiting room before your draw. These small choices keep your stress-sensitive markers at a true baseline. Consistency also compounds over time — one reason it helps to think about [how often you should get blood work](/blog/how-often-blood-work) and to repeat tests under similar conditions, so your numbers are actually comparable from one draw to the next. For the bigger picture of how all these markers fit together, the [complete guide to blood test analysis](/blog/blood-test-analysis-complete-guide) is a useful companion.

## The Complete Checklist

Here is everything condensed into a two-part routine.

**The night before:**

- Eat a normal, lower-fat dinner and finish 8 to 12 hours before your appointment if the test requires fasting.
- Stop all food after dinner. Water only from then on.
- Avoid alcohol (ideally none for 24 to 48 hours before).
- Skip any hard workout — keep the day light.
- Aim for a normal night of sleep.
- Set out your ID, lab order, and insurance card so the morning is calm.

**The morning of:**

- Do not eat if you are fasting. Drink water freely.
- Skip coffee and tea if the test requires fasting; both are fine otherwise.
- Take your prescription medications as usual with water, unless your doctor told you not to.
- Hold high-dose biotin and other supplements if advised, and be ready to list everything you take.
- Give yourself extra time so you arrive unhurried and can sit for a few minutes before the draw.
- Eat and re-caffeinate immediately after your blood is drawn.

Preparing well is not about perfection — it is about removing the handful of variables that most often distort results. Get the fasting window right, stay hydrated, hold the coffee and alcohol, keep your prescriptions, ease off hard exercise, and draw in the morning, and the number your lab produces will actually reflect you.
