You got your lab results back. Your HbA1c is 5.7%. Your doctor mentioned the word "pre-diabetic," and now you're worried.

Before you panic: an HbA1c of 5.7% is not a death sentence. But it *is* a wake-up call—and a pivotal moment where lifestyle changes can literally change your future.

Here's what that number means, why it matters, and exactly what to do about it.

## What HbA1c Actually Measures

HbA1c (hemoglobin A1c) is a blood test that measures your average blood glucose over the past 2-3 months. It's one of the standard tests for diagnosing diabetes and a core test for monitoring long-term glucose control.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" /> A continuous glucose monitor shows the same story minute by minute — covered in [using a CGM without diabetes](/blog/cgm-without-diabetes).

You may see this same test written several different ways on a lab report — **glycohemoglobin, glycated hemoglobin, Hgb A1c, A1C, or HbA1c**. They are all the same measurement: the percentage of your hemoglobin that has glucose bound to it.<Cite id="niddk-a1c-test" /> So a glycohemoglobin of 5.7% and an HbA1c of 5.7% mean exactly the same thing. (It's also worth clearing up one common mix-up: an A1c of 5.7% is not the same as a *hemoglobin* of 5.7 g/dL — that second number would signal severe anemia. This page is about the A1c percentage.)

### How It Works

Red blood cells live approximately 120 days. Glucose binds to hemoglobin (a protein in red blood cells) in a process called glycation. The more glucose in your blood over those 120 days, the more hemoglobin gets glycated.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" /> When your doctor measures HbA1c, they're essentially measuring what percentage of your hemoglobin has been "sugar-coated."

It's elegant because it can't be gamed. You can't fake a good HbA1c result by eating well the day before your test. It's a weighted average of roughly the past 3 months, with recent weeks counting most.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />

### Why It's Better Than Fasting Glucose

Fasting glucose tells you your blood sugar *right now*. It's useful but incomplete—like checking the weather on one day and assuming that's the climate for the whole season.

HbA1c is like an average temperature over 90 days. In a large US cohort it predicted diabetes at least as well as fasting glucose and was more strongly linked to cardiovascular disease.<Cite id="selvin-2010-a1c-aric" />

## HbA1c Classification (ADA 2025 Standards of Care)

The American Diabetes Association provides clear, evidence-based thresholds:

- **Normal**: &lt;5.7%
- **Prediabetes**: 5.7% – 6.4%
- **Diabetes**: ≥ 6.5%<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />

At 5.7%, you're right at the threshold. You're not diabetic. But you're not in the normal range either.

## Is an A1c of 5.7% Bad? Is It Reversible?

Short answer: it's not bad, and yes — at this level it's often reversible.

**Is it high?** Only mildly. 5.7% is the very floor of the prediabetes band, one tenth of a percent above the normal cutoff of 5.6%. It is still well below the diabetes line of 6.5%. Think of it as the earliest possible warning light, not a red alert.

**Is it reversible?** Yes, and the evidence is unusually strong. The Diabetes Prevention Program — the landmark trial covered in detail below — found that moderate lifestyle change (at least 150 minutes of exercise a week plus a goal of at least 7% weight loss) cut progression to type 2 diabetes by **58%** over about three years, beating metformin.<Cite id="knowler-2002-dpp" /> Because 5.7% sits at the very start of the range, you have the shortest distance to travel back to normal of anyone in the prediabetes band. Many people do get back to normal: across cohort studies, 33–59% of people with prediabetes returned to normal glucose levels within one to five years.<Cite id="richter-2018-intermediate-hyperglycaemia" />

**So what should you actually do?** Don't panic, and don't ignore it either. The single number to focus on is your estimated average glucose: an A1c of 5.7% works out to roughly 117 mg/dL.<Cite id="nathan-2008-adag-eag" /> Run your own number with the free [A1C to blood sugar calculator](/tools/a1c-calculator), then use the 90-day protocol further down this page to bring it down.

## What 5.7% Means for Your Health

An HbA1c of 5.7% means your average blood glucose over 3 months has been approximately **117 mg/dL**. Run your own number with the free [A1C to blood sugar calculator](/tools/a1c-calculator).

For perspective:
- Normal fasting glucose: 70–99 mg/dL
- Normal 2-hour glucose on an oral glucose tolerance test: &lt;140 mg/dL<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />
- A 3-month average of 117 mg/dL suggests frequent post-meal glucose spikes and/or sustained elevated fasting glucose

### Your Risk

With HbA1c in the 5.7–6.4% range:
- **Type 2 diabetes risk**: in pooled cohort data, a 5-year diabetes incidence of roughly 9–25% at A1C 5.5–6.0%, rising to 25–50% at 6.0–6.5%<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />
- **Cardiovascular risk**: Already slightly elevated compared to normal glucose<Cite id="huang-2016-prediabetes-cvd" />
- **Reversibility**: Often reversible, especially with lifestyle changes<Cite id="richter-2018-intermediate-hyperglycaemia" /><Cite id="knowler-2002-dpp" />

The critical point: you're at a crossroads. The direction you go now determines whether you develop type 2 diabetes or return to normal.

## Why You're Here: Root Causes of Prediabetes

Prediabetes develops from a combination of:

### 1. **Insulin Resistance**
Your cells (especially muscle and fat cells) don't respond to insulin signals as well as they should. Your pancreas compensates by producing more insulin; glucose starts to rise once that extra insulin is no longer enough to compensate.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" /> This is often driven by:

- Excess body weight, especially belly fat (visceral adiposity)
- Sedentary lifestyle<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />
- Poor diet (high refined carbs, sugar, ultra-processed foods)
- Inflammation (often linked to lifestyle)

### 2. **Pancreatic Beta Cell Dysfunction**
Your pancreatic beta cells, which produce insulin, may not be releasing enough insulin or at the right times. This can be genetic, but lifestyle can influence progression.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />

### 3. **Genetic Predisposition**
Type 2 diabetes runs in families. If a parent, sibling, or close relative has type 2 diabetes, your risk is substantially elevated.<Cite id="cdc-prediabetes-prevent-type-2" /> But genetics are not destiny—lifestyle modifications still work even with genetic risk.<Cite id="hivert-2011-dpp-genetic-score" />

Most people with prediabetes have a combination of all three factors.

## The Good News: Proven Lifestyle Interventions

Here's what research *actually shows* works:

### The Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP)
The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program followed 3,234 people with prediabetes. They were randomly assigned, in roughly equal thirds, to an intensive lifestyle intervention, to metformin, or to placebo with standard lifestyle advice.<Cite id="knowler-2002-dpp" />

**Results after 3 years:**

- **Lifestyle intervention group**: 58% reduction in type 2 diabetes progression (71% reduction in people 60+)
- **Metformin group** (for comparison): 31% reduction<Cite id="knowler-2002-dpp" />

This is one of the most important studies in diabetes medicine. Lifestyle beats medication.

### What the Lifestyle Intervention Included:

**1. Weight Loss: at least 7% of body weight (the DPP goal)**

Even modest weight loss is transformative. If you weigh 200 lbs, losing 10-14 lbs (5-7%) significantly improves insulin sensitivity.<Cite id="cdc-prediabetes-prevent-type-2" /><Cite id="magkos-2016-moderate-weight-loss" />

How?
- Visceral fat (around organs) is highly metabolically active and inflammatory.<Cite id="ibrahim-2010-visceral-adipose" /> Losing even a small amount improves insulin signaling.
- In the DPP, each 1 kg of weight lost cut the risk of developing diabetes by about 16%.<Cite id="hamman-2006-dpp-weight-loss" />

**2. Aerobic Exercise: 150 minutes/week minimum**

The magic number: **150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity** (brisk walking, cycling, jogging, swimming).<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" /><Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity-guidelines" />

Why it works:
- Muscle is insulin-sensitive. Contracting muscle takes up glucose without needing insulin.<Cite id="colberg-2016-ada-exercise" />
- Aerobic exercise increases mitochondrial density, improving metabolic efficiency.<Cite id="colberg-2016-ada-exercise" />
- It reduces visceral fat.<Cite id="ismail-2012-exercise-visceral-fat" />
- Even one bout of exercise improves glucose control for hours afterward.<Cite id="colberg-2016-ada-exercise" />

Start with 30 minutes, 5 days/week. Progress gradually.

**3. Resistance Training: 2 days/week**

Strength training complements aerobic work:<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" />
- Builds muscle mass (which is glucose-hungry and insulin-sensitive)<Cite id="colberg-2016-ada-exercise" />
- Improves resting metabolic rate<Cite id="mackenzie-shalders-2020-exercise-rmr" />

Examples: Weight lifting, bodyweight exercises (push-ups, squats), or resistance bands. 2 sessions/week, targeting all major muscle groups.<Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity-guidelines" />

**4. Dietary Changes**

The DPP didn't prescribe a named diet; its nutrition counseling focused on cutting calories and total fat.<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" /> But research shows these principles matter:

- **Reduce refined carbs**: Replace white bread, sugary cereals, and pastries with whole grains, legumes, and vegetables.<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" />
- **Increase fiber**: Aim for about 25g daily (women) and 38g daily (men) up to age 50, or 21g and 30g after that.<Cite id="nasem-dri-macronutrients" /> Fiber slows glucose absorption.
- **Choose lean proteins**: Fish, poultry, legumes, low-fat dairy.
- **Limit added sugar**: &lt;10% of total calories (roughly &lt;50g/day for a 2000-cal diet), the WHO ceiling for free sugars.<Cite id="who-healthy-diet-fact-sheet" />
- **Emphasize whole foods**: Minimize ultra-processed foods.
- **Portion control**: Even healthy foods contribute to weight gain if portions are too large.

No diet is perfect. But consistency beats perfection.

### The Timeline: When to Expect Changes

- **2 weeks**: Day-to-day glucose can start to improve, especially after meals<Cite id="colberg-2016-ada-exercise" />
- **4-8 weeks**: HbA1c may start to improve (though full effect takes 3 months)<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />
- **3 months**: Meaningful HbA1c reduction is measurable
- **6-12 months**: Potential reversal to normal HbA1c range

## Should You Take Metformin?

Metformin is a diabetes medication often offered to people with prediabetes. It reduces diabetes progression by ~31% (DPP data). That's helpful, but less powerful than lifestyle change.

**When metformin might be considered:**

- Age 25-59 with BMI ≥35 kg/m²
- Higher fasting glucose (e.g., ≥110 mg/dL) or higher A1C (e.g., ≥6.0%)
- Prior gestational diabetes<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" />

**Important**: Metformin is not a replacement for lifestyle change—it's an adjunct. Even if you take it, diet and exercise still matter.

**Side effects**: Gastrointestinal upset (manageable with extended-release formulations), rare but serious lactic acidosis (with severe kidney disease).<Cite id="ada-2025-pharmacologic-treatment" />

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

### Retest Timing
- Retest HbA1c in **3 months** after starting interventions
- If improving, retest every 6-12 months (guidelines suggest at least yearly monitoring with prediabetes)<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" />
- If stable or worsening, reassess with your doctor (may need medication)

### Additional Tests Worth Considering

**Fasting glucose**: 100-125 mg/dL = prediabetic range
**2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)**: 140-199 mg/dL = prediabetic range<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis-classification" />
**Fasting insulin**: Elevated levels suggest insulin resistance

These can help confirm prediabetes and guide treatment intensity.

## Action Plan: Next Steps

### Week 1
- Schedule a follow-up appointment with your doctor in 3 months
- Ask for a referral to a registered dietitian if available (nutrition expertise makes a huge difference)<Cite id="ada-2025-prevention-delay" />
- Start journaling food intake (awareness is step one)

### Weeks 2-4
- Establish a walking routine: 30 minutes, 5 days/week (can be broken into 10-minute segments)
- Identify and swap 2-3 high-sugar or refined-carb foods for whole-food alternatives
- Start resistance training: 2 sessions/week (even bodyweight exercises count)

### Months 2-3
- Reassess weight (should be trending down if 5-7% loss is the goal)
- Retest HbA1c at the 3-month mark
- Evaluate progress; adjust if needed

## The Mindset Shift

HbA1c 5.7% is not a diagnosis of disease. It's a measurement of metabolic drift. And drift can be corrected.

The people who reverse prediabetes successfully don't usually go on crash diets or become obsessive about exercise. They make sustainable changes: they walk more, eat whole foods most of the time, lose a modest amount of weight, and build strength.

These changes have benefits far beyond glucose: improved energy, better sleep, reduced disease risk, improved mental health, and often—weight loss leads to increased confidence and motivation.

Your HbA1c of 5.7% is the gift of early warning. Use it.
