When you get a complete blood count back, the top of the report is easy enough to find. Red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white cells, platelets. Then, a little further down, comes a row of abbreviations that most people scroll straight past: MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW. These are the red blood cell indices, and while they look like technical noise, two of them, MCV and MCH, are quietly among the most useful numbers on the whole panel. They are the reason a doctor can often narrow down the cause of anemia before running a single additional test.<Cite id="tefferi-2005-abnormal-cbc" />

The trick is that these indices do not measure how many red cells you have. They describe what those cells are like. MCV tells you their average size. MCH tells you the average amount of hemoglobin packed inside them. Put those two descriptions together and your red cells fall into recognizable categories, and each category points toward a different set of causes. This guide explains what MCV and MCH represent, their normal ranges, and how to read them when they run high or low. For the wider panel they sit within, our [complete blood count guide](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained) is a good companion.

## MCV and MCH chart: the four patterns

Because MCV and MCH move together so reliably, the useful information is rarely in either number alone. It is in which of a small number of patterns the two of them form. This chart is the fastest way to place your own result before reading the detail underneath.

| MCV | MCH | What the cells look like | Most commonly associated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Low | Small and pale (microcytic, hypochromic) | Iron deficiency, thalassemia trait, chronic inflammation<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> |
| High | High | Large (macrocytic) | Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, regular alcohol use, liver disease, underactive thyroid<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> |
| Normal, with low hemoglobin | Normal | Normal-sized cells, simply too few (normocytic anemia) | Chronic disease, kidney disease, recent or ongoing blood loss<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> |
| Normal or borderline, with high RDW | Normal | A mix of small and large cells averaging out | Combined iron and B12 or folate deficiency<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> |

One combination is conspicuously absent from that list: a low MCV alongside a genuinely high MCH. Small cells carrying an above-average amount of hemoglobin is not a typical biological pattern, and when a report appears to show it the first question is usually analytical rather than clinical.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> A high MCHC in particular has been associated with hereditary spherocytosis and with cold agglutinins interfering with the count, and is generally rechecked before it is interpreted.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="falvella-2021-cold-agglutinins-mchc" /> If your own indices look internally contradictory, that is worth raising with the clinician who ordered the panel rather than decoding alone.

## What MCV and MCH actually measure

MCV stands for mean corpuscular volume, and it is exactly what it sounds like: the average volume, or size, of a single red blood cell. It is measured in femtoliters, a unit so small it is hard to picture, but the comparison between cells is what matters. Cells that are smaller than normal are called microcytic, cells within the normal band are normocytic, and cells that are larger than normal are called macrocytic. That one word, describing size, is often the first fork in the diagnostic road.<Cite id="tefferi-2005-abnormal-cbc" />

MCH stands for mean corpuscular hemoglobin, the average mass of hemoglobin inside each red cell. Hemoglobin is the iron-containing protein that actually carries oxygen, and it is also what gives blood its red color, so MCH is closely tied to how much oxygen-carrying pigment each cell holds. When cells carry a normal amount they are called normochromic, and when they are pale and underfilled they are called hypochromic.

In practice, MCV and MCH tend to move together, because a smaller cell usually holds less hemoglobin and a larger cell usually holds more. A third index, MCHC, describes the concentration of hemoglobin rather than the raw amount, and RDW describes how much the cell sizes vary from one another.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> But MCV and MCH are the two that do most of the storytelling.

## Normal ranges for MCV and MCH

Reference ranges differ slightly between laboratories, and your report will always print its own, so treat the numbers below as typical rather than absolute.

For adults, MCV usually falls in the range of about 80 to 95 femtoliters, and some labs extend the upper end toward 100.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> Below roughly 80 is considered microcytic, and above roughly 100 is considered macrocytic.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> MCH generally runs around 27 to 32 picograms per cell in adults, regardless of sex.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" /> Because MCH tracks size so closely, a low MCH usually accompanies a low MCV, and a high MCH usually accompanies a high MCV.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

A value that sits just outside the reference range is not automatically a problem. Small deviations are common and are read in the context of your hemoglobin, your symptoms, and the rest of the panel. What makes these indices powerful is not a single borderline reading but the pattern they form together, and how far outside the range they sit.

## What does MCH mean on a blood test?

MCH appears on the report as a single figure in picograms, and it is derived rather than directly measured: the analyser divides your total hemoglobin by your red blood cell count.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> A typical adult result sits somewhere around 27 to 32 pg, and your own report will print the range its laboratory uses.

Read on its own, MCH answers one question. How much hemoglobin, on average, is each of your cells actually carrying? A low MCH means the cells are underfilled, which in practice points the same direction as a low MCV, toward iron availability first. A high MCH means each cell carries more hemoglobin than usual, and that almost always reflects cells that are simply bigger, so it points toward the macrocytic causes rather than toward anything specific about hemoglobin itself.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

MCH is easy to confuse with MCHC, which usually sits directly beneath it. MCH is the amount of hemoglobin per cell; MCHC is its concentration, the amount relative to the volume that cell occupies.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> A large cell can hold plenty of hemoglobin in absolute terms without being any more concentrated, which is why MCH can rise while MCHC stays normal on the same panel without either being an error.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

## Low MCV and MCH: small, pale cells

When MCV and MCH are both low, the red cells are small and underfilled with hemoglobin, a combination described as microcytic and hypochromic. This is one of the most common abnormal patterns on a blood count, and it has a short list of usual suspects.

Iron deficiency is the most frequent cause.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Hemoglobin needs iron to be built, so when iron runs short the body produces smaller, paler cells, and MCV and MCH both drift down. This is often an early hint of an iron problem on the CBC (a widening RDW can come even earlier), and it pairs naturally with the iron studies.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> If your indices are low, checking ferritin is a logical next step.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Our reads on a [ferritin of 30](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) and on [low ferritin with normal hemoglobin](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin) explain how iron stores relate to what the CBC is showing.

The other classic cause of a low MCV is thalassemia, an inherited condition affecting hemoglobin production. A characteristic clue is a very low MCV that seems out of proportion to only mild anemia, which can help distinguish it from simple iron deficiency.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Chronic conditions and some inflammatory states can also lower the indices, though these more often produce normal-sized cells.<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> The key point is that small, pale cells send you toward iron and hemoglobin questions first.

When a low MCV needs sorting between iron deficiency and thalassemia trait, one long-standing shortcut is the Mentzer index: MCV divided by the red blood cell count.<Cite id="mentzer-1973-index" /> A result above roughly 13 has been associated with iron deficiency, and a result below roughly 13 with thalassemia trait, on the reasoning that thalassemia tends to produce many small cells while iron deficiency produces fewer of them.<Cite id="vehapoglu-2014-mentzer-index" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> It is a rule of thumb rather than a test. Published evaluations put its accuracy well short of definitive.<Cite id="hoffmann-2015-discriminant-indices" /><Cite id="vehapoglu-2014-mentzer-index" /> It is useful mainly as an explanation of why a clinician may read your MCV alongside your RBC count rather than in isolation, and why iron studies settle the question where an index only suggests it.


## High MCV and MCH: large cells

When MCV and MCH are elevated, the red cells are larger than normal, a pattern called macrocytic. Large cells sound like they might be a good thing, but they usually signal that red cell production has been disrupted, and the causes are quite different from the microcytic list.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

The two nutritional heavyweights are vitamin B12 and folate. Both are needed for red cells to divide and mature properly, and when either is deficient the cells grow large and are released before they are finished.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-b12" /> This is why an unexplained macrocytosis often prompts a check of B12 and folate, and our guide to a [vitamin B12 level of 300](/blog/vitamin-b12-level-300) covers where that particular marker sits. Beyond nutrients, a high MCV is commonly associated with regular alcohol use, liver disease, and an underactive thyroid, each of which can enlarge red cells through its own mechanism. Certain medications can do the same.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

One honest caveat worth knowing: in older adults, an elevated MCV is not always explained by any of these. Research notes that a meaningful share of older patients have a raised MCV without an identifiable cause.<Cite id="mahmoud-1996-macrocytosis-elderly" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> That does not mean it should be ignored, only that a mildly high value on its own is a prompt to look further rather than a diagnosis in itself.

## Normal MCV with anemia, and why RDW matters

It is entirely possible to be anemic while your MCV and MCH sit squarely in the normal range. This is called normocytic anemia, and it has its own set of causes, including anemia of chronic disease, kidney disease, and recent or ongoing blood loss before the cells have had time to change size.<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> A normal MCV, in other words, does not rule out a problem. It simply steers the investigation in a different direction.

There is also a subtler trap hidden in the word average. MCV and MCH are means, and a mean can conceal a mix. If you have some small cells and some large cells at the same time, perhaps from a combined iron and B12 deficiency, the two can average out to a deceptively normal MCV. This is exactly where RDW, the red cell distribution width, earns its keep. RDW measures how much the cell sizes vary, so a high RDW alongside a normal MCV is a signal that a single average is hiding two different populations of cells.<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /><Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" /> Reading MCV, MCH, and RDW together, rather than any one alone, is what keeps a mixed picture from slipping through.

## What to do: putting the indices together

The most useful way to read MCV and MCH is as a starting direction rather than a final answer. Low and small points toward iron and hemoglobin questions. High and large points toward B12, folate, alcohol, liver, and thyroid. Normal but anemic points toward chronic disease, kidney, or blood loss. And a high RDW is your reminder to check whether an average is masking a mix.

From there, the indices connect naturally to the rest of your bloodwork. Low values pair with iron studies. High values pair with vitamin levels and sometimes thyroid tests. And the whole red cell story sits next to your hemoglobin and hematocrit, which our guides on a [hemoglobin of 14](/blog/hemoglobin-14-normal) and on [hematocrit meaning](/blog/hematocrit-levels-high-low-meaning) unpack in more depth. None of these numbers is a diagnosis on its own, and abnormal results always deserve interpretation by a clinician who can see your full history and symptoms.

What you can do is understand what your indices are describing, so a low MCV or a high MCH is a clue you can follow rather than a mystery you skip past.
