Normal blood test ranges are the values that about 95% of healthy people fall within at a given lab: for example, hemoglobin about 13.2-16.6 g/dL in men and 11.6-15.0 g/dL in women, fasting glucose 70-99 mg/dL, and total cholesterol under 200 mg/dL. Exact limits vary by lab, age, and sex, so compare each result with the range printed on your own report.

Every blood test report comes with a reference range printed next to your result. If your number falls within the range, it gets a neutral checkmark. If it falls outside, it gets a flag.

But those reference ranges are not health targets. They are statistical norms derived from the tested population. Being "in range" means a value is typical for that population. It does not, on its own, tell you how healthy you are. And for many markers, the [difference between normal and optimal](/blog/blood-tests-normal-but-feel-terrible) is where the most important health information lives.

This guide covers every common blood test marker, with standard reference ranges from published sources and, where one is commonly used, a clearly labeled target some clinicians prefer. If you want the interpretation method rather than the raw numbers, start with [how to read your blood test results](/blog/how-to-read-blood-test-results).

## Complete blood count (CBC)

| Marker | Normal range (men) | Normal range (women) | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RBC** | 4.35-5.65 M/µL | 3.92-5.13 M/µL | Mid-range |
| **Hemoglobin** | 13.2-16.6 g/dL | 11.6-15.0 g/dL | Mid-range |
| **Hematocrit** | 38.3-48.6% | 35.5-44.9% | Mid-range |
| **MCV** | 80-100 fL | 80-100 fL | Mid-range |
| **WBC** | 4,000-10,000/µL | 4,000-10,000/µL | Mid-range |
| **Platelets** | 150,000-400,000/µL | 150,000-400,000/µL | Mid-range |
| **RDW** | 12-15% | 12-15% | Lower half of range |

Sources: [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/complete-blood-count/about/pac-20384919) (RBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit) and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/4053-complete-blood-count) (MCV, WBC, platelets, RDW). Labs differ slightly, for example Mayo reports a WBC range of 3,400-9,600/µL.

If a flagged value has you worried, [is my blood test result actually bad?](/blog/is-my-blood-test-result-bad) explains how to read the flag.

**Key insight**: A higher RDW (red cell distribution width) has been associated with higher all-cause mortality in some observational studies, so it is sometimes called an underappreciated marker. It is not a diagnosis on its own.

## Metabolic panel

| Marker | Normal range | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fasting glucose** | 70-99 mg/dL | Lower half of normal range |
| **HbA1c** ([5.7% or above is prediabetes](/blog/hba1c-5-7-pre-diabetic)) | Below 5.7% | Lower half of normal range |
| **Fasting insulin** | Varies by lab (often about 2.6-24.9 µIU/mL) | No standard target |
| **HOMA-IR** | No formal cutoff (calculated) | Lower values suggest better insulin sensitivity |
| **BUN** | 6-20 mg/dL | Mid-range |
| **Creatinine** | Varies by lab and muscle mass (often about 0.7-1.3 mg/dL in men, 0.6-1.1 in women) | Stable over time |
| **eGFR** | 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or above | Higher is generally better |
| **Cystatin C** | Varies by lab (often about 0.6-1.0 mg/L) | No standard target |
| **Uric acid** | 4.0-8.6 mg/dL (M), 3.0-7.1 (F) | Mid-range |
| **Sodium** | 135-145 mEq/L | Mid-range |
| **Potassium** | 3.7-5.2 mEq/L | Mid-range |
| **Calcium** | 8.8-10.3 mg/dL | Mid-range |

Sources: MedlinePlus pages for [BUN](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003474.htm), [uric acid](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003476.htm), [sodium](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003481.htm), [potassium](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003484.htm), [calcium](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003477.htm) and [glucose](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003482.htm), and the [American Diabetes Association](https://www.diabetes.org/diabetes/a1c/diagnosis) for HbA1c and fasting glucose.

**Key insight**: A fasting glucose of 90 with a fasting insulin of 15 can look normal on paper while the HOMA-IR calculation points toward reduced insulin sensitivity. Some clinicians therefore look at fasting insulin alongside glucose, though there is no agreed standard cutoff.

## Lipid panel

| Marker | Normal range | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total cholesterol** | Below 200 mg/dL | Context-dependent |
| **LDL-C** | Below 100 mg/dL | Lower is often advised for people at higher cardiovascular risk (discuss with your clinician) |
| **HDL-C** | 40 or above (M), 50 or above (F) | Higher within range |
| **Triglycerides** | Below 150 mg/dL | Lower end of normal |
| **ApoB** | Below 130 mg/dL (130 or above is a risk-enhancing factor in ACC/AHA guidance) | Lower is generally associated with lower risk |
| **Lp(a)** | Below 50 mg/dL (50 or above is a risk-enhancing factor in ACC/AHA guidance) | Largely genetically determined |
| **Non-HDL-C** | Below 130 mg/dL | Context-dependent |

Sources: [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/11920-cholesterol-numbers-what-do-they-mean) (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL), [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/triglycerides-test/) (triglycerides) and ACC/AHA 2018 cholesterol guideline (apoB 130 mg/dL or above and Lp(a) 50 mg/dL or above as risk-enhancing factors; non-HDL below 130 mg/dL is a long-used rule of thumb, not a diagnostic cutoff).

**Key ratios** (informal rules of thumb, not diagnostic):
- Total cholesterol / HDL: lower is generally better
- Triglyceride / HDL: lower values are associated with better insulin sensitivity

**Key insight**: [ApoB counts the number of atherogenic particles](/blog/apob-heart-disease-risk) and in some studies tracks cardiovascular risk more closely than LDL-C. Many guidelines suggest measuring Lp(a) at least once in adulthood.

## Liver panel

| Marker | Normal range | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|
| **ALT** | 4-36 U/L | Lower half of range |
| **AST** | 8-33 U/L | Lower half of range |
| **GGT** | 5-40 U/L | Lower half of range |
| **ALP** | 40-129 U/L | Mid-range |
| **Bilirubin (total)** | 0.1-1.2 mg/dL | Mid-range |
| **Albumin** | 3.4-5.4 g/dL | Mid-range |

Sources: MedlinePlus pages for [ALT](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003473.htm), [AST](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003472.htm), [GGT](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003458.htm), [ALP](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003470.htm), [bilirubin](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003479.htm) and [albumin](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003480.htm).

**Calcium and albumin**: about 40 percent of blood calcium is bound to protein, mostly albumin, so a low albumin can make total calcium read low. The [corrected calcium calculator](/tools/corrected-calcium-calculator) adjusts the calcium result for your albumin.

**Key insight**: ALT and GGT values in the upper part of the normal range have been associated with fatty liver and higher cardiovascular risk in some studies, which is why some clinicians pay attention to them even when they are not flagged.

## Thyroid panel

| Marker | Normal range | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|
| **TSH** | 0.4-4.8 mIU/L | No standard target |
| **Free T4** | Varies by lab and assay | Mid-range |
| **Free T3** | Varies by lab and assay | Mid-range |
| **Anti-TPO** | Varies by lab and assay (a positive result is flagged on your report) | Negative |

Source: [MedlinePlus TSH test](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003684.htm). Free T4, free T3 and anti-TPO ranges differ enough between assays that your own report is the right reference.

**Key insight**: A TSH near the upper end of the range can be worth discussing with a clinician, especially alongside symptoms or antibodies. The upper limit of the TSH reference range has been debated for years, and clinicians do not all agree on where it should sit.

## Inflammation markers

| Marker | Normal range | Target some clinicians use (not a standard) |
|---|---|---|
| **hs-CRP** | Below 3.0 mg/L (AHA/CDC: under 1.0 lower, 1.0-3.0 average, above 3.0 higher cardiovascular risk) | Below 1.0 mg/L |
| **Homocysteine** | Varies by lab (often about 5-15 µmol/L) | Lower end of range |
| **ESR** | Men: under 15 mm/hr (under 50 years), under 20 (over 50). Women: under 20 mm/hr (under 50 years), under 30 (over 50) | Low end of range |
| **Ferritin** | 30-400 ng/mL (M), 13-150 ng/mL (F) | Mid-range |

Sources: [MedlinePlus ESR](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003638.htm), [MedlinePlus ferritin](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003490.htm) and AHA/CDC guidance on hs-CRP cardiovascular risk categories.

**Key insight**: [An hs-CRP below 1.0 mg/L](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation) falls in the lower-risk category. The JUPITER trial enrolled people with LDL below 130 mg/dL and hs-CRP of 2.0 mg/L or above, which is one reason inflammation is studied as an independent risk marker. Ranges differ between the regular CRP and high-sensitivity CRP tests, so check which one you had.

|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin D (25-OH)** | 20 ng/mL or above is generally considered adequate (NIH); 12-20 is inadequate, under 12 is deficient | Very high levels (above 50 ng/mL, 125 nmol/L) are linked to possible adverse effects in emerging NIH evidence |
| **Vitamin B12** | 299-1,054 pg/mL (MedlinePlus); many labs flag under 200-250 pg/mL as low | No standard target |
| **Folate** | Varies by lab | No standard target |
| **Iron** | 59-158 mcg/dL (M), 37-145 mcg/dL (F) | Mid-range |
| **Ferritin** | 30-400 ng/mL (M), 13-150 ng/mL (F) | Mid-range |
| **Magnesium (serum)** | 1.7-2.2 mg/dL | Mid-range |

Sources: [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, vitamin D](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/), [NIH ODS, vitamin B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/), [MedlinePlus B12](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003705.htm), [MedlinePlus serum iron](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003488.htm) and [MedlinePlus magnesium](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003487.htm).

If your iron result is in µmol/L, the [iron unit converter](/tools/iron-unit-converter) turns it into µg/dL (and explains why serum iron cannot be converted into ferritin).

**Key insight**: Serum magnesium is a poor reflection of total body magnesium (most is intracellular). A "normal" serum magnesium does not rule out low stores. RBC magnesium is sometimes used instead but is less commonly available.

## What biomarkers are usually included in a comprehensive blood panel?

A comprehensive blood panel usually combines a complete blood count (CBC), a comprehensive metabolic panel (glucose, kidney markers, electrolytes, liver enzymes), a lipid panel, and HbA1c. Many panels add thyroid, vitamin, iron, and inflammation tests. Exactly what is included depends on the lab and on what your clinician orders.

- **CBC**: red cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white cells, platelets
- **Comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP)**: glucose, BUN, creatinine, sodium, potassium, calcium, ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin, albumin
- **Lipid panel**: total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides (sometimes ApoB or Lp(a))
- **HbA1c**: average blood sugar over about three months
- **Thyroid**: TSH, often with free T4
- **Vitamins and minerals**: vitamin D, vitamin B12, ferritin and iron
- **Inflammation**: hs-CRP

## Why blood test reference ranges differ between labs

Blood test reference ranges are usually set as the central 95% of results from a group of healthy people, which means about 1 in 20 healthy people will fall slightly outside the range for any single marker. Each lab builds or adopts its range using its own instruments, reagents, and reference population, so the same sample can be flagged at one lab and not at another. Ranges also shift with age and sex, as with hemoglobin, testosterone, ESR, and ferritin. Units matter too: glucose in mg/dL versus mmol/L, or iron in mcg/dL versus µmol/L, can look very different for the same result. This is why the range printed on your own report is the primary comparison, and why using the same lab over time makes trends easier to read.

## How to use this chart

1. **Compare to YOUR lab's ranges** — your lab's printed reference range is the primary comparison. This chart provides context.
2. **Look at trends, not snapshots** — one abnormal value means little. A trending change over 2–3 tests is meaningful.
3. **Context matters** — a marathon runner's blood work looks different from a sedentary person's. Recent illness, medication, exercise, fasting status, and time of day all affect results.
4. **Normal is not the same as ideal** — the last column of each table lists targets some clinicians use. They are not established standards, so discuss any personal target with your clinician.
