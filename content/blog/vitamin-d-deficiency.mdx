faq:
  - q: "What is a normal vitamin D level?"
    a: "It depends whose framework you use, and the two mainstream ones genuinely disagree. The Institute of Medicine, followed by the NIH, treats 25(OH)D below 12 ng/mL as deficient, 12 to 20 ng/mL as inadequate, and 20 ng/mL or above as sufficient for bone and general health. The Endocrine Society's 2011 clinical guideline set deficiency below 20 ng/mL and sufficiency at 30 ng/mL or above for at-risk patients; in 2024 the Society stopped endorsing that 30 ng/mL definition and now sets no target level at all. The IOM number answers 'what does a population need for bone health', the 2011 Endocrine Society number answered 'what should I aim for in a patient I am already treating'."
  - q: "How much vitamin D should I take to raise my level?"
    a: "In the 0 to 1,000 IU per day range, each 1,000 IU raises serum 25(OH)D by roughly 12 nmol/L (about 5 ng/mL) on average, and the response flattens sharply at higher doses. Body weight matters a great deal: a 2014 PLOS ONE dose-response analysis found 13.1 nmol/L per 1,000 IU in normal-weight adults, 11.5 in overweight adults, and 8.6 in adults with obesity. The RDA is 600 IU daily for adults up to 70 and 800 IU thereafter, with a tolerable upper intake level of 4,000 IU per day for anyone over 9."
  - q: "Should I get my vitamin D tested?"
    a: "The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline recommends against routine 25(OH)D screening in healthy adults, and the USPSTF concluded in 2021 that evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic adults. Testing is still clearly warranted if you have malabsorption, chronic kidney disease, a history of bariatric surgery, osteoporosis, long-term corticosteroid use, or symptoms suggesting osteomalacia such as bone pain and proximal muscle weakness."
  - q: "Does vitamin D supplementation actually prevent disease?"
    a: "It reliably prevents rickets and osteomalacia in people who are genuinely deficient. Beyond that, the large randomized trials have mostly been null. VITAL, which gave 25,871 adults 2,000 IU daily for a median 5.3 years, found no reduction in invasive cancer, major cardiovascular events, or fractures. The clearest positive signals so far are a 15% reduction in progression to type 2 diabetes in people with prediabetes, and a borderline 22% reduction in incident autoimmune disease in VITAL."
  - q: "Can you take too much vitamin D?"
    a: "Yes. Toxicity is real but requires sustained very high intake, typically producing serum 25(OH)D above 150 ng/mL, and presents as hypercalcemia, kidney stones, and kidney injury. There is also evidence that very large intermittent doses cause harm at levels well short of classic toxicity: a randomized trial of a single annual 500,000 IU dose in older women increased falls and fractures rather than reducing them. Daily lower-dose supplementation is preferred over infrequent megadoses."

Vitamin D is one of the most misunderstood molecules in consumer health, and it is misunderstood in both directions. It is oversold as a fix for almost everything, and simultaneously under-treated in the small group of people who are genuinely, clinically deficient.

Despite the name, it is not really a vitamin. It is a prohormone your skin synthesizes from UVB light and your liver converts to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form your blood test measures.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> What follows is what your number actually means, what changed in the guidelines in 2024, and what the evidence says supplementation does and does not achieve.

## How common deficiency really is

The "one billion people are deficient" figure gets repeated without a threshold attached, which is what makes it meaningless. The threshold decides everything.

A 2023 pooled analysis in *Frontiers in Nutrition* covering 7.9 million participants across population-based studies from 2000 to 2022 gives the clearest picture:<Cite id="cui-2023-global-prevalence" />

| Serum 25(OH)D | Global prevalence below this level |
|---|---|
| Under 12 ng/mL (30 nmol/L) | 15.7% |
| Under 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) | 47.9% |
| Under 30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L) | 76.6% |

So "roughly 80% are deficient" is true only if you define deficiency at 30 ng/mL, which most official bodies do not. Under the IOM threshold, it is closer to 16%.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

The United States looks better than the global average. NHANES data cited by the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements put about 5% of Americans at risk of deficiency and 18% at risk of inadequacy.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> Rates climb with higher latitude, darker skin pigmentation, obesity, indoor work, and age.<Cite id="cui-2023-global-prevalence" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

## What your number means

| 25(OH)D (ng/mL) | IOM / NIH framing | Endocrine Society (2011) framing |
|---|---|---|
| Under 12 | Deficient — rickets and osteomalacia risk | Deficient |
| [12 to 20](/blog/vitamin-d-level-20) | Inadequate for bone health | Deficient |
| 20 to 29 | Sufficient | Insufficient |
| [30 to 50](/blog/vitamin-d-level-30) | Sufficient | Sufficient |
| Above 50 | Potential adverse effects, especially above 60 | Sufficient (the range runs to 100) |
| Above 150 | Toxicity range | Above the 30–100 sufficiency range |

The disagreement in that table is not sloppiness. The IOM asked a population question — what intake covers the bone health needs of 97.5% of people — and answered 20 ng/mL.<Cite id="ross-2011-iom-dri" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> The Endocrine Society asked a clinical question about patients already flagged as at risk and answered 30 ng/mL.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" /> Neither number was ever validated against hard outcomes in a trial, which is the crux of the whole debate.<Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" /> Our piece on [30 ng/mL specifically](/blog/vitamin-d-30-low) works through what sits in that contested band, and the broader gap between lab-normal and optimal is covered in [optimal versus normal ranges](/blog/optimal-vs-normal-blood-test-ranges).

## What changed in 2024

In June 2024 the Endocrine Society published a new guideline that reversed much of its own 2011 position.<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" /> Its main conclusions:

- **Do not routinely screen** 25(OH)D in healthy adults, including adults with obesity or darker skin. The stated reason is blunt: levels that produce outcome-specific benefits "have not been established in clinical trials."<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" />
- **No target serum level** is specified for disease prevention, and the Society no longer endorses its 2011 definitions of "sufficiency" (30 ng/mL or above) and "insufficiency" (between 20 and 30 ng/mL).<Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" />
- Empiric supplementation without testing is suggested for four groups: children and adolescents aged 1 to 18, adults 75 and older, people who are pregnant, and adults with high-risk prediabetes.
- Adults aged 50 to 74 and non-pregnant adults under 50 should take the RDA and otherwise do nothing special.
- For adults 50 and older who need vitamin D, daily lower doses are preferred over infrequent high doses.

The USPSTF reached a compatible conclusion in 2021, issuing an "I" statement: insufficient evidence to weigh the benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic adults.<Cite id="krist-2021-uspstf-vitamin-d" />

This is the real meaning of the headline on this article. Your doctor probably will not test you, because current guidance tells them not to. That is a defensible position for population-level policy. It is less satisfying if you are an individual who wants to know where you stand.

## What supplementation actually does

Here the evidence is genuinely mixed, and it is worth being precise about which claims hold.

**Strong.** Correcting true deficiency prevents and treats rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults. This is not contested.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

**Null in large trials.** VITAL randomized 25,871 adults to 2,000 IU of vitamin D3 daily or placebo for a median 5.3 years. It found no reduction in invasive cancer and no reduction in major cardiovascular events.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" /> A 2022 *NEJM* analysis of the same cohort found no reduction in total, non-vertebral, or hip fractures in generally healthy adults who were not selected for low vitamin D.<Cite id="leboff-2022-vital-fractures" />

**Modest and borderline.** The VITAL autoimmune substudy, published in the *BMJ* in 2022, found 123 confirmed autoimmune disease cases in the vitamin D arm versus 155 on placebo — an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.78 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.99, P=0.05).<Cite id="hahn-2022-vital-autoimmune" /> A real signal, but one sitting exactly on the significance boundary.

**The clearest positive.** A 2023 individual-participant meta-analysis in *Annals of Internal Medicine* pooled three randomized trials in people with prediabetes and found vitamin D reduced progression to type 2 diabetes by 15% (HR 0.85, 0.75 to 0.96), a 3-year absolute risk reduction of 3.3%. The same paper reported a much larger effect (76% reduction) among participants who maintained 25(OH)D at or above 50 ng/mL — but that comparison is between people who achieved a level, not between randomized groups, so it cannot be read as proof that chasing 50 ng/mL causes the benefit.<Cite id="pittas-2023-prediabetes" />

**Harm at the top end.** More is not better. A randomized trial of a single annual 500,000 IU dose in older women increased falls and fractures.<Cite id="sanders-2010-annual-high-dose" /> This is why the 2024 guideline explicitly prefers daily low doses.<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" />

The fair summary: vitamin D is a cheap, safe correction for a real deficiency and a poor general-purpose preventive drug.

## Raising a genuinely low level

If your level is low and you and your clinician decide to treat it:

- **Use D3 (cholecalciferol)**, which raises and maintains 25(OH)D more effectively than D2.<Cite id="tripkovic-2012-d2-d3" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- **Dose to your body size.** In the 0 to 1,000 IU range, each 1,000 IU per day raises 25(OH)D by about 12 nmol/L (roughly 5 ng/mL). A 2014 *PLOS ONE* analysis found that response varies by weight: 13.1 nmol/L per 1,000 IU in normal-weight adults, 11.5 in overweight, and 8.6 in adults with obesity. To reach 100 nmol/L (40 ng/mL) took roughly 2,080 IU daily at normal weight versus 5,473 IU with obesity.<Cite id="ekwaru-2014-body-weight" />
- **Respect the ceiling.** The RDA is 600 IU daily for adults to age 70 and 800 IU after, with a tolerable upper intake level of 4,000 IU per day. Doses above that belong under clinical supervision.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- **Take it with a fat-containing meal.** Absorption is meaningfully better.<Cite id="dawson-hughes-2015-dietary-fat" />
- **Retest at three months, not three weeks.** 25(OH)D has a half-life of about two weeks (15 days), so a new steady state takes about three months to establish.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

Deficiencies also cluster, which is why it is worth checking B12 at the same time — [the two overlap more often than most people expect](/blog/vitamin-d-b12-deficiency-together). If you are about to build a supplement routine, [get baseline bloods first](/blog/blood-tests-before-starting-supplements).

## K2, magnesium, and sunlight

**Vitamin K2.** The theory is elegant: D3 increases calcium absorption, K2 activates matrix Gla protein, which helps keep calcium out of artery walls.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" /> The human outcome data are weaker than the theory. A randomized trial of K2 plus D did not show the slowing of aortic valve calcification or coronary artery calcium progression that the mechanism predicts.<Cite id="diederichsen-2022-avadec" /> K2 is inexpensive and, unless you take warfarin, safe, so taking it is reasonable — but "always pair D3 with K2" overstates what has actually been demonstrated.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" /> We cover the [D3 and K2 evidence in detail here](/blog/vitamin-k2-d3-together).

**Magnesium.** Magnesium is a cofactor for the enzymes that hydroxylate vitamin D in the liver and kidney, so poor magnesium status can blunt your response to supplementation.<Cite id="uwitonze-2018-magnesium-vitamin-d" /><Cite id="dai-2018-magnesium-vitamin-d" /> If you are supplementing and your level will not move, this is worth checking. [Different magnesium forms](/blog/magnesium-types-compared) differ in absorption, though the evidence comes from small studies.<Cite id="ods-magnesium" />

**Sunlight.** Roughly 5 to 30 minutes of midday sun on the face, arms and legs, at least twice a week, is the heuristic some researchers suggest.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> But it is heavily conditional: at Boston's latitude (42°N) and farther north, winter sunlight produces essentially no vitamin D regardless of exposure time, and darker skin requires considerably longer.<Cite id="webb-1988-latitude" /><Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" /> Sunscreen is a smaller factor than commonly claimed, because real-world application is thinner than test conditions.<Cite id="neale-2019-sunscreen" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

Vitamin D status also interacts with inflammatory signalling, which is one reason low D and [elevated CRP](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation) often turn up on the same panel.<Cite id="zhou-2023-vitamin-d-crp" />

## When testing is genuinely worth it

Ask for a 25(OH)D test if you have malabsorption (celiac, Crohn's, prior bariatric surgery), chronic kidney or liver disease, osteoporosis or a fragility fracture, long-term corticosteroid or antiepileptic use, dark skin with limited sun exposure at high latitude (a 2011 criterion the 2024 guideline no longer supports), or unexplained bone pain with proximal muscle weakness.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" /><Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" /> It is also a reasonable inclusion in a broader [longevity blood panel](/blog/longevity-blood-panel) if you are already testing, and our guide to [how often to get blood work](/blog/how-often-blood-work) covers sensible intervals.
