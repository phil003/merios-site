MCV, or mean corpuscular volume, is the average size of your red blood cells, and it is reported on every complete blood count. In adults, a normal MCV is about 80 to 100 femtoliters (fL); below 80 is called microcytosis and above 100 is called macrocytosis, although each lab prints its own reference range.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

Of the four red cell indices near the bottom of a CBC, MCV is the one clinicians lean on most, because cell size is the first fork in working out why someone might be anemic. Small cells, normal cells and large cells each come with their own short list of usual explanations.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> This guide covers what MCV measures, the ranges, what high and low MCV are commonly associated with, how RDW sharpens the picture, and why the direction your MCV is moving across reports can matter as much as any single value. If your MCH is flagged too, our hub on [MCV and MCH patterns together](/blog/mcv-mch-blood-test-meaning) covers the combinations.

## What is MCV in a blood test?

MCV in a blood test is the average volume of a single red blood cell, reported in femtoliters, one quadrillionth of a liter. A typical adult result falls between about 80 and 100 fL. It is tied directly to two other numbers on your report, your hematocrit and your red blood cell count.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

The relationship looks like this:

**MCV (fL) = hematocrit (%) × 10 ÷ red blood cell count (millions per µL)**

So a hematocrit of 42% with a red cell count of 4.7 million per µL gives an MCV of about 89 fL, comfortably normal. The formula is useful mostly as a reminder that MCV is tied to the rest of the red cell section: anything that distorts the hematocrit or the count can nudge it.

Red cells within the normal size band are called **normocytic**, smaller ones **microcytic** and larger ones **macrocytic**. Those three words are how MCV gets used in practice. A clinician looking at a low hemoglobin will usually glance at MCV next, because it tells them whether to think about iron and hemoglobin production, about B12, folate, alcohol and the liver, or about the causes that leave cell size untouched.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> For every other line on the panel, our [CBC results guide](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained) walks through them one by one.

One point that is easy to miss: MCV is an average. It describes the typical cell, not every cell. A sample with a mix of small and large cells can average out to a normal MCV, which is exactly why it is read next to RDW, covered further down.<Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" />

## MCV normal range: low, normal and high at a glance

A normal adult MCV is roughly 80 to 100 fL. Cleveland Clinic and StatPearls both use 80 to 100, while MedlinePlus lists 79 to 95. A result below about 80 is read as low and one above about 100 as high, always against your own lab's range.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

The table below puts the range, the most common associations and the usual next checks in one place, so you can place your own result before reading the detail.

| MCV result | Range (fL) | What it describes | Commonly associated with | What is usually checked next |
|------------|------------|-------------------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| Low | Below 80 | Microcytosis: smaller red cells | Iron deficiency, thalassemia trait; less often chronic inflammation, lead, sideroblastic anemia | Ferritin first, then iron, TIBC and transferrin saturation; sometimes hemoglobin electrophoresis |
| Normal | 80 to 100 | Normocytic: typical cell size | If hemoglobin is low: chronic disease, kidney disease, recent blood loss | RDW, reticulocyte count and kidney function if anemia is present |
| High | Above 100 | Macrocytosis: larger red cells | Alcohol, low B12 or folate, medications; also liver disease, hypothyroidism, bone marrow disorders | B12 and folate, reticulocyte count, liver and thyroid tests, a blood smear |

The low and high rows follow standard reviews of microcytosis and macrocytosis; the normal row follows a review of normocytic anemia.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /><Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" />

MCV is reported in femtoliters on US reports and on most international reports alike. Some older reports write the same number as µm³ (cubic micrometers), which is an identical unit, so 88 µm³ and 88 fL mean the same thing.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

A value one or two femtoliters outside the range is common and often not the headline of the result. An MCV of 101 with a normal hemoglobin and normal B12 is a different situation from an MCV of 112 with a hemoglobin of 10. Children are read against age-specific pediatric ranges, which differ from the adult band, so a child's result should always be compared with the range for their age.

## High MCV (macrocytosis): what it means and common causes

A high MCV, above about 100 fL, means your red blood cells are larger than usual. The most common explanations in adults are regular heavy alcohol use, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, and medications. Hypothyroidism, liver disease and bone marrow disorders such as myelodysplasia are among the other common causes.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /><Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" />

Macrocytosis is frequently found on a routine CBC, often while hemoglobin is still normal.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> The causes split into two broad groups, and the split is what guides follow-up.

**Megaloblastic causes: vitamin B12 and folate.** Both vitamins are needed for red cell precursors to copy their DNA and divide. When either runs short, cells keep growing but divide less, and they enter the blood oversized.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> Pernicious anemia, an autoimmune condition that blocks B12 absorption, is one classic route.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" /> A blood smear showing oval macrocytes and neutrophils with extra nuclear lobes points strongly toward this group.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> Our read on [a vitamin B12 level of 300](/blog/vitamin-b12-level-300) covers why the B12 gray zone is wider than most reports suggest.

**Non-megaloblastic causes.** This is the larger group in everyday practice:

- **Alcohol.** Regular heavy drinking is one of the most common reasons for a raised MCV, and it can enlarge red cells even when liver tests and vitamin levels are normal.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />
- **Medications.** Certain chemotherapy drugs are well known for raising MCV, and several other long-term medications can do the same.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /><Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" /> If your MCV climbed after a new prescription, that timing is worth mentioning to your clinician.
- **Liver disease and hypothyroidism.** Both are among the more common associations, which is why an unexplained high MCV often prompts liver and thyroid tests.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> Our [full thyroid panel guide](/blog/full-thyroid-panel-test) explains what those tests include.
- **A surge of young red cells.** Reticulocytes, newly released red cells, are larger than mature ones. After bleeding or during hemolysis the marrow releases many at once, and MCV can rise. A reticulocyte count helps separate this from drug or alcohol effects.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />
- **Bone marrow disorders.** Myelodysplastic syndromes and related conditions are less common but are part of the reason a persistent, unexplained macrocytosis is followed up rather than ignored.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

Age adds a nuance. In a study of 124 patients over 75 with an MCV above 95 fL, a definite cause was found through non-invasive tests in 60% of them; a further small group turned out to have myelodysplasia on bone marrow biopsy, and many of the rest had no clear explanation.<Cite id="mahmoud-1996-macrocytosis-elderly" /> A mildly high MCV in an older adult is a prompt to look further, not a conclusion.

Finally, a high MCV is sometimes an artifact. StatPearls lists red cell clumping (agglutination), very high blood glucose, high sodium and very high white cell counts as possible sources of a falsely raised MCV.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> A single high value that does not fit the rest of the panel is often simply repeated.

## Low MCV (microcytosis): what it means and common causes

A low MCV, below about 80 fL, means your red blood cells are smaller than usual. The two most common causes are iron deficiency and thalassemia trait. Anemia of chronic disease, lead toxicity and sideroblastic anemia are the other diagnoses usually considered, and serum ferritin is the first test recommended.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mcv" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

Microcytosis is typically an incidental finding in people who had a blood count for another reason.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

**Iron deficiency** leads the list. Hemoglobin cannot be built without iron, and when iron runs short the marrow produces smaller, paler cells. A low ferritin supports the diagnosis, and in adults the next question is why: iron deficiency anemia in adults is presumed to come from blood loss until shown otherwise, most often from the gastrointestinal tract, which is why it is usually followed up rather than just treated.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Heavy menstrual bleeding is a frequent explanation in people who menstruate. Our guide to [a ferritin of 30](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) covers the zone where ferritin reads normal but iron may still be running short.

**Thalassemia trait** is the other main cause. It is an inherited difference in hemoglobin production, and it typically produces small cells with only mild anemia or none at all. If ferritin is not low, further tests usually include serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation and possibly hemoglobin electrophoresis; people with beta-thalassemia trait usually have a raised hemoglobin A2.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> This is one of the reasons to confirm the cause before starting iron on your own.

**Anemia of chronic disease** can produce small cells too, though it more often leaves MCV normal. It is suggested by low iron with a decreased TIBC, a pattern that reflects iron being held back rather than missing.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" />

**Lead exposure and sideroblastic anemia** are less common but appear on every standard list of microcytic causes.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

Unlike a high MCV, a falsely low MCV is rarely an analytical artifact: StatPearls lists no common causes of a spuriously decreased value.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> A low MCV is usually a real signal worth following.

## MCV and RDW together: the second number that changes the reading

RDW, the red cell distribution width, measures how much your red cell sizes vary from one another; a typical range is about 11.5% to 15%.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> Reading MCV next to RDW was proposed in 1983 as a way to improve the classification of anemia, in particular to separate iron deficiency from thalassemia trait or chronic disease and to catch early iron or folate deficiency.<Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" />

The logic is simple. Iron deficiency and B12 or folate deficiency develop gradually, so the blood holds a mix of older normal cells and newer abnormal ones, and RDW widens. Conditions such as thalassemia trait tend to make cells uniformly small, so RDW often stays normal.<Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

| | Normal RDW (cells similar in size) | High RDW (cells vary in size) |
|---|---|---|
| **Low MCV** | Leans toward thalassemia trait or chronic disease | Leans toward iron deficiency |
| **Normal MCV** | Chronic disease, kidney disease, acute blood loss | Early iron, B12 or folate deficiency, or a mix of deficiencies |
| **High MCV** | Leans toward alcohol, liver disease, medications | Leans toward B12 or folate deficiency, or a reticulocyte surge |

These are tendencies drawn from the classic MCV and RDW classification, not rules, and there is plenty of overlap between the boxes.<Cite id="bessman-1983-mcv-rdw" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /> The middle-right box deserves the most attention: a normal MCV with a high RDW can be the average of a small-cell and a large-cell problem cancelling each other out.<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" />

## MCV vs MCH vs MCHC: a quick comparison

MCV is cell size, MCH is the amount of hemoglobin per cell, MCHC is how concentrated that hemoglobin is, and RDW is how much cell sizes vary.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

| Index | What it measures | Unit | Typical adult range |
|-------|------------------|------|---------------------|
| MCV | Average **size** of a red cell | femtoliters (fL) | about 80 to 100 |
| MCH | Average **amount** of hemoglobin per cell | picograms (pg) | about 27 to 33 |
| MCHC | Hemoglobin **concentration** in the cell | g/dL | about 32 to 36 |
| RDW | **Variation** in cell size | percent (%) | about 11.5 to 15 |

MCV and MCH usually move together, because a bigger cell holds more hemoglobin and a smaller one holds less.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" /> For MCH on its own, including how it differs from MCHC, see our [MCH blood test guide](/blog/mch-blood-test).

## When MCV is usually retested, and why the trend matters

Red blood cells circulate for roughly 120 days, so the average cell in your sample was made over the past few months. That is why MCV tends to move slowly. A real change in the cells being produced, whether from an iron shortfall, a B12 problem, a medication or a change in drinking, usually shows up as a gradual drift across reports rather than an overnight jump. It is also why a retest a week later rarely tells you much: most of the cells being measured are the same ones.

In practice, timing depends on the context. A single borderline value with an otherwise normal panel is often simply rechecked at the next routine blood test. A value that does not fit the rest of the panel may be repeated soon to rule out an artifact.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> When a cause has been found and addressed, a follow-up count is usually spaced weeks to months later, giving a new population of red cells time to replace the old one. Your clinician will set the interval that fits your situation.

The trend is also where a normal result can still be informative. An MCV that has crept from 88 to 98 over three years is telling you something that neither value says alone, even though both sit inside the reference range. The same is true in the other direction for a slow slide toward 80, which often travels with falling ferritin. Two practical tips if you are comparing reports:

- **Compare like with like.** Different labs and analyzers can differ by a few femtoliters, so a small shift after switching labs may be method, not biology.
- **Read MCV with its neighbors.** A change in MCV means more when hemoglobin, MCH and RDW move in the same direction.

## What to do with an abnormal MCV result

Treat the number as a direction, not a verdict. If your MCV is **low**, the useful questions are whether your hemoglobin is also low, what your RDW shows and what your ferritin says. If your MCV is **high**, the usual first-line checks are B12, folate, a reticulocyte count, liver and thyroid tests, along with an honest look at alcohol intake and medications.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> In both directions, review the full panel with the clinician who ordered it before changing anything on your own, including starting iron or B12 supplements, which can make the underlying cause harder to pin down afterward.
