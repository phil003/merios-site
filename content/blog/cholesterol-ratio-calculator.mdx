Your lipid panel comes back. Total cholesterol 215, LDL 130, HDL 55, triglycerides 140. Is that good? Bad? Dangerous?

Individual cholesterol numbers are hard to interpret in isolation. That is where **cholesterol ratios** come in — they tell you how the numbers relate to each other, which is often more informative than any single value.

## The three cholesterol ratios that matter

### 1. Total cholesterol / HDL ratio

The simplest and most widely used ratio.

**Formula**: Total Cholesterol ÷ HDL

| Ratio | Risk level |
|---|---|
| **Below 3.0** | Excellent — very low risk |
| **3.0–3.5** | Ideal |
| **3.5–5.0** | Acceptable — average risk |
| **Above 5.0** | Elevated risk |
| **Above 6.0** | High risk |

Clinical reviews place the higher-risk threshold at a ratio above 5.0 for men and 4.5 for women.<Cite id="millan-2009-lipoprotein-ratios" />

**Why it works**: HDL particles remove cholesterol from arteries and transport it back to the liver (reverse cholesterol transport).<Cite id="millan-2009-lipoprotein-ratios" /> A high ratio means you have lots of potentially atherogenic cholesterol relative to your protective HDL capacity.

### 2. LDL / HDL ratio

Focuses specifically on the balance between atherogenic (LDL) and protective (HDL) lipoproteins.

**Formula**: LDL ÷ HDL

| Ratio | Risk level |
|---|---|
| **Below 1.5** | Excellent |
| **1.5–2.5** | Good |
| **2.5–3.5** | Borderline |
| **Above 3.5** | Elevated risk |

The same reviews put the higher-risk LDL/HDL threshold above 3.5 for men and 3.0 for women.<Cite id="millan-2009-lipoprotein-ratios" />

**Limitation**: LDL cholesterol measures the mass of cholesterol inside LDL particles, not the number of particles. Two people can have the same LDL-C but very different particle counts — which is why ApoB is increasingly preferred over LDL-C.<Cite id="sniderman-2019-apob" />

### 3. Triglyceride / HDL ratio — the metabolic health ratio

This is the ratio that metabolic health researchers care most about. It is one of the best lipid-panel-based proxies for **[insulin resistance](/blog/homa-ir-insulin-resistance)** and **small dense LDL particles**.<Cite id="mclaughlin-2003-tg-hdl-insulin-resistance" /><Cite id="hanak-2004-tg-hdl-ldl-phenotype" />

**Formula**: Triglycerides ÷ HDL

| Ratio | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Below 1.0** | Excellent — strong insulin sensitivity |
| **1.0–2.0** | Optimal |
| **2.0–3.0** | Borderline — insulin resistance may be developing |
| **3.0–3.5** | Elevated — likely insulin resistant |
| **Above 3.5** | High — strong correlation with metabolic syndrome and small dense LDL |

**Why this ratio is so powerful**: High triglycerides and low HDL are the lipid hallmarks of insulin resistance. When triglycerides are high, the liver produces more VLDL particles, which are remodeled into small, dense LDL — a particularly atherogenic type. Simultaneously, HDL levels tend to fall, completing the pattern.<Cite id="berneis-2002-ldl-heterogeneity" /> The [TG/HDL ratio](/blog/triglyceride-hdl-ratio-calculator) captures this entire metabolic dysfunction in a single number.

A TG/HDL ratio of 3.8 or higher has been shown to identify a preponderance of small dense LDL particles (pattern B) with approximately 80% accuracy — without needing an advanced NMR lipid panel.<Cite id="hanak-2004-tg-hdl-ldl-phenotype" /> A ratio of 3.0 or higher identified insulin resistance in overweight adults, with roughly two-thirds sensitivity and specificity.<Cite id="mclaughlin-2003-tg-hdl-insulin-resistance" />

## Why ratios matter more than total cholesterol alone

Consider two patients:

**Patient A**: Total cholesterol 250, HDL 85, Triglycerides 70
- TC/HDL ratio: 2.9 (excellent)
- TG/HDL ratio: 0.8 (excellent)
- Likely very low cardiovascular risk despite "high" total cholesterol

**Patient B**: Total cholesterol 195, HDL 35, Triglycerides 250
- TC/HDL ratio: 5.6 (high risk)
- TG/HDL ratio: 7.1 (very high — metabolic syndrome likely)
- Likely high cardiovascular risk despite "normal" total cholesterol

Patient B would pass a basic cholesterol screening (total below 200).<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" /> Patient A might be flagged for "high cholesterol." The ratios tell the real story.

## Beyond ratios: ApoB and the future

Cholesterol ratios are useful, but they are still indirect measures. The field is moving toward more precise markers:

**ApoB** [directly counts atherogenic particles](/blog/apob-heart-disease-risk). One ApoB protein sits on each LDL, VLDL, IDL, and Lp(a) particle.<Cite id="sniderman-2019-apob" /> European guidelines set ApoB goals below 100, 80 and 65 mg/dL for moderate-, high- and very-high-risk people.<Cite id="mach-2019-esc-eas-dyslipidaemia" /> ApoB is increasingly regarded as the most accurate lipid marker of cardiovascular risk.<Cite id="sniderman-2011-ldl-nonhdl-apob-meta" /> If your lab used different units, the free [Lp(a) unit converter](/tools/lpa-unit-converter) shows the equivalent range.

**Non-HDL cholesterol** (total cholesterol minus HDL) captures all atherogenic cholesterol in a single number.<Cite id="mach-2019-esc-eas-dyslipidaemia" /> It predicts risk better than LDL-C, though not as well as ApoB, and is already available on any standard lipid panel.<Cite id="sniderman-2011-ldl-nonhdl-apob-meta" />

The hierarchy of predictive accuracy, from least to most precise: total cholesterol → LDL-C → non-HDL-C → ApoB.<Cite id="sniderman-2011-ldl-nonhdl-apob-meta" /> Ratios hold up well too: in data from 61 cohorts, the total/HDL ratio predicted death from heart disease better than non-HDL or total cholesterol.<Cite id="psc-2007-cholesterol-vascular-mortality" />

## How to improve your cholesterol ratios

Most interventions that improve ratios work by raising HDL, lowering triglycerides, or both:

- **Exercise** — aerobic exercise modestly raises HDL (by about 2.5 mg/dL on average) and lowers triglycerides (by about 11% in overweight adults)<Cite id="kodama-2007-exercise-hdl-meta" /><Cite id="kelley-2005-aerobic-exercise-lipids-meta" />
- **Reduce refined carbohydrates and sugar** — a core [dietary intervention for triglycerides](/blog/how-to-lower-triglycerides), since higher sugar intake raises them in controlled trials<Cite id="te-morenga-2014-sugars-lipids-meta" />
- **Lose excess weight** — even a 3 kg (about 7 lb) loss lowers triglycerides by at least 15 mg/dL on average<Cite id="jensen-2014-obesity-guideline" />
- **[Omega-3 fatty acids](/blog/omega-3-index-test)** — EPA+DHA at 2–4 g/day lowers triglycerides by roughly 15–30%<Cite id="skulas-ray-2019-aha-omega3-triglycerides" /><Cite id="wang-2023-omega3-dose-response-meta" />
- **Replace saturated fat with unsaturated fat** — lowers the total/HDL ratio<Cite id="mensink-2003-fatty-acids-tc-hdl-meta" />
- **Moderate alcohol or abstain** — alcohol raises HDL slightly but also raises triglycerides; net effect depends on amount<Cite id="rimm-1999-alcohol-lipids-meta" />
- **Increase fiber** — soluble fiber (oats, beans, psyllium) binds bile acids and lowers LDL<Cite id="gunness-2010-soluble-fibre-mechanisms" /><Cite id="brown-1999-soluble-fiber-meta" />
