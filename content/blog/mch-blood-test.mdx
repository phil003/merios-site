MCH, or mean corpuscular hemoglobin, is the average amount of hemoglobin inside one of your red blood cells, calculated from your hemoglobin and your red blood cell count. In adults, a normal MCH is about 27 to 33 picograms (pg) per cell, although each lab prints its own reference range on the report.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

It shows up on every complete blood count, in a cluster with MCV, MCHC and RDW near the bottom of the red cell section, and it is often flagged with an H or an L without a single sentence about what it measures. This guide covers what the number represents, the normal range, what a low or high MCH is commonly associated with, and how MCH differs from MCHC and MCV. If your MCV is flagged too, our companion piece on [MCV and MCH patterns together](/blog/mcv-mch-blood-test-meaning) goes deeper on the combinations.

## What is MCH in a blood test?

MCH in a blood test is the average weight of hemoglobin inside each of your red blood cells, reported in picograms. A typical adult result falls between about 27 and 33 pg. It is not measured directly: the analyzer divides your total hemoglobin by your red blood cell count, and the result appears automatically on a standard CBC.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

Hemoglobin is the iron-containing protein that picks up oxygen in your lungs and releases it in your tissues, so MCH is a measure of how well loaded your oxygen carriers are. Every red blood cell is essentially a small bag of hemoglobin, and MCH describes how much is in the average bag. The calculation looks like this:

**MCH (pg) = hemoglobin (g/dL) × 10 ÷ red blood cell count (millions per µL)**

That formula matters for interpretation. Because MCH is derived from two other numbers, anything that distorts either one distorts MCH as well.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> It also means MCH carries no information that is not already present in your hemoglobin and red cell count. Its value is as a summary: it turns two raw numbers into a single description of the cells that is easy to compare against a reference range. For the rest of the panel it sits in, our [CBC results guide](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained) walks through every line.

When each cell carries a typical amount of hemoglobin, the cells are described as normochromic. When they carry less, they are hypochromic, which literally means under-colored, because hemoglobin is also what makes blood red. Under a microscope, hypochromic cells show a wider pale center.<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />

## MCH normal range: low, normal and high

A normal MCH for adults is roughly 27 to 33 picograms per cell. Some labs use 27 to 32 pg, and MedlinePlus lists 26 to 33 pg for women and 27 to 33 pg for men. A result below about 27 is read as low and one above about 33 as high, always against your own lab's range.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" /><Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

| MCH result | Range (pg per cell) | What it describes | What usually travels with it |
|------------|---------------------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| Low | Below 27 | Hypochromic: cells carry less hemoglobin than usual | Often a low MCV; RDW and ferritin help narrow down the cause |
| Normal | 27 to 33 | Normochromic: a typical amount of hemoglobin per cell | MCV and MCHC usually also in range |
| High | Above 33 | Usually larger cells holding more hemoglobin in total | Often a high MCV, while MCHC commonly stays normal |

MCH is reported in picograms per cell on US reports and on most international (SI unit) reports alike, so unlike hemoglobin, which switches between g/dL and g/L, there is usually no unit to convert.

Labs vary. The reference range printed on your own report is the one that applies to your sample, because it reflects that lab's analyzer and the population its range was built from. Children are read against age-specific pediatric ranges rather than the adult band, so a child's result should always be compared with the range for their age.

One more thing worth saying plainly: a value one or two picograms outside the range is common and is often not the headline of your result. MCH is read in context, alongside your hemoglobin, your MCV and how you feel. An MCH of 26.5 with a perfectly normal hemoglobin is a different situation from an MCH of 26.5 with a hemoglobin of 10.

## Low MCH: what does it mean?

A low MCH, below about 27 pg, means your red blood cells carry less hemoglobin than usual, a pattern called hypochromia. It most often points toward iron deficiency and usually travels with a low MCV. Thalassemia trait and long-term inflammation are the other common explanations, so a low MCH is a reason to check ferritin with your clinician.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

**Iron deficiency** is the most common cause by a wide margin. Hemoglobin cannot be assembled without iron, so when iron runs short the bone marrow tends to produce cells that are both smaller and paler, and MCH can start to drift down before hemoglobin itself leaves the reference range. Cleveland Clinic notes that blood loss, such as heavy menstrual bleeding, is the most common reason for iron-deficiency anemia.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /> Serum ferritin is usually the first follow-up test.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Our guide to [a ferritin of 30](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) covers the gray zone where ferritin is technically normal, and [ferritin vs iron saturation](/blog/ferritin-vs-iron-saturation) explains the difference between stored and circulating iron. If your report includes serum iron and TIBC, the [transferrin saturation calculator](/tools/transferrin-saturation-calculator) turns them into a saturation percentage.

**Thalassemia trait** is the other important cause, and one that is easy to miss. It is an inherited difference in hemoglobin production, and a common clue is a disproportion: a strikingly low MCH and MCV alongside only mild anemia, or none at all.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> Iron deficiency of the same severity would usually have pulled hemoglobin down further. When the numbers do not line up that way, thalassemia trait is worth raising with your clinician, and it is one reason to confirm the cause before starting iron on your own.

**Chronic inflammation and chronic disease**, including long-term infections, kidney disease, liver disease and cancer, can lower MCH as well.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /> In these situations iron may be present in the body but less available to the marrow. This is also why ferritin alone can mislead: it rises with inflammation and can read normal even when iron is not reaching the red cells.

Less commonly, **lead exposure**, **sideroblastic anemia** and **other hemoglobin disorders** can produce a similar picture.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /> A low MCH is a direction to investigate, not a destination.

## High MCH: what does it mean?

A high MCH, above about 33 pg, usually means your red blood cells are larger than normal and so hold more hemoglobin. It almost always travels with a high MCV. Common associations include low vitamin B12 or folate, regular heavy alcohol use, liver disease, thyroid problems and some medications, such as certain chemotherapy drugs.<Cite id="cleveland-clinic-mch" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

A bigger cell has room for more hemoglobin, so MCH rises alongside MCV, and in practice a high MCH is read as a sign of macrocytosis.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> That means the causes worth looking at are the macrocytic ones.

**Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency** head the list. Both are needed for red cells to divide properly as they mature. Without enough of them, cells keep growing but divide less, and are released into circulation oversized.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> Our read on [a vitamin B12 level of 300](/blog/vitamin-b12-level-300) covers where that marker sits and why its gray zone is wider than most reports suggest.

**Regular heavy alcohol use** is one of the most common explanations for raised MCV and MCH in adults, and **medications** are another.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" /> If your MCH rose after starting a new drug, the timing is worth mentioning to your clinician. **Liver disease** and **hypothyroidism** are also among the more common associations, which is one reason an unexplained high MCV and MCH often prompts liver and thyroid tests.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

A caveat that deserves more airtime: MCH can also read falsely high for technical reasons. StatPearls lists very fatty (lipemic) samples, high bilirubin, clumping of red cells and some protein disorders as possible sources of a spuriously raised MCH.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> A single high MCH that does not match the rest of the panel is sometimes simply repeated before anyone interprets it.

## MCH vs MCHC (and MCV): what is the difference?

MCH is the total amount of hemoglobin in an average red cell, in picograms, about 27 to 33. MCHC is the concentration of hemoglobin relative to the cell's size, in g/dL, about 32 to 36. MCV is the cell's average size, in femtoliters, roughly 80 to 100. MCH depends on cell size, while MCHC corrects for it.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /><Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

| Index | What it measures | Unit | Typical adult range |
|-------|------------------|------|---------------------|
| MCV | Average **size** of a red blood cell | femtoliters (fL) | about 80 to 100 |
| MCH | Average **amount** of hemoglobin per cell | picograms (pg) | about 27 to 33 |
| MCHC | **Concentration** of hemoglobin within the cell | g/dL | about 32 to 36 |

A useful mental model is a glass of lemonade. MCV is the size of the glass. MCH is how much lemon syrup is in it. MCHC is how strong the drink tastes. A large glass can hold a lot of syrup in total, a high MCH, while still tasting no stronger than usual, a normal MCHC, which is the pattern often seen in B12 or folate deficiency.

This is why MCH rarely adds much on its own but matters as part of a pattern. MCH and MCV both low often points toward iron or thalassemia trait. Both high often points toward B12, folate, alcohol, medications, liver or thyroid. And a normal MCH with a low hemoglobin steers attention toward the normocytic causes, such as chronic disease, kidney disease and recent blood loss.<Cite id="medlineplus-rbc-indices" />

One more index earns a mention. **RDW** describes how much your cell sizes vary from one another. In small-cell patterns, a raised RDW is more typical of iron deficiency, because the marrow is producing a mix of older normal cells and newer small ones, while a normal RDW leans toward thalassemia trait, where the cells tend to be uniformly small.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> It is a clue rather than a test, but that single extra number often helps separate the two most common explanations.

## What to do with an abnormal MCH result

Start by resisting the urge to treat the number as a verdict. MCH is a calculated summary, not a diagnosis, and a single flagged value on an otherwise normal count is frequently nothing to worry about.

If your MCH is **low**, the questions worth answering are whether your hemoglobin is also low, whether your MCV is low, and what your ferritin says. Those results together usually help distinguish iron deficiency from thalassemia trait from inflammation. If iron deficiency is confirmed, the next question is why: in adults, unexplained iron deficiency is itself a finding that is usually followed up, often by looking for a source of blood loss.

If your MCH is **high**, the common first-line checks are B12, folate, liver and thyroid tests, along with an honest look at alcohol intake and medications. Those cover most of the usual explanations.<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

In both directions, a single reading means less than a trend.
