# Ferritin vs Iron Saturation: What Each Tells You You can compute yours with the free [transferrin saturation calculator](/tools/transferrin-saturation-calculator).

If you have ever looked at an iron panel, you have probably noticed it is not one number but a cluster of them: serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, total iron-binding capacity, and iron saturation. Two of these, ferritin and iron saturation, get the most attention, and they are frequently confused for each other. They both have the word iron attached, they often move in the same direction, and a single one is sometimes reported on its own. But they answer genuinely different questions about your body, and knowing which is which is the key to reading an iron panel correctly.

The cleanest way to hold the difference in your head is a supply-chain image. Ferritin is your warehouse inventory: how much iron your body has stored for later. Iron saturation, usually reported as TSAT, is the delivery fleet: how much iron is loaded up and moving through your bloodstream right now. A business can have a full warehouse and empty trucks, or a bare warehouse and busy trucks, and each situation means something different. The same is true of your iron.

## What ferritin measures: your iron warehouse

Ferritin is a protein that stores iron inside your cells, mostly in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. A small amount circulates in your blood, and that circulating amount tends to track with your total body iron stores. When your reserves are full, blood ferritin is higher. When you have been slowly losing iron, ferritin falls, often before you develop any anemia at all. That early-warning quality is why ferritin is considered the single most useful blood marker of iron stores.

The reference ranges vary by lab, but the World Health Organization set clear thresholds for deficiency. In apparently healthy adults, a ferritin concentration below 30 micrograms per liter is used to indicate iron deficiency. Some clinicians use a slightly higher cutoff when symptoms are present, but 30 is the widely cited line for depleted stores. A very low ferritin, in the mid-teens or below, signals that the warehouse is close to empty. Our detailed reads on a [ferritin of 30](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) and a [ferritin level of 15](/blog/ferritin-level-15) walk through what those specific numbers tend to mean in practice.

Ferritin has one crucial quirk that we will come back to: it is an acute-phase reactant. That means it rises in response to inflammation, infection, and liver stress, independent of how much iron you actually have. This is the single most common reason iron panels get misread.

## What iron saturation measures: iron in transit

Iron does not float freely in your blood. It rides on a carrier protein called transferrin, which acts like a fleet of trucks shuttling iron from storage and absorption sites to the bone marrow, where new red blood cells are built. Iron saturation, or transferrin saturation, is simply the percentage of that carrying capacity that is currently occupied by iron.

It is a calculated value, not a directly measured one. The formula is serum iron divided by total iron-binding capacity, multiplied by 100. If your serum iron is 85 and your total iron-binding capacity is 300, your saturation is about 28 percent, comfortably in the normal zone. Because it reflects iron that is actively available for red cell production, TSAT captures a more real-time picture than ferritin does. It answers the question, is iron actually reaching the places that need it right now.

Most labs report a normal transferrin saturation of roughly 20 to 50 percent, with the optimal band often quoted a little wider at 15 to 55 percent. A saturation below 20 percent points toward low iron availability, and below 10 percent is severely low and strongly suggests deficiency. On the other end, a saturation above 45 to 50 percent starts to raise the possibility of iron overload, above 60 percent warrants a hemochromatosis workup, and above 70 percent is markedly elevated.

## The reference ranges, side by side

Putting the two together makes their roles easier to keep straight.

Ferritin is measured in micrograms per liter and reflects stored iron. In healthy adults, below 30 suggests deficiency, a broad normal range runs from roughly 30 up into the low hundreds depending on sex and lab, and persistently high values point toward inflammation, liver disease, or overload. Because ferritin climbs with inflammation, a normal-looking number does not automatically rule out deficiency.

Iron saturation is a percentage and reflects iron in transit. Below 20 percent suggests low availability, 20 to 50 percent is the usual normal band, and above 45 to 50 percent raises the question of overload. Because it is calculated from serum iron, which fluctuates through the day and even from meal to meal, a single saturation value is best interpreted alongside ferritin rather than in isolation.

The practical takeaway is that neither number is the whole story. Ferritin tells you about reserves and iron saturation tells you about supply, and it is the combination that carries the meaning.

## Why the two can disagree, and what each pattern means

The interesting clinical information often lives in the disagreements between these two markers. Four broad patterns are worth recognizing.

Low ferritin with low iron saturation is the classic picture of absolute iron deficiency. The warehouse is empty and the trucks are running light, so there is simply not enough iron in the system. This is the pattern behind most straightforward iron-deficiency anemia, and it is what you would expect from chronic blood loss, heavy menstruation, or poor absorption.

Normal or high ferritin with low iron saturation is the pattern people miss, and it is covered in its own section below because it is so important. In brief, it suggests iron is present but locked away and unavailable.

High ferritin with high iron saturation is the signature of iron overload, including hereditary hemochromatosis. Here both the warehouse and the trucks are overloaded, and the concern flips from too little iron to too much, which can accumulate in organs over time.

High ferritin with normal or low iron saturation usually reflects inflammation, liver disease, or a metabolic driver rather than true overload. The ferritin is elevated as an inflammatory response, not because iron stores are genuinely excessive. This is exactly why a high ferritin should never be read as overload on its own.

## The inflammation problem: when ferritin lies

Because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, inflammation can push it up regardless of your real iron status. This creates one of the most clinically significant scenarios in the whole iron panel: functional iron deficiency.

In chronic inflammatory conditions, infection, kidney disease, obesity, and many other states, the body raises a hormone called hepcidin. Hepcidin effectively traps iron inside storage cells and blocks it from being released into the blood. The result is a normal or even elevated ferritin, because the warehouse is stocked, paired with a low iron saturation, because almost nothing is being loaded onto the trucks. The person can be functionally short of usable iron while their ferritin looks reassuring. If you have wondered why someone can feel deficient with a normal ferritin, this is often the answer, and it is a close cousin of the situation we describe in [low ferritin with normal hemoglobin](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin).

This is also why iron saturation earns its place on the panel. When inflammation blunts the usefulness of ferritin, saturation becomes the more honest indicator of whether iron is actually available. A low TSAT despite a normal ferritin is a strong clue that iron is present but not usable, and it changes how deficiency gets managed. ## What to do: reading them together

The single most useful habit is to stop reading either number in isolation. When you get an iron panel, look at ferritin and iron saturation as a pair and ask what story they tell together. Are stores full or empty, and is iron actually moving.

If both are low, the picture is usually straightforward deficiency. If ferritin is normal or high but saturation is low, think about inflammation and functional deficiency rather than assuming your iron is fine, and consider whether an inflammatory marker should be checked alongside. If both are high, that is the pattern that deserves a conversation about overload rather than more iron. And if you have started supplementing but your numbers are not budging the way you expected, the mismatch between stores and availability is often the reason, which we explore in [why iron supplements are not working](/blog/iron-supplements-not-working).

None of this is a substitute for a clinician who can see your full history, symptoms, and the rest of your labs. Iron is a marker where context genuinely changes the interpretation, and the safest approach is to bring an unusual pattern to a professional rather than self-treat.
