Your blood work shows CRP (C-reactive protein): 3.2 mg/L. The lab report says "normal," but you've heard that CRP measures inflammation, and inflammation causes heart disease. Should you be concerned?

CRP is one of the most widely ordered but frequently misunderstood blood markers. It tells you something important about inflammation, but not quite what most people think.

## CRP vs. hs-CRP: Which Test Did You Get?

First, clarify which test you actually had. There are two:

**Standard CRP** — Measures levels from about 5 to &gt;100 mg/L.<Cite id="fda-2005-crp-assay-review-criteria" /> It's sensitive to acute infections and major inflammation but relatively insensitive to the low-grade chronic inflammation associated with heart disease. Most emergency departments and hospitals use this to detect acute bacterial infections or severe inflammation.

**[High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP)](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation)** — A more refined version that measures even tiny amounts of CRP (from well below 1 mg/L up to about 10 mg/L).<Cite id="fda-2005-crp-assay-review-criteria" /> It's designed to detect the chronic, low-grade inflammation that predicts cardiovascular disease.<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" /> If you got a CRP specifically for cardiovascular risk assessment, you almost certainly got hs-CRP.

A CRP of 3.2 mg/L is usually reported as normal on a standard CRP test, where values up to about 8–10 mg/L are considered healthy,<Cite id="medlineplus-crp-test" /> but on hs-CRP it falls just inside the high-risk band.

## hs-CRP Cardiovascular Risk Categories

The American Heart Association (AHA) and Centers for Disease Control (CDC) define cardiovascular risk by hs-CRP as:<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

- **&lt;1.0 mg/L** — Low risk
- **1.0–3.0 mg/L** — Average risk
- **&gt;3.0 mg/L** — High risk
- **&gt;10 mg/L** — Likely acute infection or inflammation, not chronic cardiovascular inflammation; repeat the test

If your CRP is 3.2 mg/L, you're just barely in the **high-risk category** for cardiovascular disease based on inflammation alone.

## What Elevated CRP Actually Means

CRP is an acute-phase reactant, meaning it rises in response to inflammation or tissue damage. It's produced primarily by the liver in response to signals from immune cells.<Cite id="pepys-2003-crp-update" />

Importantly: **CRP doesn't cause heart disease. It reflects inflammation.**<Cite id="wensley-2011-crp-mendelian-randomisation" />

The inflammation that damages arteries comes from multiple sources:

**Atherosclerotic plaque instability** — Your artery walls contain cholesterol plaques. Inflammation destabilizes these plaques, increasing the risk of rupture and clot formation, which causes heart attacks.<Cite id="libby-2002-inflammation-atherosclerosis" />

**Endothelial dysfunction** — The delicate lining of your blood vessels (the endothelium) becomes dysfunctional, making arteries stiffer and more prone to clot formation.

**Immune activation** — Chronic immune activation drives persistent inflammation, partly mediated by oxidized LDL cholesterol and, possibly, bacterial lipopolysaccharides from your gut.

Elevated hs-CRP is a *marker* of this underlying inflammation. It's useful because it's easily measured and predicts cardiovascular risk independent of cholesterol.<Cite id="kaptoge-2010-crp-risk-meta-analysis" /><Cite id="ridker-2002-crp-vs-ldl" /> But it's not the root cause—it's a flag that something pro-inflammatory is happening.

## What Causes Elevated CRP?

A CRP of 3.2 mg/L can result from multiple sources. Not all are cardiovascular:<Cite id="kushner-2006-minor-crp-elevation" />

**Cardiovascular/atherosclerotic causes:**
- Atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease
- Arterial inflammation
- Recent heart attack or stroke
- Hypertension

**Metabolic causes:**
- Obesity (adipose tissue produces inflammatory cytokines)<Cite id="fontana-2007-visceral-fat-il6" />
- Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes<Cite id="pradhan-2001-crp-il6-diabetes" />
- Metabolic syndrome
- High triglycerides or small, dense LDL particles

**Autoimmune and inflammatory conditions:**
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Lupus
- Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis)
- Psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis
- Autoimmune thyroid disease

**Lifestyle and chronic stress:**
- Chronic psychological stress<Cite id="johnson-2013-psychosocial-stress-crp" />
- Poor sleep quality<Cite id="irwin-2016-sleep-inflammation" />
- Sedentary lifestyle<Cite id="fedewa-2017-exercise-training-crp" />
- Poor diet (especially high in processed foods and refined carbs)<Cite id="ciaffi-2025-ultra-processed-food-inflammation" />
- Smoking<Cite id="wannamethee-2005-smoking-inflammatory-markers" />
- Excessive alcohol<Cite id="imhof-2001-alcohol-inflammation-markers" />

**Infections (usually acute, but sometimes chronic):**
- Acute bacterial or viral infections (CRP rises within hours)<Cite id="pepys-2003-crp-update" />
- Chronic infections like Helicobacter pylori, Chlamydia, periodontitis<Cite id="paraskevas-2008-periodontitis-crp" />
- In the context of a normal blood draw (not during acute illness), chronic infections are more likely

**Hormonal and age-related:**
- Higher in women than in men<Cite id="khera-2005-crp-race-sex" />
- Increases with age<Cite id="franceschi-2018-inflammaging" />
- Oral hormone replacement therapy can raise CRP<Cite id="vongpatanasin-2003-oral-transdermal-estrogen-crp" />

**Medications:**
- Statins lower CRP rather than raise it (by 17% in the PRINCE trial and 37% in JUPITER)<Cite id="albert-2001-prince-pravastatin-crp" /><Cite id="ridker-2008-jupiter" />
- Oral estrogen therapy raises it<Cite id="vongpatanasin-2003-oral-transdermal-estrogen-crp" />

The practical takeaway: Elevated CRP is a red flag for *something*, but you need more information to know what.

## The JUPITER Trial and Statin Controversy

In 2008, the JUPITER trial made headlines. It studied nearly 18,000 people with normal LDL cholesterol but elevated hs-CRP (2.0 mg/L or higher). Half received rosuvastatin (a statin), half received placebo.

**Result:** The statin group had 44% fewer cardiovascular events over a median of about 2 years.<Cite id="ridker-2008-jupiter" />

This led many cardiologists to prescribe statins to people with high CRP but normal cholesterol—the idea being: if inflammation is driving your risk, lower inflammation with statins.

**However:** Subsequent research has been less conclusive. Some anti-inflammatory drug trials show benefits, others don't.<Cite id="nidorf-2020-lodoco2" /><Cite id="ridker-2019-cirt" /> The CANTOS trial (2017), which tested canakinumab (a drug that blocks interleukin-1β, which sits upstream of IL-6), showed reduced cardiovascular events but increased infection risk.<Cite id="ridker-2017-cantos" />

**Current medical consensus:** In US guidelines, elevated CRP alone (with normal lipids) is not by itself an indication for statins: an hs-CRP of 2.0 mg/L or higher is a "risk-enhancing factor" that can tip an intermediate-risk adult toward one.<Cite id="grundy-2019-aha-acc-cholesterol-guideline" /> A 2025 American College of Cardiology scientific statement goes further, calling for broad hs-CRP screening.<Cite id="mensah-2025-acc-inflammation-statement" /> Your full cardiovascular risk profile matters—age, blood pressure, smoking status, family history, diabetes status, and yes, cholesterol levels.<Cite id="arnett-2019-acc-aha-primary-prevention" />

If you have a CRP of 3.2 mg/L:
- Ask your doctor for your full cardiovascular risk score (Framingham or ASCVD risk calculator)
- Don't assume you need statins based on CRP alone
- Focus on lifestyle modifications first (see below)
- If you have other risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, family history), discuss statins or other medications

## How to Lower Elevated CRP

The good news: [CRP is modifiable](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation).

**Diet:**
- Emphasize anti-inflammatory foods: fatty fish (omega-3 rich), olive oil, vegetables, berries, nuts, legumes
- Minimize pro-inflammatory foods: refined carbohydrates, ultra-processed foods, fried foods, excess sugar
- Mediterranean diet is well-studied and lowers CRP<Cite id="schwingshackl-2014-mediterranean-diet-inflammation" />

**Exercise:**
- Aerobic exercise (150 min/week moderate intensity or 75 min/week vigorous)<Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity-guidelines" /> reliably lowers CRP, though the average effect is modest<Cite id="fedewa-2017-exercise-training-crp" />
- Both aerobic and resistance training lower CRP<Cite id="kim-2022-resistance-training-crp" />
- Exercise effects are apparent even without weight loss<Cite id="fedewa-2017-exercise-training-crp" />

**Weight loss:**
- If obese, even 5–10% weight loss reduces CRP
- Adipose tissue is metabolically active and inflammatory<Cite id="fontana-2007-visceral-fat-il6" />
- In the Look AHEAD trial, a year of intensive lifestyle change with 8.8% weight loss cut median hs-CRP by 43.6%, versus 16.7% with usual care<Cite id="belalcazar-2010-look-ahead-crp" />

**Sleep:**
- Poor-quality, disturbed sleep is linked to higher CRP<Cite id="irwin-2016-sleep-inflammation" />
- Target 7–9 hours/night<Cite id="cdc-about-sleep" />
- Sleep apnea, which is linked to higher CRP, should be screened for and treated<Cite id="van-der-touw-2019-sleep-apnea-crp" />

**Stress management:**
- Chronic stress elevates CRP<Cite id="johnson-2013-psychosocial-stress-crp" />
- Mind-body practices such as meditation and yoga modestly reduce CRP in trials<Cite id="morgan-2014-mind-body-therapies-immune" />
- Aim for some form of daily stress reduction

**Smoking cessation:**
- Smoking is a major driver of CRP<Cite id="wannamethee-2005-smoking-inflammatory-markers" />
- Quitting lowers CRP, but gradually: the excess fades over years, not weeks<Cite id="bakhru-2005-smoking-cessation-nhanes" />

**Oral health:**
- Chronic periodontitis (gum disease) raises CRP
- Regular dental cleanings and flossing help; treating periodontitis lowers CRP modestly<Cite id="paraskevas-2008-periodontitis-crp" />

**Managing blood sugar:**
- Diabetes and insulin resistance raise CRP<Cite id="pradhan-2001-crp-il6-diabetes" />
- Improving glucose control (through diet and exercise) lowers CRP<Cite id="belalcazar-2010-look-ahead-crp" />

**[Omega-3 supplementation](/blog/omega-3-index-test):**
- Fish oil (EPA/DHA) 1–3 grams/day can modestly reduce CRP<Cite id="kavyani-2022-omega3-inflammation-umbrella" />
- In the few trials of dietary fish rather than supplements, CRP did not fall significantly<Cite id="li-2014-marine-omega3-crp" />
- Effect size is small to moderate

**Curcumin (from turmeric):**
- A meta-analysis of six small trials found lower CRP with curcumin supplements, mainly bioavailability-enhanced forms taken for 4+ weeks<Cite id="sahebkar-2014-curcuminoids-crp" />
- Evidence comes from small trials; you can't out-supplement a bad lifestyle

**Probiotics and gut health:**
- Emerging research suggests that gut dysbiosis drives chronic inflammation<Cite id="satokari-2020-sugar-gut-bacteria" />
- A healthy gut microbiome may lower CRP
- Evidence is still preliminary; focus on fiber, fermented foods, and whole grains

## Retesting CRP: When and How Often?

If your CRP is elevated:
- Retest in 3–6 months after lifestyle interventions
- CRP is designed to show change; it shouldn't be static
- If you've improved diet, exercise, sleep, and weight, expect CRP to drop
- If CRP remains high despite good lifestyle habits, investigate other causes (autoimmune disease, occult infection, sleep apnea)

## Don't Test CRP Alone

For a fuller picture of inflammation, test these alongside hs-CRP:

- **[Homocysteine](/blog/high-homocysteine-heart-risk)**
- **Fibrinogen** (typical lab range 200–400 mg/dL)<Cite id="medlineplus-fibrinogen" />
- **Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio** (calculated from a CBC; roughly 0.8–3.5 in healthy adults)<Cite id="forget-2017-nlr-normal-values" />
- **Ferritin** above 300 (men) or 200 (women) can also reflect inflammation, since ferritin is an acute-phase reactant<Cite id="sandnes-2021-hyperferritinemia" />
- **HbA1c and fasting insulin**, because inflammation and metabolic dysfunction reinforce each other<Cite id="pradhan-2001-crp-il6-diabetes" />

## Important Caveats

**CRP is a marker, not a diagnosis.** Elevated CRP doesn't tell you that you have heart disease—it tells you that you have inflammation that may increase your risk of heart disease.

**Age and sex matter.** Women naturally have higher CRP than men.<Cite id="khera-2005-crp-race-sex" /> CRP rises with age. These are normal patterns, not necessarily abnormal.

**Acute illness skews results.** If you just recovered from a cold, infection, or had a vaccine recently, CRP may be transiently elevated.<Cite id="tsai-2005-influenza-vaccine-crp" /> Retest at least 2 weeks after acute illness has resolved.<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

**CRP alone is insufficient for risk assessment.** A cardiologist would also look at blood pressure, lipid panel, glucose, smoking status, family history, and possibly [coronary calcium scoring](/blog/cac-score-coronary-calcium-scan) or other imaging before making treatment decisions.<Cite id="arnett-2019-acc-aha-primary-prevention" />

## The Bottom Line

A CRP of 3.2 mg/L puts you in the high-risk category for inflammation-related cardiovascular disease, but it's not a diagnosis and doesn't automatically mean you need medication. It's a signal that your lifestyle or underlying health status is driving chronic inflammation.

Start with lifestyle modifications—Mediterranean diet, regular exercise, weight management, stress reduction, and sleep optimization can all help lower CRP. If CRP remains elevated after 3–6 months of serious lifestyle work, or if you have other cardiovascular risk factors, discuss further evaluation and treatment options with your doctor.

Inflammation is modifiable. The fact that your CRP is elevated isn't destiny—it's information.
