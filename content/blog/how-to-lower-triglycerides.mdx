Triglycerides are the most diet-responsive marker on a standard lipid panel. If yours are elevated, the good news is that you probably do not need medication — intensive dietary and lifestyle changes alone can cut triglycerides by 50% or more.<Cite id="miller-2011-aha-triglycerides-statement" /> For a specific value in that range, [what a triglyceride level of 180 means](/blog/triglycerides-180-high) breaks it down.

The bad news is that most people do not know which changes actually matter, and the generic advice ("eat less fat") is wrong: replacing fat with carbohydrates raises fasting triglycerides.<Cite id="mensink-2003-fatty-acids-tc-hdl-meta" />

## What triglycerides actually are

Triglycerides are fat molecules circulating in your blood, packaged inside lipoprotein particles (primarily VLDL). After you eat, your liver converts excess calories — especially from sugar and refined carbohydrates — into triglycerides and ships them out via VLDL particles for storage or energy use.<Cite id="ginsberg-2021-eas-trl-consensus" /><Cite id="stanhope-2009-fructose-beverages" />

Fasting triglycerides largely reflect how much triglyceride your liver is producing and how efficiently it is cleared. When this number is high, it usually means your liver is being overloaded with substrate — typically from excess carbohydrates, alcohol, or total caloric surplus.<Cite id="ginsberg-2021-eas-trl-consensus" /><Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" />

## Why high triglycerides matter

Elevated triglycerides are not benign. They signal several downstream risks:

**Cardiovascular disease** — high triglycerides drive production of [small, dense LDL particles](/blog/apob-heart-disease-risk) (a particularly atherogenic kind) and increase triglyceride-rich remnant lipoproteins that directly damage arterial walls.<Cite id="berneis-2002-ldl-heterogeneity" /><Cite id="ginsberg-2021-eas-trl-consensus" />

**Insulin resistance** — elevated triglycerides are one of the earliest metabolic markers of developing [insulin resistance](/blog/homa-ir-insulin-resistance), and people who go on to develop type 2 diabetes often have less favorable levels many years before diagnosis.<Cite id="mclaughlin-2003-tg-hdl-insulin-resistance" /><Cite id="hulsegge-2017-risk-factor-trajectories-diabetes" /> If you do not have a fasting insulin result, the [TyG index calculator](/tools/tyg-index-calculator) estimates insulin resistance from triglycerides and glucose alone.<Cite id="guerrero-romero-2010-tyg-clamp" />

**Metabolic syndrome** — triglycerides of 150 mg/dL or higher plus a waist circumference above 40 inches (men) or 35 inches (women) is two of the five criteria for metabolic syndrome.<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" /><Cite id="alberti-2009-harmonizing-metabolic-syndrome" />

**Pancreatitis risk** — triglycerides above 500 mg/dL carry a real risk of acute pancreatitis. Above 1000 mg/dL, this becomes an urgent medical concern.<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" /><Cite id="berglund-2012-endocrine-society-hypertriglyceridemia" />

## The triglyceride-to-HDL ratio: a hidden gem

One of the most useful lipid metrics is the **[triglyceride-to-HDL ratio](/tools/triglyceride-hdl-ratio)**. Divide your triglycerides by your HDL-C (both in mg/dL):

| TG/HDL ratio | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Below 1.0** | Excellent — likely insulin sensitive with large, buoyant LDL |
| **1.0-2.0** | Good |
| **2.0-3.0** | Borderline — early insulin resistance signal |
| **Above 3.0** | Concerning — likely insulin resistant with small, dense LDL pattern |
| **Above 5.0** | High risk for metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular events |

This ratio costs nothing extra and gives you a reasonable proxy for insulin resistance without even measuring insulin. In overweight adults, a ratio of 3.0 or more identified insulin resistance with about two-thirds sensitivity and specificity.<Cite id="mclaughlin-2003-tg-hdl-insulin-resistance" /> A ratio of 3.8 or more predicted the small, dense LDL pattern with about 80% accuracy.<Cite id="hanak-2004-tg-hdl-ldl-phenotype" />

## How to lower triglycerides: what actually works

### 1. Cut sugar and refined carbs (the biggest lever)

This is one of the most impactful changes. Your liver converts excess sugar, especially fructose, into triglycerides.<Cite id="stanhope-2009-fructose-beverages" /> Cutting added sugars and fructose (sugary beverages, fruit juice) and swapping refined starches like white bread, pasta, and processed snacks for unsaturated fats and whole foods can lower triglycerides by roughly 10-20% beyond the effect of weight loss.<Cite id="miller-2011-aha-triglycerides-statement" />

You do not need to go zero-carb. Focus on eliminating **refined** carbohydrates and keeping total carb intake moderate (100-200g/day from whole food sources like vegetables, legumes, and whole grains).

### 2. Eliminate or drastically reduce alcohol

Alcohol raises triglycerides by increasing the liver's VLDL secretion, slowing triglyceride breakdown and increasing the flow of fatty acids to the liver.<Cite id="klop-2013-alcohol-triglycerides" /> Even "moderate" drinking raises triglycerides a little on average, and obesity amplifies the effect.<Cite id="rimm-1999-alcohol-lipids-meta" /><Cite id="klop-2013-alcohol-triglycerides" /> If your triglycerides are above 150 and you drink regularly, a 30-day alcohol elimination is the most informative experiment you can run.

### 3. Add omega-3 fatty acids

[EPA and DHA](/blog/omega-3-index-test) (the omega-3s from fatty fish and fish oil) lower triglycerides mainly by reducing the liver's VLDL production (by about 25-30% at 3-4 grams a day), with variable effects on clearance.<Cite id="ginsberg-2021-eas-trl-consensus" />

Effective doses for triglyceride lowering are **2-4 grams of combined EPA/DHA per day**.<Cite id="skulas-ray-2019-aha-omega3-triglycerides" /> That is much higher than most over-the-counter fish oil supplements provide: a typical 1,000 mg capsule contains about 180 mg EPA and 120 mg DHA.<Cite id="ods-omega3-fact-sheet" /> Check the label for EPA + DHA content, not total fish oil. Prescription omega-3s (Vascepa, Lovaza) deliver pharmaceutical-grade doses.<Cite id="skulas-ray-2019-aha-omega3-triglycerides" />

### 4. Exercise regularly

Aerobic exercise — running, cycling, swimming, brisk walking — lowers triglycerides partly by increasing lipoprotein lipase activity, which clears triglyceride-rich particles from your bloodstream.<Cite id="gill-2003-exercise-postprandial-lipids" /> Even a single session blunts the triglyceride rise after your next meal, but the effect is short-lived, so exercise needs to be frequent.<Cite id="pearson-2022-exercise-postprandial-lipemia-meta" />

Aim for 150+ minutes per week.<Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity-guidelines" /> Consistency matters more than intensity.

### 5. Lose excess body fat

Excess, insulin-resistant body fat releases fatty acids that push the liver to overproduce triglyceride-rich VLDL.<Cite id="ginsberg-2021-eas-trl-consensus" /> Losing even 5-10% of body weight can lower triglycerides by about 20%.<Cite id="miller-2011-aha-triglycerides-statement" />

### 6. Improve sleep

Poor sleep (under 6 hours or fragmented) raises evening cortisol and worsens glucose tolerance in short-term studies.<Cite id="spiegel-1999-sleep-debt" /> Its direct effect on triglycerides is less clear: long-term studies have not found a consistent link between short sleep and abnormal blood lipids.<Cite id="kruisbrink-2017-sleep-lipids-meta" />

### 7. Consider medication if lifestyle is not enough

For triglycerides persistently above 200 mg/dL despite lifestyle optimization:

- **Fibrates** (fenofibrate) — lower triglycerides 20-50%<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" />
- **Prescription omega-3** (icosapent ethyl / Vascepa) — the REDUCE-IT trial showed 25% cardiovascular risk reduction in statin-treated patients with elevated triglycerides<Cite id="bhatt-2019-reduce-it" />
- **Statins** — modestly lower triglycerides (about 7-30%, depending on dose) in addition to [their LDL-lowering effect](/blog/statins-side-effects-when-to-stop)<Cite id="ncep-atp3-2001-executive-summary" />
- **Niacin** — effective but fallen out of favor due to side effects and lack of cardiovascular outcome benefit in trials<Cite id="hps2-thrive-2014-niacin" />
