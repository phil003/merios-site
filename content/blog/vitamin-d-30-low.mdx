faq:
  - q: "Is vitamin D 30 ng/mL good?"
    a: "It's the lower edge of 'sufficient' by the Endocrine Society's 2011 clinical definition (>=30 ng/mL), a definition the Society withdrew in 2024; the IOM considers 20 ng/mL and above adequate. Many longevity-focused clinicians target 40–60 ng/mL, but trials haven't shown a benefit from going higher. At 30, you're not deficient."
  - q: "How much vitamin D do I need to go from 30 to 50 ng/mL?"
    a: "More than most people expect. In a large dataset of supplement users, an average level of about 50 ng/mL took roughly 5,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 at normal weight, about 6,700 IU with overweight and about 11,000 IU with obesity — all above the 4,000 IU upper limit, so a target that high needs medical supervision. The IOM also links levels above 50 ng/mL to potential adverse effects. Take vitamin D with a fat-containing meal and recheck levels at 12 weeks."
  - q: "Should I take vitamin K2 with vitamin D3?"
    a: "It's optional. Vitamin D3 increases calcium absorption from the gut, and K2 (such as MK-7) activates proteins involved in keeping calcium out of arteries. But the idea that the combination is safer than D3 alone hasn't been shown in trials: a two-year trial of K2 plus vitamin D did not slow arterial or valve calcification, and in the large VITAL trial vitamin D alone (2,000 IU a day) caused no excess hypercalcemia or other adverse events. If you take warfarin, don't add K2 without your doctor."
  - q: "Is 75 nmol/L the same as 30 ng/mL?"
    a: "Yes. Labs outside the US report 25-OH vitamin D in nmol/L; dividing by 2.496 gives ng/mL. So 75 nmol/L is about 30 ng/mL, and 50 nmol/L is about 20 ng/mL, the level the IOM considers adequate."
  - q: "Do I need a prescription for vitamin D supplements?"
    a: "No. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is available over the counter. Doses up to 4,000 IU daily stay within the upper limit, but the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests healthy adults under 75 don't need more than the RDA (600–800 IU); discuss your target with your doctor based on your results."

## Is Vitamin D 30 ng/mL Bad?

**Not deficient — and whether it's below optimal is debated.** 30 ng/mL is the lower threshold of "sufficient" per the Endocrine Society's 2011 guideline, a definition the Society withdrew in 2024.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" /><Cite id="mccartney-2024-epistemic-humility" /> Below 20 = deficient. 20–30 = insufficient. 30+ = sufficient (2011 clinical definition). 40–60 = optimal (longevity clinics). At 30, you're in the lowest band of the old "sufficient" range and above the IOM's 20 ng/mL bar for bone health; trials have not shown that going higher improves immune function, mood, or cardiovascular outcomes.<Cite id="ross-2011-iom-dri" /><Cite id="manson-2019-vital" /><Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" />

## Where Does 30 ng/mL Fall?

| Category | 25-OH Vitamin D (ng/mL) |
|----------|-------------------------|
| Deficient | &lt;20 |
| Insufficient | 20–29 |
| **Your value (30 ng/mL)** | **Low end of sufficient** |
| Sufficient | 30–39 |
| Optimal (longevity-focused) | 40–60 |
| High | 60–80 |
| Toxicity risk | &gt;150 |

These bands mix the Endocrine Society's 2011 definitions with a longevity-clinic target. The IOM considers 20 ng/mL and above adequate and links levels above 50 ng/mL to potential adverse effects; toxicity typically occurs above 150 ng/mL.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />

The "sufficient" cutoff was set around bone health, largely where parathyroid hormone levels plateau.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" /> It was never set to optimize for cardiovascular, immune, or cognitive outcomes. Some longevity clinicians believe **40–60 ng/mL** is where most non-skeletal benefits plateau, but trials and large cohorts don't show it: VITAL raised levels from about 30 to about 42 ng/mL without cutting cancer or cardiovascular events, and in a cohort of 247,574 people, mortality was lowest at 20–24 ng/mL.<Cite id="manson-2019-vital" /><Cite id="durup-2012-mortality" />

If your report reads 75 nmol/L rather than 30 ng/mL, that is the same result: outside the US, 25-OH vitamin D is reported in nmol/L, and dividing by 2.496 gives ng/mL.<Cite id="ginde-2009-urti" /> The [vitamin D converter](/tools/vitamin-d-unit-converter) does the conversion both ways, and the [nmol/L to mg/dL conversion table](/tools/lpa-unit-converter) covers the other markers labs report in two different unit systems.

## Why Some Clinicians Say 30 Isn't Enough

The usual arguments for going higher, and what the evidence shows:

- **Upper respiratory infections:** vitamin D modulates the immune response, but in US survey data, people at 30 ng/mL or above reported the fewest recent infections, and in trials supplementation cut infection risk only slightly, regardless of starting level.<Cite id="ginde-2009-urti" /><Cite id="jolliffe-2021-respiratory-infections" />
- **All-cause mortality:** in a Copenhagen cohort, mortality was lowest at 20–24 ng/mL and rose again at higher levels.<Cite id="durup-2012-mortality" />
- **Exercise performance:** vitamin D supports muscle function, but supplementation improves strength only slightly, mostly in people who are very deficient or over 65.<Cite id="beaudart-2014-muscle" />
- **Mood:** vitamin D receptors are found throughout the brain, but supplementation did not prevent depression or improve mood scores in VITAL.<Cite id="eyles-2005-vdr-brain" /><Cite id="okereke-2020-vital-dep" />
- **Fractures:** in VITAL and in trial meta-analyses, supplementation did not reduce fractures in people who weren't selected for deficiency.<Cite id="leboff-2022-vital-fractures" /><Cite id="bolland-2018-musculoskeletal" />

Low vitamin D and low B12 share several risk factors — if fatigue persists, check whether you have [vitamin D and B12 deficiency together](/blog/vitamin-d-b12-deficiency-together).<Cite id="ods-vitamin-b12" />

## Why You Might Be Sitting at 30

The usual reasons a level settles around 30:

- **Limited sun exposure:** northern latitudes, indoor work, or (sensibly) avoiding the sun for skin cancer prevention.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- **Darker skin:** melanin reduces vitamin D synthesis, so the same sun exposure produces less (about six times the UV dose in one small study).<Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" />
- **Malabsorption:** celiac disease, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis reduce vitamin D absorption.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" />
- **Older age:** skin becomes less efficient at making vitamin D with age.<Cite id="maclaughlin-1985-aging-skin" />
- **Diet:** fatty fish, egg yolks and fortified dairy are the main food sources, and vegan diets often fall short without supplementation.<Cite id="crowe-2011-epic-oxford" />

## How to Go from 30 to 50 ng/mL

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests that healthy adults under 75 don't need more than the RDA (600–800 IU) to prevent disease, so going higher is a choice to make with your clinician.<Cite id="demay-2024-endocrine-society" /> The protocol usually used:

1. **Vitamin D3 2,000–4,000 IU/day** with a fat-containing meal (vitamin D is fat-soluble; in one trial, peak absorption was 32% higher with a fat-containing meal than with a fat-free one)<Cite id="dawson-hughes-2015-dietary-fat" />
2. **Add vitamin K2 MK-7 100–200 mcg/day** — activates proteins involved in calcium handling, though a trial of K2 plus vitamin D did not slow arterial or valve calcification<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" /><Cite id="diederichsen-2022-avadec" />
3. **Recheck in 12 weeks** — adjust dose up or down based on actual blood level
4. **Maintain** with the lowest dose that keeps you in your target range

Expect a slower climb than the headline target implies: in a large dataset of supplement users, 4,000 IU/day averaged about 47 ng/mL at normal weight, and an average of 50 ng/mL took about 5,000 IU/day — above the 4,000 IU upper limit.<Cite id="ekwaru-2014-body-weight" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> People with obesity typically need two to three times the dose for the same level.<Cite id="ekwaru-2014-body-weight" /> People with darker skin make less vitamin D from sunlight, so they may start lower.<Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" /> People with malabsorption (Crohn's, celiac, gastric bypass) may need 6,000–10,000 IU/day under medical supervision.<Cite id="holick-2011-endocrine-society" />

## Sun Exposure — Helpful but Not Sufficient for Most

Skin synthesizes vitamin D from UVB exposure. About 5–30 minutes of midday sun on the face, arms, hands and legs at least twice a week usually leads to sufficient vitamin D synthesis.<Cite id="ods-vitamin-d" /> But three caveats:

- At about 42°N (Boston), winter sun makes essentially no vitamin D from November through February; at 52°N, from October through March<Cite id="webb-1988-latitude" />
- Skin pigmentation reduces synthesis (very dark skin needed about six times the UV dose in one small study)<Cite id="clemens-1982-skin-pigment" />
- Sunscreen blocks vitamin D synthesis in lab tests, though in real-life use it has little effect on blood levels<Cite id="neale-2019-sunscreen" />

For most people in the US, supplementation is more reliable than relying on sun.

## Don't Just Take D3 — Test the Cofactors

Vitamin D works as part of a system. If you're supplementing, also check:
- **[Magnesium status](/blog/magnesium-deficiency-symptoms-women)** (serum or RBC magnesium, though no single test is considered reliable) — required to activate vitamin D<Cite id="ods-magnesium" /><Cite id="uwitonze-2018-magnesium-vitamin-d" />
- **Vitamin K2** (no routine blood test for vitamin K status; supplement MK-7 100–200 mcg/day)<Cite id="ods-vitamin-k" />
- **Calcium intake** — adequate but not excessive (1,000–1,200 mg/day from food preferred)<Cite id="iom-2011-dri-tables" />
