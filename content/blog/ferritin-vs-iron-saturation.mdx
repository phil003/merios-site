## Ferritin vs iron saturation: the short answer

**They are not the same test.** Ferritin measures the iron your body has put into storage. Transferrin saturation, usually written TSAT or "iron saturation", measures the share of your iron-carrying capacity that is loaded right now, meaning iron actually in transit. The two often move together, and when they do not, the disagreement is where the useful information sits.

| | Ferritin | Iron saturation (TSAT) |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Iron in storage | Iron in transit on transferrin |
| Unit | micrograms per liter | percent |
| Usual lab range | roughly 13 to 150 (women) and 30 to 400 (men), varying by lab | roughly 15 to 45% (women) and 20 to 50% (men) |
| Deficiency signal | under 30 in clinical use (WHO's stricter cut-off: under 15) | under 20% |
| Overload signal | persistently high, read together with TSAT | over 45 to 50% |
| Known weakness | rises with inflammation, so it can mask deficiency | calculated from serum iron, so it moves with time of day and meals |

Ranges are from MedlinePlus; the cut-offs come from WHO, UK gastroenterology guidelines and the European haemochromatosis guideline.<Cite id="medlineplus-ferritin" /><Cite id="medlineplus-tibc" /><Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /><Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" />

The combination worth knowing before anything else: **a normal ferritin alongside a TSAT under 20%**. That is not a clean bill of health. It is the pattern associated with functional iron deficiency, where iron is present but held in storage rather than released,<Cite id="tawfik-2024-us-iron-deficiency" /><Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" /> and it is easily read as "your iron is fine". Lab ranges are drawn from reference populations that often include people with iron deficiency, not from what a well-functioning iron system looks like, which is why a result sitting inside the range can still be worth a second look.<Cite id="truong-2024-ferritin-reference" />

## Why an iron panel reports both

If you have ever looked at an iron panel, you have probably noticed it is not one number but a cluster of them: serum iron, ferritin, transferrin, total iron-binding capacity, and iron saturation. Two of these, ferritin and iron saturation, get the most attention, and they are frequently confused for each other. They both have the word iron attached, they often move in the same direction, and a single one is sometimes reported on its own. But they answer genuinely different questions about your body, and knowing which is which is the key to reading an iron panel correctly.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" />

The cleanest way to hold the difference in your head is a supply-chain image. Ferritin is your warehouse inventory: how much iron your body has stored for later. Iron saturation, usually reported as TSAT, is the delivery fleet: how much iron is loaded up and moving through your bloodstream right now. A business can have a full warehouse and empty trucks, or a bare warehouse and busy trucks, and each situation means something different. The same is true of your iron.

## What ferritin measures: your iron warehouse

Ferritin is a protein that stores iron inside your cells, mostly in the liver, the bone marrow, and the macrophages that recycle old red blood cells.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /><Cite id="ganz-2013-iron-homeostasis" /> A small amount circulates in your blood, and that circulating amount tends to track with your total body iron stores.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /> When your reserves are full, blood ferritin is higher. When you have been slowly losing iron, ferritin falls, often before you develop any anemia at all.<Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" /> That early-warning quality is why ferritin is considered the single most useful blood marker of iron stores.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />

The reference ranges vary by lab, but the World Health Organization set clear thresholds for deficiency. In apparently healthy adults, a ferritin concentration below 15 micrograms per liter is used to indicate iron deficiency.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /> Many clinicians use a higher cut-off of 30, which catches far more cases — in one study it identified 92% of iron-deficient patients with 98% specificity — and the American Gastroenterological Association uses 45 when anemia is present.<Cite id="mast-1998-ferritin-cutoff" /><Cite id="aga-2020-ida" /> A very low ferritin, in the mid-teens or below, signals that the warehouse is close to empty.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /> Our detailed reads on a [ferritin of 30](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) and a [ferritin level of 15](/blog/ferritin-level-15) walk through what those specific numbers tend to mean in practice.

Ferritin has one crucial quirk that we will come back to: it is an acute-phase reactant. That means it rises in response to inflammation, infection, and liver stress, independent of how much iron you actually have.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" /> This is a major reason iron panels get misread.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" />

## What iron saturation measures: iron in transit

Iron does not float freely in your blood. It rides on a carrier protein called transferrin, which acts like a fleet of trucks shuttling iron from storage and absorption sites to the bone marrow, where new red blood cells are built.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /><Cite id="ganz-2013-iron-homeostasis" /> Iron saturation, or transferrin saturation, is simply the percentage of that carrying capacity that is currently occupied by iron.

It is a calculated value, not a directly measured one. The formula is serum iron divided by total iron-binding capacity, multiplied by 100.<Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" /> If your serum iron is 85 and your total iron-binding capacity is 300, your saturation is about 28 percent, comfortably in the normal zone. Because it reflects iron that is actively available for red cell production, TSAT captures a more real-time picture than ferritin does.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /> It answers the question, is iron actually reaching the places that need it right now.

Labs commonly report a normal transferrin saturation of roughly 20 to 50 percent for men and 15 to 45 percent for women.<Cite id="medlineplus-tibc" /> A saturation below 20 percent points toward low iron availability.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" /> On the other end, a saturation above 45 percent in women or 50 percent in men is defined as elevated and raises the possibility of iron overload.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" />

## The reference ranges, side by side

Putting the two together makes their roles easier to keep straight.

Ferritin is measured in micrograms per liter and reflects stored iron. In healthy adults, below 30 suggests deficiency (WHO's stricter line is 15), lab normal ranges run from the low teens in women or about 30 in men up into the low hundreds, and persistently high values point toward inflammation, liver disease, or overload.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" /><Cite id="medlineplus-ferritin" /> Because ferritin climbs with inflammation, a normal-looking number does not automatically rule out deficiency.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />

Iron saturation is a percentage and reflects iron in transit. Below 20 percent suggests low availability, about 15 to 50 percent is the usual normal band depending on sex and lab, and above 45 to 50 percent raises the question of overload.<Cite id="medlineplus-tibc" /><Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" /> Because it is calculated from serum iron, which fluctuates through the day and even from meal to meal, a single saturation value is best interpreted alongside ferritin rather than in isolation.<Cite id="dale-2002-diurnal-iron" /><Cite id="aasm-2025-rls" />

The practical takeaway is that neither number is the whole story. Ferritin tells you about reserves and iron saturation tells you about supply, and it is the combination that carries the meaning.

## Why the two can disagree, and what each pattern means

The interesting clinical information often lives in the disagreements between these two markers. Four broad patterns are worth recognizing.

Low ferritin with low iron saturation is the classic picture of absolute iron deficiency. The warehouse is empty and the trucks are running light, so there is simply not enough iron in the system. This is the pattern behind most straightforward iron-deficiency anemia, and it is what you would expect from chronic blood loss, heavy menstruation, or poor absorption.<Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" />

Normal or high ferritin with low iron saturation is the pattern people miss, and it is covered in its own section below because it is so important. In brief, it suggests iron is present but locked away and unavailable.

High ferritin with high iron saturation is the signature of iron overload, including hereditary hemochromatosis. Here both the warehouse and the trucks are overloaded, and the concern flips from too little iron to too much, which can accumulate in organs over time.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" />

High ferritin with normal or low iron saturation usually reflects inflammation, liver disease, or a metabolic driver rather than true overload.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" /><Cite id="acg-2019-hemochromatosis" /> The ferritin is usually elevated as an inflammatory or metabolic response, not because iron stores are genuinely excessive. This is exactly why a high ferritin should never be read as overload on its own.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" />

## What 15%, 28% or 45% saturation actually means

Most people arrive at this page holding one number. Here is how to place it.

- **Below 20%** — the range that suggests iron is genuinely short in transit. Paired with low ferritin, this is straightforward iron deficiency. Paired with normal or high ferritin, it points instead towards inflammation locking iron away in storage.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" />
- **20% to 45%** — the usual reference band. A saturation of 28% sits comfortably inside it, and on its own is not a reason for concern.<Cite id="medlineplus-tibc" /> If you feel unwell with a value here, the answer is more likely in ferritin, CRP or something outside the iron panel entirely.
- **Above 45%** — the level commonly used as a screening trigger for iron overload, including hereditary haemochromatosis (the European guideline uses 45% for women and 50% for men). A single raised value is not a diagnosis; it is a reason to repeat the test in the morning and to look at ferritin alongside it, because saturation alone runs high in some people without any overload.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" />

Two caveats worth knowing. Transferrin saturation **varies a lot from sample to sample and rises after an iron-containing meal or supplement**, so a morning draw with no iron supplements or iron-rich food in the previous 24 hours is the comparable one; fasting by itself doesn't improve its accuracy for overload screening.<Cite id="easl-2022-haemochromatosis" /><Cite id="aasm-2025-rls" /> And because it is a ratio of iron to binding capacity, anything that moves transferrin — pregnancy, oral contraceptives, liver disease, malnutrition — moves the percentage without iron itself having changed.<Cite id="medlineplus-tibc" />

## The inflammation problem: when ferritin lies

Because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, inflammation can push it up regardless of your real iron status. This creates one of the most clinically significant scenarios in the whole iron panel: functional iron deficiency.

In chronic inflammatory conditions, infection, kidney disease, obesity, and many other states, the body raises a hormone called hepcidin. Hepcidin effectively traps iron inside storage cells and blocks it from being released into the blood.<Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" /> The result is a normal or even elevated ferritin, because the warehouse is stocked, paired with a low iron saturation, because almost nothing is being loaded onto the trucks. The person can be functionally short of usable iron while their ferritin looks reassuring.<Cite id="tawfik-2024-us-iron-deficiency" /> If you have wondered why someone can feel deficient with a normal ferritin, this is often the answer, and it is a close cousin of the situation we describe in [low ferritin with normal hemoglobin](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin).

This is also why iron saturation earns its place on the panel. When inflammation blunts the usefulness of ferritin, saturation becomes the more honest indicator of whether iron is actually available.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /> A low TSAT despite a normal ferritin is a strong clue that iron is present but not usable, and it changes how deficiency gets managed.<Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" /> Checking an inflammatory marker at the same time, such as the ones covered in our piece on [inflammation markers](/blog/understanding-crp-inflammation), helps confirm whether inflammation is the reason the two iron values disagree.<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" />

## What to do: reading them together

The single most useful habit is to stop reading either number in isolation. When you get an iron panel, look at ferritin and iron saturation as a pair and ask what story they tell together. Are stores full or empty, and is iron actually moving.

If both are low, the picture is usually straightforward deficiency. If ferritin is normal or high but saturation is low, think about inflammation and functional deficiency rather than assuming your iron is fine, and consider whether an inflammatory marker should be checked alongside. If both are high, that is the pattern that deserves a conversation about overload rather than more iron. And if you have started supplementing but your numbers are not budging the way you expected, the mismatch between stores and availability is often the reason, which we explore in [why iron supplements are not working](/blog/iron-supplements-not-working).

None of this is a substitute for a clinician who can see your full history, symptoms, and the rest of your labs. Iron is a marker where context genuinely changes the interpretation, and the safest approach is to bring an unusual pattern to a professional rather than self-treat. What you can do is arrive at that conversation already understanding what each number represents, so you can ask better questions.
