There is a number that strongly predicts your metabolic future.<Cite id="song-2007-homa-diabetes-risk" /> It takes two data points from a fasting blood test, a simple equation, and 10 seconds of math. Yet almost no doctor calculates it.

It is called **HOMA-IR** — Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance — and it may be the most useful metabolic metric you have never heard of.

## What HOMA-IR tells you

Your body runs on glucose. To get glucose from the blood into cells, it needs insulin. When cells respond well to insulin, you need only a small amount to clear glucose efficiently. When cells become resistant — due to excess visceral fat, inactivity, poor sleep, chronic stress, or genetic predisposition — the pancreas has to produce **more insulin** to get the same job done.<Cite id="defronzo-2009-banting" />

HOMA-IR quantifies this relationship.<Cite id="matthews-1985-homa" /> It asks: **given your fasting glucose level, how much insulin is your body producing to maintain it?**

A low score means your body is efficient — low insulin, low glucose. A high score means your pancreas is working hard, even if your glucose appears normal on paper.

## The HOMA-IR formula

The calculation is straightforward:

**HOMA-IR = (Fasting Glucose in mg/dL × Fasting Insulin in µIU/mL) ÷ 405**<Cite id="wallace-2004-homa" />

You need both numbers from a fasting blood draw (at least 8 hours without food). Here are some real examples:

| Fasting glucose | Fasting insulin | HOMA-IR | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 85 mg/dL | 4 µIU/mL | **0.84** | Excellent sensitivity |
| 90 mg/dL | 6 µIU/mL | **1.33** | Optimal |
| 95 mg/dL | 10 µIU/mL | **2.35** | Borderline resistant |
| 100 mg/dL | 15 µIU/mL | **3.70** | Insulin resistant |
| 110 mg/dL | 22 µIU/mL | **5.98** | Significant resistance |

Notice something critical in this table: the first two people have fasting glucose values that any doctor would call normal (85 and 90 mg/dL). The difference between them and the third person only becomes visible when you add insulin to the picture.

## HOMA-IR reference ranges

| HOMA-IR score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Below 1.0** | Excellent insulin sensitivity |
| **1.0–1.5** | Optimal — target range for longevity |
| **1.5–2.0** | Normal — acceptable but worth tracking |
| **2.0–2.5** | Borderline insulin resistance |
| **2.5–3.5** | Insulin resistance — action needed |
| **3.5–5.0** | Significant insulin resistance |
| **Above 5.0** | Severe insulin resistance / metabolic syndrome |

These bands are a rough guide, not a clinical standard: HOMA-IR values depend on the insulin assay, and research cut-offs for insulin resistance vary with age, sex and ethnicity — population studies have proposed values from under 2 to about 3.5.<Cite id="wallace-2004-homa" /><Cite id="tahapary-2022-homa-cutoffs" /> Some metabolic health experts argue that trouble begins below 2.5 — and one population study that anchored its cut-off to metabolic syndrome put it at about 2.0.<Cite id="gayoso-diz-2013-homa-cutoffs" />

## Why this matters: the timeline of metabolic disease

Type 2 diabetes does not appear overnight. It follows a progression that typically unfolds over many years — in the Whitehall II cohort, insulin sensitivity was already falling steeply 5 years before diagnosis:<Cite id="tabak-2009-whitehall-trajectories" />

**Phase 1 — Silent compensation (HOMA-IR rising, glucose normal)**
Your cells are becoming resistant. Your pancreas compensates by producing more insulin. Fasting glucose stays in the 80–95 mg/dL range. Standard blood work shows nothing wrong. But HOMA-IR is climbing — from 1.0 to 1.5 to 2.5.

**Phase 2 — Impaired fasting glucose (glucose 100–125 mg/dL)**
The pancreas cannot fully compensate anymore. [Glucose begins to creep up](/blog/fasting-glucose-100-borderline). This is "pre-diabetes." By this point, you have likely been insulin resistant for years.

**Phase 3 — Type 2 diabetes (glucose 126 mg/dL or higher)**
Beta cell function has declined substantially — by over 80%, by one leading estimate.<Cite id="defronzo-2009-banting" /> Glucose management is failing. Medications are often needed.

HOMA-IR catches the problem in Phase 1. Fasting glucose catches it in Phase 2. [HbA1c confirms it](/tools/a1c-calculator) in Phase 2–3. The earlier you intervene, the easier it is to reverse.

## What drives HOMA-IR higher

### Visceral fat

The fat stored around your organs — liver, pancreas, intestines — is metabolically active tissue that secretes inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) which directly impair insulin signaling.<Cite id="kahn-2006-obesity-insulin-resistance" /> Visceral fat is one of the strongest predictors of insulin resistance. You can be at a "normal" BMI and still have excess visceral fat (this is sometimes called "TOFI" — thin outside, fat inside).<Cite id="neeland-2019-visceral-fat" />

### Physical inactivity

Skeletal muscle is responsible for most insulin-stimulated glucose disposal — roughly 90% in clamp studies.<Cite id="defronzo-1985-peripheral-glucose" /> When muscles are inactive, they downregulate GLUT4 transporters — the channels that allow glucose to enter the cell. Inactivity alone is enough: a week of bed rest made healthy young men insulin resistant, with less GLUT4 in their muscle.<Cite id="bienso-2012-bed-rest" />

### Poor sleep

Sleep restriction — even just a few nights of 5–6 hours — increases insulin resistance acutely: a week of 5-hour nights cut insulin sensitivity by about 20% in healthy men.<Cite id="buxton-2010-sleep-restriction" /> Another study found that restricting sleep to 4 hours for 6 nights lowered glucose tolerance in healthy young men.<Cite id="spiegel-1999-sleep-debt" /> Sleep apnea and shift work are both linked to insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes.<Cite id="punjabi-2004-sleep-apnea" /><Cite id="gan-2015-shift-work" />

### Chronic stress

Cortisol promotes gluconeogenesis (the liver releasing glucose into the blood) and directly antagonizes insulin action.<Cite id="rizza-1982-cortisol" /> Chronic psychological stress predicts new-onset type 2 diabetes, with sustained cortisol elevation one likely pathway.<Cite id="hackett-2017-stress-diabetes" /> This may be one reason HOMA-IR can be elevated even in lean, active individuals who are under constant stress.

### Dietary patterns

Diets high in refined carbohydrates, sugar-sweetened beverages, and processed foods cause repeated large insulin spikes.<Cite id="holt-1997-insulin-index" /> Over time, cells downregulate their response — high insulin is both a result and a driver of insulin resistance.<Cite id="shanik-2008-hyperinsulinemia" /> The liver becomes a particular problem: excess fructose and carbohydrate drive hepatic de novo lipogenesis (the liver converting sugar to fat), leading to fatty liver, which further worsens insulin resistance.<Cite id="stanhope-2009-fructose" />

### Genetics

Some people have a genetic predisposition to insulin resistance. A variant near IRS1 affects insulin signaling and is linked to insulin resistance and high insulin, while common TCF7L2 variants raise diabetes risk mainly by impairing insulin secretion.<Cite id="rung-2009-irs1" /><Cite id="florez-2006-tcf7l2-dpp" /> Family history of type 2 diabetes is one of the strongest risk factors<Cite id="scott-2013-interact-family-history" /> — but it is a risk factor, not a destiny. Lifestyle still modifies the expression: in the Diabetes Prevention Program, the extra risk from TCF7L2 looked smaller in the lifestyle group.<Cite id="florez-2006-tcf7l2-dpp" />

## How to improve your HOMA-IR

The good news: HOMA-IR responds well to lifestyle change. In trials, interval training lowered insulin resistance,<Cite id="jelleyman-2015-hiit" /> and viscous fiber cut HOMA-IR by about 1.9 points in people with type 2 diabetes.<Cite id="jovanovski-2019-viscous-fiber" />

### 1. Build muscle

Resistance training 3–4 times per week increases muscle mass and glucose uptake capacity.<Cite id="holten-2004-strength-training" /> Each kilogram of skeletal muscle added is another glucose sink that reduces the burden on insulin.<Cite id="srikanthan-2011-muscle-mass" /> Compound movements (squats, deadlifts, rows, presses) are the most efficient.

### 2. Zone 2 cardio

150+ minutes per week of [zone 2 exercise](/blog/zone-2-cardio-heart-rate) (60–70% max heart rate — the pace where you can still hold a conversation) improves mitochondrial density and fat oxidation.<Cite id="piercy-2018-physical-activity-guidelines" /><Cite id="toledo-2007-exercise-mitochondria" /> This is the metabolic base that makes everything else work better.

### 3. Reduce refined carbohydrates

You do not need to go zero-carb. But replacing refined grains, sugary beverages, and processed snacks with whole foods, vegetables, and complex carbohydrates makes a measurable difference.<Cite id="evert-2019-nutrition-consensus" /> Prioritize meals that combine protein, fat, and fiber — this blunts the glucose spike.<Cite id="holt-1997-insulin-index" />

### 4. Prioritize sleep

Aim for 7–8 hours of quality sleep.<Cite id="cdc-sleep" /> Address sleep apnea if present. Keep a consistent sleep schedule. Limit screen exposure before bed. Sleep is not a luxury — it is insulin's best friend.

### 5. Manage stress

Chronic stress management — whether through exercise, meditation, social connection, or simply reducing overcommitment — can lower cortisol, which directly opposes insulin.<Cite id="rizza-1982-cortisol" /> In people with diabetes, psychosocial interventions have modestly improved A1C.<Cite id="ada-2025-lifestyle" />

### 6. Lose visceral fat

If you carry excess visceral fat, even a 5–10% reduction in body weight can markedly improve insulin sensitivity in liver, muscle and fat tissue.<Cite id="magkos-2016-weight-loss" /> The combination of resistance training + caloric deficit + adequate protein (muscle gains from training plateaued at about 1.6 g per kg body weight a day in trials) is a well-supported approach.<Cite id="morton-2018-protein-resistance-training" />

## How to get your HOMA-IR tested

You need a fasting blood draw that includes both fasting glucose and [fasting insulin](/blog/fasting-insulin-levels-chart). Here is the practical path: If you do not have a fasting insulin result, the [TyG index calculator](/tools/tyg-index-calculator) estimates insulin resistance from triglycerides and glucose alone.

1. **Ask your doctor** to add fasting insulin to your next blood panel. Most will agree if you explain why.
2. **[Order it yourself](/blog/blood-test-without-doctor)** through Quest Diagnostics, LabCorp, or similar direct-to-consumer lab services in the US.
3. **Calculate it** using the [HOMA-IR calculator](/tools/homa-ir-calculator) or the formula above, or let Merios do it automatically.

Test in the morning after 10–12 hours of fasting. Avoid intense exercise the evening before, as it can acutely lower insulin.<Cite id="mikines-1988-exercise-insulin-action" /> Get tested at roughly the same time of day for comparable results.
