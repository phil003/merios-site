There are three ways to get wearable data and bloodwork into one place in 2026. Buy the blood test from your wearable's maker (WHOOP, Oura and Ultrahuman sell one, and Apple has announced one). Join a lab membership that also syncs wearables, such as Superpower. Or bring results from any lab into an app that already holds your wearable data, such as Apple Health or a lab-agnostic tracker.

What combining adds is context, not diagnosis. A wearable can show that you slept four hours, lifted heavy or were fighting a cold the week of your draw, which changes how some results read. It cannot measure your LDL, ferritin or hormones, and most links between wearable metrics and blood markers are associations seen across groups, not rules that apply to you.

One disclosure: this page is published by Merios, which makes an iPhone app that puts lab results next to Apple Health data, so it competes with several products below. Prices were checked on each vendor's own page on 23 October 2026. For a ranking of the tracking apps themselves, see [the 8 best blood test tracking apps](/blog/best-blood-test-tracking-apps); this page covers the wearable side.

## Which wearables now sell or show blood tests

### WHOOP Advanced Labs

As of 23 October 2026, WHOOP's shop lists a Basic Health Panel at $69 for 53 biomarkers, a Comprehensive Health Panel at $199 for 75, and five specialized panels (Heart, Metabolic, Performance, Women's, Men's) at $299 each, all marked HSA/FSA eligible. The Galleri multi-cancer test is listed at $649.<Cite id="whoop-panels" /> Repeat testing costs $349 for two comprehensive tests a year, $599 for four and $899 for six.<Cite id="whoop-advanced-labs" /> Blood is drawn at Quest locations and a clinician reviews the results.<Cite id="whoop-basic-panel" />

Since 18 August 2026 no WHOOP membership is needed to buy the tests,<Cite id="whoop-2026-no-membership" /> but the link to recovery and sleep data still requires the strap: trade press noted that WHOOP is not pulling in Garmin or Apple Watch data.<Cite id="gadgetsandwearables-2026-whoop" /> Members can upload past results from other labs, as a PDF or screenshot, free.<Cite id="whoop-2025-uploads" /> WHOOP's own pages disagree, though: its main Advanced Labs page still says 65 biomarkers and that a membership is required.<Cite id="whoop-advanced-labs" /> Our [WHOOP Advanced Labs vs Function Health comparison](/compare/whoop-advanced-labs-vs-function-health) works through the first-year cost, and [WHOOP Advanced Labs vs Oura Health Panels](/compare/whoop-advanced-labs-vs-oura-health-panels) puts the two wearable makers' panels side by side.

## How can wearables work with blood test results?

Wearables add context around a single draw and trends over months. Almost all of it is association, not cause and effect.

### Context around the draw

**Hard training.** In 15 healthy men not used to it, one hour of weightlifting raised AST, ALT and creatine kinase for at least seven days.<Cite id="pettersson-2008-exercise-lfts" /> A heavy session logged two days before the draw can explain a flagged liver enzyme, and is a reason to ask about a retest after a quiet week.

**Illness.** Acute infection can raise resting heart rate: data from 47,249 Fitbit users improved state-level tracking of flu-like illness,<Cite id="radin-2020-fitbit-ili" /> and in one smartwatch study, 26 of 32 people with COVID-19 showed changes in heart rate, steps or sleep.<Cite id="mishra-2020-smartwatch-covid" /> Infection is also the most common cause of a markedly raised CRP,<Cite id="landry-2017-crp-causes" /> and hs-CRP swings widely within the same person (median coefficient of variation around 44%).<Cite id="gough-2024-crp-variation" /> Because hs-CRP is read against cut-points of under 1, 1 to 3, and above 3 mg/L for cardiovascular risk,<Cite id="ridker-2016-hscrp" /> a result drawn in a week when your watch showed a raised resting heart rate is a reason to retest once well, not your baseline.

Across studies, lower HRV goes with higher inflammatory markers, with SDNN, the HRV statistic Apple Watch has long reported to Apple Health, among the most consistent measures.<Cite id="williams-2019-hrv-inflammation" /><Cite id="apple-healthkit-sdnn" /> That is a population finding; your HRV cannot stand in for a CRP test. Why devices disagree on HRV is covered in [why WHOOP and Apple Watch HRV numbers differ](/blog/heart-rate-variability-explained), and how watches fold these signals into one number in [Apple Watch recovery, HRV and readiness scores](/blog/apple-watch-recovery-hrv-readiness-score).

**Short sleep.** Six nights of four hours in bed lowered glucose tolerance and TSH and raised evening cortisol in young men,<Cite id="spiegel-1999-sleep-debt" /> and a single four-hour night cut whole-body insulin sensitivity by about a fifth on a clamp test in nine healthy adults.<Cite id="donga-2010-one-night" /> After a bad week, glucose, insulin, TSH and cortisol deserve a second look. How far to trust the tracker's sleep figure is its own question: see [Apple Watch vs Oura sleep tracking accuracy](/blog/apple-watch-sleep-vs-oura-ring).

### Slow trends side by side

**Ferritin and training.** Iron deficiency, with or without anemia, can cause fatigue and exercise intolerance, and is usually diagnosed by a ferritin under 30 ng/mL when there is no inflammation.<Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" /> It is common in athletes: about 15 to 35% of female athlete cohorts.<Cite id="sim-2019-athlete-iron" /> Endurance training normally lowers resting heart rate,<Cite id="reimers-2018-rhr-exercise" /> so a falling ferritin alongside a resting heart rate that creeps up at a steady training load is a pattern worth showing a clinician. Keep expectations modest: in trials of iron-deficient adults without anemia, iron improved self-reported fatigue but not VO2 max or other objective measures of capacity.<Cite id="houston-2018-iron-idna" />

**Thyroid and heart rate.** Hyperthyroidism commonly causes palpitations and can cause arrhythmias,<Cite id="lee-2023-hyperthyroidism" /> so a low TSH next to a resting heart rate that has risen for no training reason is a combination clinicians take seriously, though neither number diagnoses anything alone.

**Sleep, glucose and HbA1c.** In a meta-analysis of people with type 2 diabetes, short sleep was associated with an HbA1c about 0.23 percentage points higher, and poor sleep quality with about 0.35 points higher.<Cite id="lee-2017-sleep-hba1c" /> Randomized trials show sleep restriction reduces insulin sensitivity,<Cite id="sondrup-2022-sleep-insulin" /> and among 953 healthy adults wearing a CGM and an activity monitor, poor sleep efficiency and later bedtimes were associated with higher glucose after the next morning's breakfast.<Cite id="tsereteli-2022-sleep-glucose" />

A CGM is the one consumer wearable that measures a metabolic variable directly; the FDA cleared the first over-the-counter model in 2024 for adults who do not use insulin.<Cite id="fda-2024-otc-cgm" /> Its estimated A1c, now called the glucose management indicator, can disagree with your lab HbA1c,<Cite id="bergenstal-2018-gmi" /> so keep both. When a CGM is worth wearing: [CGMs for people without diabetes](/blog/cgm-without-diabetes).

### Where combining adds little: lipids

Be skeptical of any app that links your cholesterol to last night's sleep score. Normal meals lower LDL by about 8 mg/dL on average at most, and ApoB and Lp(a) are not affected by fasting status at all.<Cite id="nordestgaard-2016-nonfasting" /> In a meta-analysis of prospective studies, sleep duration was not associated with developing abnormal lipids.<Cite id="kruisbrink-2017-sleep-lipids" /> Regular aerobic exercise raises HDL, but modestly, by about 2.5 mg/dL on average.<Cite id="kodama-2007-hdl" /> The exception is triglycerides: exercise before a meal blunts the rise in triglycerides afterwards, but the effect is transient, so it only shows up if you trained recently.<Cite id="pearson-2022-postprandial-tg" />

## What to look for when choosing a wearable or app that integrates blood test data

- **Any lab, or its own lab only.** WHOOP and Oura both accept uploads of past results, which matters if you have years of bloodwork.<Cite id="whoop-2025-uploads" /><Cite id="oura-health-panels" /> If a service shows only its own panels, your timeline starts the day you join.
- **Which wearable metrics actually sync.** Oura's Apple Health page, updated 14 October 2026, lists sleep, heart rate, respiratory rate, steps and workouts among what it writes, but not HRV, resting heart rate or Readiness,<Cite id="oura-apple-health" /> so an app reading Oura data through Apple Health may not see those.
- **Unit handling.** The same marker can come in different units; the European consensus on lipid testing, for instance, gives every cut-point in both mmol/L and mg/dL.<Cite id="nordestgaard-2016-nonfasting" /> An app that mixes units without converting will draw a false trend, so check one past result by hand after importing.
- **Who reviews results.** WHOOP and Oura include clinician review; Apple's announced panel uses third-party providers.<Cite id="quest-2026-apple-labs" /> Software-only apps have no clinician.
- **Privacy and control.** On iPhone, Health data is encrypted while the phone is locked, and you choose which record categories each app may read.<Cite id="apple-download-health-records" /><Cite id="apple-view-health-records" /> Check how you get your values back out; Function, for example, offers a PDF download.<Cite id="function-faq" />
- **The price model.** Wearable-native labs stack hardware, a membership and each test; lab memberships charge one yearly fee including draws; software-only apps charge for analysis, not blood.
- **A retest rhythm you will keep.** Trend density costs money; [how often to get blood work](/blog/how-often-blood-work) sets out sensible intervals.

### Comparing wearables for tracking metabolic markers

Glucose, through a CGM, is the only metabolic marker a consumer wearable measures; HbA1c, fasting insulin, triglycerides and ApoB still need blood. Look for an app that puts CGM data and lab HbA1c on one timeline and labels the estimated A1c as an estimate.

### Comparing wearables for tracking hormonal markers

No consumer wearable measures testosterone, estradiol, cortisol or TSH. What wearables offer is temperature-based cycle estimates: Apple Watch Series 8 and later use wrist temperature to estimate ovulation after it has happened, and Apple says this should not be used for birth control or to diagnose a condition.<Cite id="apple-ovulation-estimates" /> Hormones need a blood panel; the wearable's job is context, since sleep debt alone lowered TSH and raised evening cortisol in the study above.<Cite id="spiegel-1999-sleep-debt" />

## How to keep wearable data and bloodwork in one place with Apple Health

1. **Let each wearable write to Health.** Turn on Apple Health sharing in the wearable's app, then check its export list; Oura, for one, does not list HRV or resting heart rate among what it writes.<Cite id="oura-apple-health" />
2. **Connect your lab results.** In the Health app, tap Summary, then your picture or initials, then Health Records, then Get Started (or Add Account). Find your clinic, hospital or lab, tap Connect Account and sign in with your patient-portal login. New records then download automatically.<Cite id="apple-download-health-records" />
3. **Find and pin markers.** Tap Browse, scroll to Lab Results, and pin the markers you follow so they stay at the top.<Cite id="apple-view-health-records" />
4. **Keep the PDFs anyway.** Health Records works only with organizations that support it, so results from a lab or clinic outside that list will not appear.<Cite id="apple-download-health-records" />
5. **Share with care.** Give another app only the record categories it needs, and decide whether it also gets future records.<Cite id="apple-view-health-records" />

The limit is that lab results live under Health Records, apart from your sleep and heart charts, so you do the lining-up yourself. Apple's announced redesign adds a Longevity tab that draws on clinical records and lab values such as A1c and LDL.<Cite id="apple-2026-health-app" />

## If you would rather an app did the lining-up

Merios, which publishes this page, is the lab-agnostic kind: it reads sleep, HRV and activity from Apple Health, and Merios Plus ($44 a year, with a 7-day free trial on the annual plan) scans a lab report PDF or photo from any lab so your markers share that timeline. It sells no tests, has no clinicians reviewing results, runs on iPhone only, and does not connect to WHOOP or Oura directly, only to what they write to Apple Health. It is on the [US App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/merios/id6760352598). For seven other options weighed on the same criteria, see [the full ranking of blood test tracking apps](/blog/best-blood-test-tracking-apps).
