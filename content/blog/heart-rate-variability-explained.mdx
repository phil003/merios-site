Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. Even at rest, the interval between heartbeats varies, and that variation says something useful about your nervous system and your recovery.

This variation is called **Heart Rate Variability (HRV)**. The single most common question about it is not what it means, but why two devices on the same wrist report numbers that look nothing alike.

## Recovery HRV vs Apple Watch HRV: the short answer

If your Whoop says 78 and your Apple Watch says 34, neither device is broken. They are not measuring the same thing.

| | Recovery wearables (Whoop, Oura) | Apple Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Metric usually reported | RMSSD | SDNN |
| When it is measured | One long, quiet stretch during sleep | Short opportunistic samples across the day |
| What mostly moves it | Parasympathetic (vagal) activity | Vagal activity plus slower influences |
| Typical use | A daily recovery or readiness score | A general trend line in the Health app |
| Comparable across devices? | No | No |

Both numbers can be correct at the same time. They are two different summaries of the same heartbeat signal.

## Why the two numbers are not interchangeable

Three things differ at once, and they compound.

- **The metric.** Recovery-focused wearables typically report **RMSSD**, which captures beat-to-beat differences and tracks parasympathetic (vagal) activity closely. Apple Watch reports **SDNN**, the standard deviation of the intervals across the whole recording, which folds in slower influences as well. RMSSD and SDNN are different statistics computed from the same raw intervals, so their absolute values are simply not interchangeable.
- **The window.** Whoop and Oura compute recovery from a long, quiet stretch during sleep, when confounders are at their lowest. Apple Watch takes short opportunistic samples across the day, while you are sitting, walking, or running a Breathe session, so its readings carry whatever you happened to be doing at the time.
- **The processing.** Each vendor applies its own artifact rejection and averaging before showing you a number. Two devices worn on the same wrist will disagree even when both are working exactly as designed.

## Which number should you actually track?

The practical rule: **compare a device only to itself.**

Your Apple Watch HRV trend over six weeks is meaningful. Your Apple Watch number against a friend's Oura number is noise. If you switch devices, treat it as starting a new baseline rather than as a change in your physiology.

A related trap is reading a single night. HRV swings substantially day to day with sleep, alcohol, illness, training load and even room temperature, so a weekly average is far more informative than any one morning's figure. ## HRV in 30 seconds

HRV measures the time differences between successive heartbeats, usually in milliseconds. A **higher** HRV is generally associated with better [cardiovascular fitness](/blog/apple-watch-vo2-max-accuracy) and a well-balanced autonomic nervous system. A **lower** HRV is often associated with stress, fatigue, or incomplete recovery.

## Why HRV adds something resting heart rate doesn't

[Resting heart rate](/blog/resting-heart-rate-suddenly-high) tells you how fast your heart beats. HRV says more about **how your autonomic nervous system is balanced**.

Two people can share a resting heart rate of 62 bpm and have very different HRV. In group data, higher HRV tends to accompany better recovery from exercise, more effective stress regulation and more restful sleep. Lower HRV has also been associated with higher cardiovascular risk in large population studies, though that is a population-level association and not a prediction about any individual. Neither metric diagnoses anything on its own, and an unexplained shift in either is worth raising with a clinician rather than self-interpreting.

## What affects your HRV?

Several factors influence your daily readings:

- **[Sleep quality](/blog/deep-sleep-how-to-increase)** — a poor night's sleep can noticeably lower HRV the following morning
- **Alcohol** — even moderate drinking tends to suppress HRV for a day or more
- **Exercise timing** — intense workouts temporarily lower HRV; the recovery pattern is the informative part
- **Stress** — both acute and chronic stress are associated with reduced variability
- **Illness** — a drop often accompanies the onset of an infection, alongside other symptoms
- **Hydration and room temperature** — both shift autonomic balance enough to move a single night's reading

## How to improve your HRV

HRV responds to habit change, though the size of the response varies a lot between people.

1. **Prioritise sleep** — consistent, sufficient sleep is the most reliable lever
2. **Breathwork** — slow, deep breathing acutely increases vagal activity
3. **Move alcohol away from bedtime** — overnight HRV is visibly sensitive to it
4. **Regular aerobic exercise** — [consistent cardio](/blog/zone-2-cardio-heart-rate) is associated with improved vagal tone over time
5. **Cold exposure** — cold showers or face immersion can acutely raise HRV, though the durable effect is less clear
