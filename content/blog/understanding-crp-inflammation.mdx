faq:
  - q: "What does 'elevated inflammatory markers' mean on a blood test?"
    a: "It means one or more acute-phase proteins came back above the lab's reference range — most often C-reactive protein (CRP), sometimes ESR, ferritin, fibrinogen, or white cell count. It is a signal, not a diagnosis. Your liver raises these proteins in response to infection, injury, autoimmune activity, excess visceral fat, and dozens of other triggers. The phrase tells you your immune system is active. It does not tell you why."
  - q: "Is a CRP of 5 mg/L high?"
    a: "For cardiovascular risk purposes, yes — the AHA/CDC framework puts anything above 3.0 mg/L in the high-relative-risk band. But 5 mg/L is also the range you would expect from a cold you had last week, a dental infection, a hard unaccustomed workout, or a flare of an autoimmune condition. A single value of 5 mg/L should be repeated at least two weeks later, when you are well, before you treat it as your baseline."
  - q: "What is the difference between CRP and hs-CRP?"
    a: "They measure the same protein. The difference is assay sensitivity. A standard CRP test is designed to detect the large rises seen in infection and acute illness, and typically cannot resolve values below about 3-5 mg/L. A high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) assay resolves down to about 0.3 mg/L or lower, which is the range that matters for cardiovascular risk stratification. If you are tracking inflammation for prevention rather than diagnosing an acute illness, order hs-CRP."
  - q: "Can high inflammatory markers be caused by something harmless?"
    a: "Frequently. Recent infection, a vaccination, intensive gum (periodontal) treatment, poor sleep, intense unaccustomed exercise, and oral estrogen (combined contraceptives or oral hormone therapy) all raise CRP without indicating cardiovascular disease. Obesity raises it through a real mechanism — adipose tissue secretes interleukin-6, which drives hepatic CRP production — but that is a different problem from vascular inflammation. Context determines meaning."
  - q: "How do I lower high inflammatory markers?"
    a: "The interventions with the strongest evidence are losing visceral fat, regular aerobic and resistance exercise, stopping smoking, treating obstructive sleep apnea and periodontal disease if present, improving sleep duration and quality, and a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern. Statins also lower hs-CRP, partly independently of their effect on LDL. Retest after 8-12 weeks of consistent change rather than after a few days."

Your blood test came back with something flagged next to CRP. Maybe it reads 4.2 mg/L. Maybe it just says "elevated inflammatory markers" and leaves you to work out the rest.

That phrase does a lot of work in a consultation room and almost none on a printout. Inflammation is not a diagnosis. It is a signal that something has raised a protein your liver makes — an infection, an injury, excess visceral fat, an autoimmune process, or a cold you barely noticed. Here is what elevated inflammatory markers mean, how to read hs-CRP against the bands cardiologists use, and where the evidence is strong versus genuinely contested.

## What "inflammatory markers" refers to

There is no single inflammation test. The phrase covers proteins and cell counts that move when your innate immune system is active:

- **C-reactive protein (CRP)** — made by the liver, driven largely by interleukin-6. It rises within hours and falls within days, which makes it the most responsive of the group.<Cite id="pepys-2003-crp-update" />
- **ESR** — an indirect, slower measure reflecting fibrinogen. It lags CRP in both directions.<Cite id="bray-2016-esr-crp" />
- **Ferritin** — an iron storage protein that is also an acute-phase reactant, which is why inflammation makes iron stores look better than they are.<Cite id="sandnes-2021-hyperferritinemia" />
- **Fibrinogen** — a clotting protein that also rises with inflammation.<Cite id="bray-2016-esr-crp" />
- **[White blood cell count](/blog/white-blood-cell-count-high-low)** — non-specific, but a persistently high count alongside high CRP points to an active process.

[Homocysteine](/blog/high-homocysteine-heart-risk) is often listed with these, but it is a different animal: a methylation intermediate, not an acute-phase protein. It carries cardiovascular risk information, but it will not move because you have a chest infection.<Cite id="marti-carvajal-2017-homocysteine-lowering" /> For the full panel, see our breakdown of [inflammation markers and what each adds](/blog/inflammation-markers-silent-threat).

CRP is the one worth understanding properly: cheap, fast, responsive, and the only one with a formal cardiovascular risk framework attached.<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

## Standard CRP versus hs-CRP

Both measure the same protein; they differ in how low they resolve. A standard CRP assay is built for acute illness and generally cannot distinguish values below roughly 3-5 mg/L — it reports "normal" and stops.<Cite id="fda-2005-crp-assay-review-criteria" /> A high-sensitivity assay resolves to about 0.3 mg/L or lower.<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

The cardiovascular risk signal sits entirely in that low range. For prevention, order **hs-CRP**; for suspected infection, standard CRP is the right test.

## The hs-CRP risk bands

The categories most labs use come from a 2003 joint scientific statement by the American Heart Association and the CDC:<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

| hs-CRP (mg/L) | AHA/CDC category | Practical reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 1.0** | Low relative cardiovascular risk | Typical of lean, active, non-smoking adults |
| **1.0 to 3.0** | Average relative risk | Roughly the middle third of adults |
| **[Above 3.0](/blog/crp-3-elevated)** | High relative risk | Roughly double the risk of the lowest band |
| **Above 10.0** | Not a cardiovascular reading | Active inflammatory or infectious process; repeat when well |

Two caveats. These are **relative** risk bands — a 25-year-old and a 65-year-old at 3.5 mg/L do not carry the same absolute risk. And the 2018 AHA/ACC cholesterol guideline uses a different number: hs-CRP at or above **2.0 mg/L** is a "risk-enhancing factor" that can tip an intermediate-risk adult toward a statin.<Cite id="grundy-2019-aha-acc-cholesterol-guideline" /> The 2025 ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia update draws the same line in Europe, listing a persistently elevated hs-CRP above 2 mg/L as a risk modifier.<Cite id="mach-2025-esc-eas-dyslipidaemia-update" /> Both numbers are real; they answer different questions. Our guide to [optimal versus normal ranges](/blog/optimal-vs-normal-blood-test-ranges) covers why that gap exists.

## One reading is not a result

CRP has high within-person variability. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in *PLOS ONE* found a median within-subject coefficient of variation of about 44% for hs-CRP across 11 studies, with estimates from 27% to 76%.<Cite id="gough-2024-crp-within-subject-variation" /> Concretely: a true value of 2.0 mg/L can produce single readings anywhere from roughly 1.0 to 3.5 mg/L on different days, with nothing about your health having changed.

The AHA/CDC statement anticipated this and recommended **two measurements about two weeks apart**, averaged to give your stable baseline. Fasting is not required.<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />

So a single hs-CRP of 3.4 mg/L is not a finding. Two readings of 3.4 and 3.1 mg/L a month apart, taken while you were well, is.

## What raises CRP that has nothing to do with your arteries

| Cause | Typical effect on CRP |
|---|---|
| Acute bacterial infection | Large — often 50 to several hundred mg/L<Cite id="landry-2017-markedly-elevated-crp" /> |
| Viral infection, recent vaccination | Moderate, transient over days<Cite id="tsai-2005-influenza-vaccine-crp" /> |
| Surgery, injury, hard exercise | Transient rise over 24-72 hours<Cite id="kasapis-2005-physical-activity-crp" /> |
| Obesity | Sustained low-grade rise; adipose tissue secretes IL-6<Cite id="fontana-2007-visceral-fat-il6" /> |
| Smoking | Sustained; reverses over years after quitting<Cite id="bakhru-2005-smoking-cessation-nhanes" /> |
| Oral estrogen (combined pill, oral HRT) | Sustained rise via first-pass liver effect<Cite id="dreon-2003-oral-contraceptive-crp" /> |
| Autoimmune disease (RA, lupus, IBD) | Variable, tracks disease activity<Cite id="pepys-2003-crp-update" /> |
| Periodontal disease, sleep apnea | Modest but sustained<Cite id="paraskevas-2008-periodontitis-crp" /><Cite id="van-der-touw-2019-sleep-apnea-crp" /> |
| Chronic kidney disease | Sustained<Cite id="ridker-2026-zeus-design" /> |
| Statin therapy | Lowers hs-CRP, partly independent of its LDL effect<Cite id="albert-2001-prince-pravastatin-crp" /> |

The oral estrogen row is the one most often missed: it raises CRP through hepatic first-pass metabolism without a matching rise in vascular inflammation. Transdermal estrogen largely does not.<Cite id="vongpatanasin-2003-oral-transdermal-estrogen-crp" />

## Where the evidence is strong, and where it is not

**Prediction: strong.** A 2023 collaborative analysis in *The Lancet* by Ridker and colleagues pooled 31,245 statin-treated patients from the PROMINENT, REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH trials. Highest versus lowest quartile, hs-CRP predicted major adverse cardiovascular events at an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.31 (95% CI 1.20-1.43), cardiovascular death at 2.68 (2.22-3.23) and all-cause death at 2.42 (2.12-2.77). The matching LDL-C figures were 1.07 (0.98-1.17), 1.27 (1.07-1.50) and 1.16 (1.03-1.32). In people already on a statin, residual inflammation predicted outcomes more strongly than residual cholesterol.<Cite id="ridker-2023-residual-inflammation-statins" /> On the strength of data like these, a 2025 American College of Cardiology scientific statement called for broad hs-CRP screening in both primary and secondary prevention.<Cite id="mensah-2025-acc-inflammation-statement" />

**Causation: contested, and probably not.** A mendelian randomisation analysis by the CRP CHD Genetics Collaboration (*BMJ*, 2011) covered 194,418 participants including 46,557 coronary heart disease cases. Observed CRP associated with CHD at a risk ratio of 1.33 (1.23-1.43) per standard deviation; genetically raised CRP, which lifestyle cannot confound, gave 1.00 (0.90-1.13). Their conclusion: CRP itself is unlikely to be even a modest causal factor.<Cite id="wensley-2011-crp-mendelian-randomisation" />

**Lowering it on purpose: mixed.** This is the live question:

- **JUPITER (2008)** randomised 17,802 adults with LDL under 130 mg/dL and hs-CRP at or above 2.0 mg/L to rosuvastatin and cut events sharply (HR 0.56, 0.46-0.69) — but statins lower LDL too, so it cannot isolate inflammation.<Cite id="ridker-2008-jupiter" />
- **CANTOS (2017)** tested canakinumab, an IL-1-beta antibody, in 10,061 post-heart-attack patients. The 150 mg dose reduced events (HR 0.85, 0.74-0.98) with no change in LDL — the first clean signal that lowering inflammation alone can help. The effect was modest and fatal infections rose.<Cite id="ridker-2017-cantos" />
- **CIRT (2019)** tested low-dose methotrexate and found no benefit. It also failed to lower IL-6 or CRP.<Cite id="ridker-2019-cirt" />
- **Colchicine** succeeded in COLCOT (2019) and LoDoCo2 (2020);<Cite id="tardif-2019-colcot" /><Cite id="nidorf-2020-lodoco2" /> a low-dose formulation won FDA approval in 2023 for cardiovascular risk reduction.<Cite id="nelson-2023-low-dose-colchicine-review" />
- **ZEUS (2026)** tested ziltivekimab, an IL-6 inhibitor, in over 6,300 patients with atherosclerotic disease, chronic kidney disease and hs-CRP at or above 2 mg/L.<Cite id="ridker-2026-zeus-design" /> In topline results released by its sponsor in July 2026, it lowered IL-6 and hs-CRP as expected and did not reduce cardiovascular events (HR 0.99, 0.88-1.11).<Cite id="novo-nordisk-2026-zeus-topline" />

Taken together: hs-CRP is a powerful risk marker and, so far, a disappointing drug target. Read it as a thermometer, not a thermostat — treat a persistently elevated result as serious information, and address what is causing it rather than the number itself.

## What to do about a confirmed elevated hs-CRP

Assuming two readings two weeks apart, both taken while well, both [above 3 mg/L](/blog/crp-level-above-3-heart-risk):

1. **Reduce visceral fat.** The largest modifiable driver in most people — adipose tissue is an endocrine organ that secretes IL-6.<Cite id="fontana-2007-visceral-fat-il6" /> Across weight-loss studies, including surgery, CRP fell by about 0.13 mg/L for every kilogram lost.<Cite id="selvin-2007-weight-loss-crp" />
2. **Exercise consistently.** Aerobic and resistance training both lower CRP over months.<Cite id="fedewa-2017-exercise-training-crp" /><Cite id="kim-2022-resistance-training-crp" /> A single hard session raises it briefly, so do not test the next day.<Cite id="kasapis-2005-physical-activity-crp" />
3. **Fix sleep.** Disturbed, poor-quality sleep is linked to higher inflammatory markers;<Cite id="irwin-2016-sleep-inflammation" /> untreated sleep apnea is a common hidden cause.<Cite id="van-der-touw-2019-sleep-apnea-crp" />
4. **Stop smoking.** Large effect: smokers run roughly double the CRP of never-smokers, and the excess fades over years rather than weeks.<Cite id="wannamethee-2005-smoking-inflammatory-markers" /><Cite id="bakhru-2005-smoking-cessation-nhanes" />
5. **Eat a Mediterranean-style pattern.** In trials it lowered hs-CRP by about 1 mg/L.<Cite id="schwingshackl-2014-mediterranean-diet-inflammation" /> Our [protocol for lowering CRP](/blog/how-to-lower-crp-levels) ranks interventions by effect size, and the [omega-3 index test](/blog/omega-3-index-test) shows whether your fish intake is landing.
6. **See a dentist.** Periodontal disease is underrated here: it raises CRP, and treating it lowers CRP modestly.<Cite id="paraskevas-2008-periodontitis-crp" />

Retest after 8 to 12 weeks. Inflammation moves faster than most biomarkers, but not in days.

## When this needs a doctor, not a protocol

- A repeat hs-CRP above 10 mg/L when you feel well<Cite id="pearson-2003-aha-cdc-inflammation-markers" />
- Inflammation with unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, or persistent joint swelling
- A steady upward trend across three or more tests despite lifestyle change
- Elevated CRP with anemia, a high white cell count, or abnormal liver or kidney markers

These warrant a workup for autoimmune disease and occult infection. hs-CRP is not a cancer screen.

## Where CRP fits in a wider panel

Inflammation is one axis of cardiovascular risk, not the whole picture. It reads best alongside [ApoB](/blog/apob-heart-disease-risk), [Lp(a)](/blog/lp-a-lipoprotein-a-high), fasting insulin and blood pressure — which is how the [longevity blood panel](/blog/longevity-blood-panel) is built. hs-CRP is also one of the nine blood markers (alongside age) in the PhenoAge algorithm, so you can see its weighting in our [biological age calculator](/tools/phenoage-calculator).<Cite id="levine-2018-phenoage" /> Vitamin D status interacts with inflammatory signalling, which is one reason [vitamin D deficiency](/blog/vitamin-d-deficiency) and high CRP often appear together.<Cite id="zhou-2023-vitamin-d-crp-mendelian-randomisation" />
