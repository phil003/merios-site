## What Is Hematocrit?

Hematocrit measures the percentage of your blood composed of red blood cells (RBCs). When you get a [complete blood count (CBC)](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained), hematocrit is reported as a percentage—for example, 45% hematocrit means red blood cells occupy 45% of your total blood volume, with the remaining 55% being plasma and other components.<Cite id="billett-1990-hemoglobin-hematocrit" />

This simple measure tells you a lot. It reflects your body's oxygen-carrying capacity, your bone marrow's ability to produce RBCs, and your overall blood health. Both unusually high and unusually low hematocrit can signal underlying issues ranging from benign (like mild dehydration) to serious (like bone marrow disorders).<Cite id="medlineplus-hematocrit" />

## Normal Hematocrit Ranges

Reference ranges vary slightly by lab, but the standard benchmarks are well established:

| Group | Normal hematocrit |
|---|---|
| Men, adult | 40-54% |
| Women, adult | 36-48% |
| Pregnant women | 33% or higher (32% or higher in the second trimester), lowered by plasma expansion |
| Teens, 12-19 | about 33-44% (girls), 37-50% (boys) |
| Children, 6-11 | about 33-44% |
| Infants | 32-42% (newborns run higher, 45-61%) |

Adult ranges are standard published values.<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> The teen and child figures are the middle 95% of values in a US national survey.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" /> The pregnancy figures are CDC anemia cut-offs, and the infant range is from the NIH's MedlinePlus.<Cite id="cdc-1998-iron-deficiency" /><Cite id="medlineplus-hematocrit" />

**Key insight:** Women naturally have lower hematocrit than men, largely because of sex hormones: testosterone stimulates red blood cell production.<Cite id="murphy-2014-sex-difference-hemoglobin" /><Cite id="bachman-2014-testosterone-erythropoiesis" /> Pregnancy drops hematocrit further due to intentional plasma expansion—a physiologic adaptation that prepares the body for blood loss at delivery.<Cite id="horowitz-2013-anemia-pregnancy" />

Age, altitude, and individual variation matter.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" /><Cite id="cdc-1998-iron-deficiency" /> Someone living at sea level may have a hematocrit of 45%, while an athlete training at altitude might have 50% and still be healthy. Always compare your result to your lab's specific reference range, printed on your report.

## Is Your Specific Hematocrit Normal? (35, 40, 45, 50, 55)

Reference ranges are one thing; the number on your own report is another. Here is how the most-searched values read against the adult ranges above — remembering that the same number can be normal for one person and a flag for another, depending on sex, age, and hydration.

**Hematocrit of 35%** — Below the male range (40-54%) and just under the female range (36-48%). For a man, 35% is low and usually points toward anemia — most often [iron deficiency](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin), worth confirming with iron studies.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /> For a woman, 35% is borderline-low (the CDC's anemia cut-off for adult women is 35.7%), common with heavy periods or low iron, and worth checking if you feel tired or short of breath.<Cite id="cdc-1998-iron-deficiency" /> The red-cell indices tell you which kind of anemia it is — see [what MCV and MCH mean](/blog/mcv-mch-blood-test-meaning).

**Hematocrit of 40%** — Comfortably normal for women, and low-normal for men (right at the 40% floor). On its own, 40% in a man is rarely alarming, but if it has drifted down from a previous higher value, it is worth watching. In women, 40% is a healthy mid-range number.

**Hematocrit of 45%** — A textbook-healthy value. It sits mid-range for men and in the upper part of the normal range for women, reflecting good oxygen-carrying capacity. No action needed unless you have symptoms or it represents a big change from your baseline.

**Hematocrit of 50%** — High-normal for men (still inside 40-54%) and above range for women. In a man, 50% is often fine, but check for the usual drivers of a rising count: dehydration on the day of the draw, sleep apnea, smoking, or testosterone therapy.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" /> In a woman, 50% is elevated and worth investigating.

**Hematocrit of 55%** — Above the normal range for both sexes and into [polycythemia](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained) territory. First rule out dehydration, then look for a cause: chronic low oxygen, smoking, testosterone therapy, or a bone-marrow condition like polycythemia vera.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" /> Testosterone-therapy guidelines call for pausing treatment once hematocrit passes 54%, and polycythemia vera is treated to keep it below 45%, because thickened blood raises clotting and cardiovascular risk.<Cite id="bhasin-2018-testosterone-guideline" /><Cite id="tefferi-2023-polycythemia-vera" /><Cite id="marchioli-2013-cyto-pv" /> This is a number to discuss with your doctor rather than dismiss.

## What High Hematocrit Means

A hematocrit above the normal range is called **polycythemia** or **erythrocytosis**—an elevated red blood cell count relative to plasma.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />

### Causes of High Hematocrit

**Dehydration (a common cause)**
Losing fluids without replacing them concentrates your blood, raising hematocrit artificially. A blood test after hard exercise, heavy sweating, or inadequate water intake often shows elevated hematocrit. This settles once you are rehydrated.<Cite id="billett-1990-hemoglobin-hematocrit" />

**Polycythemia Vera**
This myeloproliferative disorder causes the bone marrow to overproduce red blood cells autonomously. WHO criteria flag hemoglobin above 16.5 g/dL or hematocrit above 49% in men (16.0 g/dL or 48% in women), alongside bone marrow findings and, in most cases, a JAK2 mutation.<Cite id="arber-2016-who-myeloid" /><Cite id="khoury-2022-who-myeloid" /> Patients are at increased risk for blood clots, stroke, and myocardial infarction.<Cite id="tefferi-2023-polycythemia-vera" /><Cite id="marchioli-2013-cyto-pv" />

**Chronic Hypoxia**
Your body compensates for low oxygen by producing more RBCs. Causes include:<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />
- High altitude residence (the effect starts from around 3,000 feet)<Cite id="cdc-1998-iron-deficiency" />
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
- Cyanotic heart disease
- Sleep apnea (especially untreated)

**Testosterone Replacement Therapy or Anabolic Steroids**
Testosterone stimulates erythropoietin (EPO), which signals the bone marrow to produce more RBCs.<Cite id="bachman-2014-testosterone-erythropoiesis" /> In trials in middle-aged and older men, those on testosterone were nearly four times as likely as men on placebo to have a hematocrit above 50%, the most frequent side effect of treatment. This is a known side effect; monitoring is essential.<Cite id="calof-2005-testosterone-adverse-events" />

**Smoking**
Chronic smokers have elevated hematocrit as an adaptation to reduced oxygen availability from carbon monoxide exposure.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />

**EPO-Secreting Tumors**
Rarely, kidney tumors or other cancers produce EPO ectopically, driving excessive RBC production.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />

**Key insight:** High hematocrit thickens blood, increasing viscosity. In a large population study, each 5-point rise in hematocrit raised the risk of venous blood clots (such as DVT) by about 25%, with a stronger link in men.<Cite id="braekkan-2010-hematocrit-vte" /> In polycythemia vera, a randomized trial found that keeping hematocrit at 45-50% rather than below 45% led to more cardiovascular deaths and major blood clots,<Cite id="marchioli-2013-cyto-pv" /> whereas for secondary erythrocytosis there is no definitive evidence of extra clot risk.<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />

## What Low Hematocrit Means

Low hematocrit is called **anemia**—insufficient red blood cells to meet oxygen demands. It's among the most common blood abnormalities.<Cite id="gbd-2023-anaemia" />

### Causes of Low Hematocrit

**Iron Deficiency Anemia**
Iron is essential for hemoglobin synthesis. Without it, RBCs are small (microcytic) and contain less hemoglobin. Causes include heavy menstrual bleeding, GI bleeding (ulcers, hemorrhoids, occult bleeding), inadequate dietary intake, and poor absorption.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="pasricha-2021-iron-deficiency" /> This is the most common cause globally.<Cite id="gbd-2023-anaemia" /> Ferritin (your iron stores) often falls long before hemoglobin does, which is why [normal hemoglobin can still hide iron deficiency](/blog/low-ferritin-normal-hemoglobin).<Cite id="pasricha-2021-iron-deficiency" />

**Nutritional Deficiencies**
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency:** RBCs are large (macrocytic); seen in pernicious anemia, vegans without supplementation, or those with absorption issues<Cite id="ods-vitamin-b12" />
- **Folate deficiency:** Also causes macrocytic anemia; associated with poor diet, alcohol abuse, or certain medications<Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />
- **Vitamin C deficiency:** Vitamin C helps absorb iron from plant foods, and severe deficiency (scurvy) can lead to iron deficiency anemia<Cite id="ods-vitamin-c" />

**Chronic Disease Anemia**
Kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, cancer, and chronic infections reduce EPO production or increase inflammation, suppressing RBC synthesis.<Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-of-inflammation" />

**Kidney Disease**
The kidneys produce erythropoietin. Advanced chronic kidney disease causes EPO deficiency and anemia.<Cite id="niddk-anemia-ckd" />

**Blood Loss**
Acute hemorrhage (trauma, surgery) or chronic bleeding (GI ulcers, heavy periods) deplete RBC reserves faster than the bone marrow can replenish them.<Cite id="brill-2000-normocytic-anemia" /><Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" />

**Bone Marrow Disorders**
Aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, and leukemia impair RBC production.<Cite id="medlineplus-hematocrit" />

**Hemolysis (RBC Destruction)**
Conditions like hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, or sickle cell disease destroy RBCs faster than they're replaced.<Cite id="medlineplus-hematocrit" />

**Pregnancy**
Plasma volume expands by close to 50% during pregnancy, more than red cell mass does, intentionally lowering hematocrit.<Cite id="aguree-2019-plasma-volume-pregnancy" /><Cite id="horowitz-2013-anemia-pregnancy" /> This is normal and protective, part of the body's preparation for blood loss at delivery.<Cite id="horowitz-2013-anemia-pregnancy" />

**Key insight:** Low hematocrit causes fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, dizziness, and reduced exercise capacity.<Cite id="who-anaemia-factsheet" /> By WHO criteria, anemia is severe once hemoglobin falls below 8 g/dL.<Cite id="who-2024-hemoglobin-cutoffs" />

## Hematocrit vs. Hemoglobin: How They Differ

These are often confused, but they measure different things:

| Measure | What It Is | Units | Typical Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hematocrit** | % of blood volume as RBCs | % | 45% (men) |
| **Hemoglobin** | Oxygen-carrying protein in RBCs | g/dL | 15 g/dL (men) |

Hemoglobin is the protein that binds oxygen. Hematocrit is the volume occupied by the cells containing that protein. Normally, they move together—a low [hemoglobin level](/blog/hemoglobin-14-normal) usually means low hematocrit.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" /> But discrepancies occur, and because hemoglobin divided by hematocrit gives the MCHC (how densely the cells are filled with hemoglobin), they point to the cells' hemoglobin content rather than their size:<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />

- **Hematocrit high relative to hemoglobin:** cells are underfilled with hemoglobin (a low MCHC), the hypochromic pattern of iron deficiency<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" />
- **Hemoglobin high relative to hematocrit:** cells are unusually dense (a high MCHC), as in hereditary spherocytosis<Cite id="sarma-1990-red-cell-indices" />
- **Both low:** True anemia requiring investigation

## When to Be Concerned

**High hematocrit warrants investigation if:**
- Hematocrit >55% and you're not dehydrated
- You have symptoms: headache, itching, blurred vision, or chest pain<Cite id="tefferi-2023-polycythemia-vera" /><Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />
- You're on testosterone replacement<Cite id="bhasin-2018-testosterone-guideline" />
- You have risk factors for thrombosis

**Low hematocrit warrants investigation if:**
- Hematocrit &lt;36% (below normal for women) or &lt;40% (below normal for men), especially if it's a new change
- You have fatigue, dyspnea, dizziness, or reduced exercise tolerance
- It's dropping over time
- You have unexplained symptoms

Ask your doctor about next steps: iron studies, reticulocyte count, B12/folate levels, kidney function, or bone marrow biopsy if indicated.<Cite id="van-vranken-2010-microcytosis" /><Cite id="kaferle-2009-macrocytosis" />

## Hematocrit and Cardiovascular Risk

Elevated hematocrit (especially >50%) increases blood viscosity, raising the blood's resistance to flow.<Cite id="lowe-1987-blood-rheology" /> This:
- Elevates clotting risk<Cite id="braekkan-2010-hematocrit-vte" />
- Can cause headaches, visual disturbances, or light-headedness in polycythemia vera<Cite id="mithoowani-2020-erythrocytosis" />

Clinical guidelines call for checking hematocrit in men on testosterone replacement (at baseline, at 3-6 months, then yearly) and pausing therapy if it rises above 54%. If hematocrit climbs too high, options include stopping temporarily and restarting at a reduced dose, or therapeutic phlebotomy (blood removal).<Cite id="bhasin-2018-testosterone-guideline" />

Conversely, very low hematocrit (anemia) forces the heart to beat faster to deliver oxygen, also raising cardiovascular strain.<Cite id="medlineplus-anemia" /> The optimal range protects both oxygen delivery and cardiovascular workload.

## How to Optimize Hematocrit

**If hematocrit is high:**
- Ensure adequate hydration
- If on TRT, discuss with your doctor; dose reduction or blood donation may help<Cite id="bhasin-2018-testosterone-guideline" />
- Address sleep apnea if present
- Quit smoking
- Avoid high-altitude training if at risk for thrombosis

**If hematocrit is low:**
- Get [iron studies](/blog/ferritin-30-low-normal) (ferritin, iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation) to assess iron status
- Check [B12 and folate levels](/blog/vitamin-b12-level-300)
- Screen for GI bleeding if cause is unclear<Cite id="pasricha-2021-iron-deficiency" />
- Increase iron-rich foods (red meat, legumes, dark leafy greens)
- Add vitamin C to enhance iron absorption
- Treat underlying conditions (kidney disease, thyroid disorder, infection)
