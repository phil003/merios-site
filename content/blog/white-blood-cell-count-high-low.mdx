    a: "A normal WBC count for adults is 4,500–11,000 white blood cells per microliter (µL) of blood. This can vary slightly between labs. Children typically have higher normal ranges. Your count fluctuates throughout the day — it is normally lowest in the morning and rises as the day goes on."

    a: "A high WBC count (leukocytosis, above 11,000/µL) most commonly indicates your immune system is responding to an infection or inflammation. Other causes include physical or emotional stress, corticosteroid medications, smoking, allergies, and intense exercise. Rarely, very high counts (above 30,000) can indicate a blood cancer like leukemia. Most mild elevations (11,000–15,000) are caused by common infections or stress and resolve on their own."

    a: "A low WBC count (leukopenia, below 4,500/µL) can be caused by viral infections (which temporarily suppress WBC production), autoimmune conditions, certain medications (chemotherapy, antibiotics, antipsychotics), bone marrow disorders, or nutritional deficiencies (B12, folate, copper).   - q: "When should I worry about my white blood cell count?"
    a: "Seek medical attention for an absolute neutrophil count below 1,000/µL (infection risk rises significantly), a WBC above 30,000/µL without obvious infection, or a count that is trending steadily up or down over multiple tests. A white blood cell count is one of the most fundamental numbers on any [complete blood count](/blog/cbc-blood-test-results-explained). Your **white blood cells** (WBCs, also called leukocytes) are your immune system's army — they detect, fight, and remember every pathogen, abnormal cell, and foreign substance that enters your body.

When your WBC count is abnormal, it is your immune system sending a signal. The question is: what is it saying?

## Normal WBC ranges

| Population | Normal range |
|---|---|
| **Adults** | 4,500–11,000/µL |
| **Children (3–11 years)** | about 4,000–12,700/µL |
| **Toddlers (1–2 years)** | about 4,900–14,100/µL |
| **Newborns** | 13,000–38,000/µL at 12 hours of life, falling to 5,000–20,000/µL by two weeks |

The adult and newborn figures come from a standard clinical review.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" /> The children's ranges are the middle 95% of values in a US national survey.<Cite id="nchs-2005-hematology-reference" />

WBC counts naturally fluctuate throughout the day (lowest in the morning, rising toward evening), during exercise, and with stress.<Cite id="sennels-2011-diurnal-variation" /><Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" /> A single mildly abnormal reading often means nothing clinically significant.

## High white blood cell count (leukocytosis)

Leukocytosis is defined as WBC above 11,000/µL.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

### Common causes

**Infection** is a leading cause. Bacterial infections typically cause the most pronounced WBC elevation, driven by a surge in neutrophils. A moderately raised WBC with a respiratory infection, UTI, or skin infection is expected and resolves as the infection clears.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

**Physical and emotional stress** triggers the release of stress hormones such as adrenaline, which flush white blood cells off the vessel walls and out of the bone marrow's storage pool into circulation. A stressful day, surgery, burns, or even an anxiety episode can temporarily raise WBC.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

**Medications** — corticosteroids (prednisone, dexamethasone) commonly cause leukocytosis, sometimes pushing WBC above 20,000.<Cite id="shoenfeld-1981-prednisone-leukocytosis" /> Lithium, beta-agonists, and epinephrine also elevate WBC.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" />

**Smoking** causes a chronic, modest rise in white cells, and the count falls back within about a year of quitting.<Cite id="higuchi-2016-smoking-wbc" />

**Exercise** — intense exercise causes a transient spike in WBC that can last several hours. If your blood draw was within a few hours of a hard workout, this may explain a mild elevation.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" /><Cite id="peake-2017-exercise-immune-recovery" />

**Allergies and asthma** — specifically cause eosinophil elevation, one of the five WBC subtypes.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

### When high WBC is serious

A WBC above 30,000/µL without obvious infection warrants urgent evaluation. Possible causes include severe bacterial sepsis, leukemia (chronic or acute), or myeloproliferative disorders. A very high WBC with many immature cells ("blasts") on the differential is a red flag that requires immediate hematology referral.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />

## Low white blood cell count (leukopenia)

Leukopenia is defined as WBC below 4,500/µL.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" />

### Common causes

**Viral infections** are the most common cause of temporary leukopenia.<Cite id="tefferi-2005-abnormal-cbc" /> Influenza, COVID-19, HIV, and mononucleosis can all lower white cell counts.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" /><Cite id="medlineplus-differential" /><Cite id="zhao-2020-covid-lymphopenia" /> With short-lived infections, the count usually recovers quickly once the illness passes.<Cite id="boxer-2012-neutropenia" />

**Medications** — chemotherapy is the most well-known cause. Other medications that can lower WBC include certain antibiotics (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), antipsychotics (clozapine), anticonvulsants, immunosuppressants, and some anti-thyroid drugs.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" /><Cite id="lazaro-2015-neutropenia-ra" /> Medication-induced leukopenia is usually reversible.<Cite id="andersohn-2007-agranulocytosis" />

**Autoimmune conditions** — lupus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune diseases can destroy white blood cells or suppress their production.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" /><Cite id="lazaro-2015-neutropenia-ra" />

**Nutritional deficiencies** — severe [B12 deficiency](/blog/vitamin-b12-level-300), folate deficiency, or copper deficiency can impair WBC production in the bone marrow.<Cite id="kim-2025-neutropenia" /><Cite id="halfdanarson-2008-copper-deficiency" /><Cite id="ods-vitamin-b12" />

**Benign ethnic neutropenia** — now often called Duffy-null associated neutrophil count — comes from a common genetic variant: about two-thirds of Black Americans in one study carried it, and the pattern is also seen in people of Middle Eastern and Caribbean descent.<Cite id="merz-2023-duffy-null-anc" /><Cite id="atallah-yunes-2019-benign-ethnic-neutropenia" /> Neutrophil counts are constitutionally lower (a median of about 2,800/µL versus 5,000/µL in non-carriers) without any increased infection risk.<Cite id="merz-2023-duffy-null-anc" /> This is a normal genetic variant, not a disease — but standard lab ranges flag roughly a fifth to a half of healthy carriers as neutropenic, and it frequently causes unnecessary concern and testing when not recognized.<Cite id="hibbs-2026-duffy-null-reference-intervals" /><Cite id="atallah-yunes-2019-benign-ethnic-neutropenia" />

**Bone marrow disorders** — myelodysplastic syndromes, aplastic anemia, and bone marrow infiltration (from leukemia or metastatic cancer) can reduce all blood cell lines. These are less common but important to exclude when leukopenia is persistent and unexplained.<Cite id="medlineplus-wbc" />

### When low WBC is serious

Infection risk climbs as the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) falls, and below 1,000/µL (moderate neutropenia) it is significantly increased.<Cite id="lazaro-2015-neutropenia-ra" /><Cite id="kim-2025-neutropenia" /> This level of neutropenia requires medical attention and potentially protective measures. An ANC below 500 is considered severe neutropenia and carries high risk of serious, life-threatening infections.<Cite id="kim-2025-neutropenia" /><Cite id="boxer-2012-neutropenia" />

## The WBC differential: why subtypes matter

A total WBC count is a headline number. The differential tells the full story. Typical adult percentages are shown below.<Cite id="medlineplus-differential" />

| Subtype | Normal % | Elevated in | Low in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophils** | 40–60% | Bacterial infection, stress, steroids | Some medications, autoimmune |
| **Lymphocytes** | 20–40% | Viral infection, some leukemias | HIV, steroids, some autoimmune<Cite id="statpearls-2024-cbc" /> |
| **Monocytes** | 2–8% | Chronic infection, autoimmune | Rare (some cancers) |
| **Eosinophils** | 1–4% | Allergies, asthma, parasites, drug reaction | Cushing syndrome, acute stress |
| **Basophils** | 0.5–1% | Allergic conditions, some leukemias | Very rarely relevant |

Looking at which subtype is driving the abnormality is how doctors narrow the diagnosis. "High WBC" driven by high neutrophils points toward bacterial infection. "High WBC" driven by high eosinophils points toward allergies or parasites.<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" /> Same total, completely different meaning.

## Tracking WBC over time

A single WBC reading is a snapshot. A trend tells a story. Serial measurements over months can reveal:

- **Resolution of an acute process** — WBC normalizes after infection clears
- **Chronic elevation** — persistent leukocytosis from smoking, chronic inflammation, or occult infection<Cite id="riley-2015-leukocytosis" />
- **Gradual decline** — may indicate progressive bone marrow failure or medication effect
- **Stability** — a slightly low WBC that is stable over years is likely benign (ethnic variant or constitutional)<Cite id="lazaro-2015-neutropenia-ra" />

WBC is only one line of the complete blood count — your red-cell markers like [hemoglobin](/blog/hemoglobin-14-normal) and [hematocrit](/blog/hematocrit-levels-high-low-meaning) tell a parallel story worth reading alongside it.
