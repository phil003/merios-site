  - q: "Why is my ferritin still low after taking iron supplements?"
    a: "The most common reasons are: taking iron with absorption blockers (coffee, tea, calcium, dairy), using a poorly absorbed form (ferric or polysaccharide iron), chronic inflammation trapping iron in storage (elevated hepcidin), an underlying gut absorption issue (celiac, H. pylori, low stomach acid), or simply not taking enough for long enough. Refilling iron stores usually means continuing supplements for about 3 months after hemoglobin normalizes."
  - q: "What is the best form of iron supplement to take?"
    a: "Ferrous sulfate is the most studied and cheapest option and the guideline standard, but it often causes GI side effects. Ferrous bisglycinate (iron bisglycinate chelate) is usually better tolerated: in trials, mostly in pregnant women, it caused fewer GI side effects than other iron supplements. Avoid ferric forms such as ferric pyrophosphate unless specifically prescribed — they are less bioavailable."
  - q: "Should I take iron with vitamin C?"
    a: "It helps with food, but it's probably unnecessary with tablets.   - q: "How long does it take to raise ferritin levels?"
    a: "Hemoglobin usually starts rising within 2–4 weeks. Ferritin takes longer: guidelines advise continuing iron for about 3 months after hemoglobin normalizes to replenish stores, and people with severe depletion may need longer. If ferritin hasn't moved after 3 months of proper supplementation, investigate absorption issues or underlying causes."

You have been dutifully swallowing iron pills for weeks, maybe months. Your ferritin barely moved. Or you stopped because the GI side effects were unbearable. Either way, you are still exhausted, still losing hair, still waking up tired.

This is one of the most common stories in the iron deficiency world — and almost always, the problem is not that "iron doesn't work for me." The problem is **how** and **what** you are taking, or something else going on underneath. Oral iron *does* work for the large majority of people;<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /> it just has to clear six specific hurdles first. Here they are, in the order worth checking.

## Reason 1: You are taking iron with the wrong things

Iron absorption is remarkably sensitive to what else is in your gut at the same time.

**Absorption blockers (avoid within 2 hours of iron):**

- **Coffee and tea** — tannins and polyphenols reduce iron absorption by up to 60-90%<Cite id="hurrell-1999-polyphenols" />
- **Calcium and dairy** — the calcium in a serving of milk or cheese cut iron absorption by 50-60% in meal studies<Cite id="hallberg-1991-calcium" />
- **Whole grains and legumes** — phytates bind iron and reduce uptake<Cite id="kumar-2022-ida-review" />
- **Antacids, PPIs, and H2 blockers** — reducing stomach acid cuts iron absorption (stomach acid aids iron absorption)<Cite id="lam-2017-ppi" /><Cite id="kumar-2022-ida-review" />
- **Other mineral supplements** — calcium supplements reduce iron absorption just as dairy does<Cite id="hallberg-1991-calcium" />

**Absorption enhancers (take with iron):**

- **Vitamin C** — improves non-heme iron absorption from food, but adding it to iron tablets didn't improve results in a large trial<Cite id="ods-iron" /><Cite id="li-2020-vitamin-c" />
- **Empty stomach** — or at minimum, away from meals, which can cut absorption by up to 75%<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />
- **Acidic environment** — stomach acid is your friend here<Cite id="kumar-2022-ida-review" />

A common mistake: taking iron with your morning coffee. In meal studies, a cup of coffee cut iron absorption by 39% to more than 80%, depending on the meal.<Cite id="morck-1983-coffee" />

## Reason 2: You are taking the wrong form

Not all iron supplements are equal.

| Form | Absorption | GI tolerance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ferrous bisglycinate** | Good | Good | Fewer GI side effects in trials (mostly pregnancy) — chelated, gentle |
| **Ferrous sulfate** | Moderate-High | Poor-Moderate | Cheapest, most studied, guideline standard; GI side effects common |
| **Ferrous gluconate** | Moderate-High | Poor-Moderate | Less elemental iron per tablet (12% by weight) |
| **Ferrous fumarate** | Moderate-High | Poor-Moderate | High elemental iron content (33% by weight) |
| **Ferric iron forms** | Low | Variable | Poorly absorbed — avoid unless prescribed |
| **Polysaccharide iron** | Low-Moderate | No better than sulfate | Less effective than ferrous sulfate in a trial in children |

At standard doses, ferrous sulfate, gluconate and fumarate work about equally well and differ mainly in how much elemental iron each tablet carries (about 20%, 12% and 33% by weight); ferrous sulfate roughly doubles the odds of GI side effects compared with placebo.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="ods-iron" /><Cite id="tolkien-2015-ferrous-sulfate-gi" /> Bisglycinate's edge is tolerability, polysaccharide iron raised hemoglobin and ferritin less than ferrous sulfate (with more diarrhea) in a trial in children, and ferric pyrophosphate is absorbed less well than ferrous sulfate.<Cite id="fischer-2023-bisglycinate" /><Cite id="powers-2017-polysaccharide-iron" /><Cite id="moretti-2006-ferric-pyrophosphate" />

If you have been taking ferric pyrophosphate or iron from a prenatal vitamin with calcium, you may be absorbing a fraction of the iron you think you are.<Cite id="moretti-2006-ferric-pyrophosphate" /><Cite id="hallberg-1991-calcium" />

**Switch to ferrous bisglycinate** (brands like Thorne Iron Bisglycinate, Pure Encapsulations Iron-C). In trials, mostly in pregnant women, it caused about a third as many GI side effects as other iron supplements.<Cite id="fischer-2023-bisglycinate" />

## Reason 3: You are not taking enough (or not long enough)

Many people take the 18 mg of elemental iron in a typical women's multivitamin and expect it to fix a deficiency.<Cite id="ods-iron" /> That is well below a treatment dose. To replenish depleted iron stores:

- **Therapeutic dose:** 50-100 mg elemental iron per day (or every other day — more on this below)<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />
- **Duration:** about 3 months after hemoglobin normalizes to replenish ferritin stores<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />
- **Common mistake:** stopping iron when you "feel better" — hemoglobin usually starts rising within 2 weeks, but stores need about 3 more months after hemoglobin normalizes<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />

### The every-other-day protocol

Research published in *The Lancet Haematology* showed that iron absorption is actually **higher** when iron is taken every other day rather than daily.<Cite id="stoffel-2017-alternate-day" /> This is because a single dose of iron triggers hepcidin release (a hormone that blocks iron absorption), and hepcidin stays elevated for about 24 hours.<Cite id="moretti-2015-hepcidin" /><Cite id="stoffel-2020-alternate-day-ida" />

Taking a double dose every other day (for example 100 mg instead of 50 mg daily) on an empty stomach delivers a similar amount of absorbed iron with fewer side effects: in a 2023 trial, ferritin ended up the same at equal total doses, but GI side effects were less frequent on alternate days.<Cite id="stoffel-2020-alternate-day-ida" /><Cite id="von-siebenthal-2023-alternate-day" />

## Reason 4: Chronic inflammation is blocking absorption

This is the sneaky one. If your body is chronically inflamed, it raises **hepcidin** — a liver hormone that blocks iron absorption in the gut and traps iron in storage cells (macrophages).<Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" /> Your body is essentially hiding iron from potential pathogens.<Cite id="ganz-2018-iron-infection" />

Signs this might be your situation:

- Ferritin is low-normal or normal but you still feel iron-deficient
- CRP is elevated (above 5 mg/L, the cut-off WHO uses to flag inflammation when reading ferritin)<Cite id="who-2020-ferritin" />
- You have an autoimmune condition, chronic infection, or obesity<Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" />
- Iron supplements cause no ferritin increase<Cite id="pasricha-2021-iron-deficiency" />

If you suspect inflammatory iron trapping, ask for: **hsCRP, ferritin, serum iron, TIBC, and transferrin saturation**. A pattern of normal-to-high ferritin with low transferrin saturation and elevated CRP points to inflammation, which can mask or coexist with true deficiency.<Cite id="weiss-2019-anemia-inflammation" /><Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /> You can compute yours with the free [transferrin saturation calculator](/tools/transferrin-saturation-calculator).

## Reason 5: A gut absorption issue

Several GI conditions impair iron absorption:

- **Celiac disease** — damages the duodenum, where iron is absorbed.<Cite id="kumar-2022-ida-review" /> Undiagnosed celiac turns up in about 1 in 30 people with iron-deficiency anemia, which is why guidelines advise testing for it.<Cite id="mahadev-2018-celiac" /><Cite id="aga-2020-ida" />
- **H. pylori infection** — this stomach bacterium is linked to iron deficiency, and treating it alongside iron raised ferritin more than iron alone in trials.<Cite id="hudak-2017-h-pylori" /> Ask for an H. pylori breath or stool test if iron doesn't respond.<Cite id="aga-2020-ida" />
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis) — gut inflammation reduces absorption and increases iron loss through GI bleeding.<Cite id="pasricha-2021-iron-deficiency" /><Cite id="auerbach-2025-iron-deficiency" />
- **Low stomach acid** (hypochlorhydria) — seen with atrophic gastritis and long-term PPI use; stomach acid aids iron absorption.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" /><Cite id="lam-2017-ppi" /><Cite id="kumar-2022-ida-review" />
- **Gastric bypass surgery** — bypasses the duodenum where iron absorption occurs.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />

If you have been supplementing correctly for 3+ months with no improvement, a GI workup is warranted.<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />

## Reason 6: Ongoing blood loss

No amount of supplementation will fix iron deficiency if you are losing more than you are absorbing. Common sources:

- **Heavy menstrual periods** — the most common cause of iron deficiency in premenopausal women<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />
- **GI bleeding** — ulcers, polyps, hemorrhoids, or occult blood loss<Cite id="bsg-2021-ida" />
- **Frequent blood donation**<Cite id="ods-iron" />
- **Endurance exercise** — foot-strike hemolysis and GI blood loss in runners<Cite id="peeling-2008-athletes" />
