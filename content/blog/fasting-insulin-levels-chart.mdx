Your fasting glucose is 92 mg/dL. Your doctor says it is normal. You move on with your day.

But what if the real story is hiding behind a number your doctor never ordered?

**Fasting insulin** is a metabolic marker that can flag trouble years before fasting glucose does: in a 24-year study of adults with normal blood sugar, high fasting insulin was the strongest predictor of later dysglycemia.<Cite id="dankner-2009-hyperinsulinemia" /> Insulin resistance is an early warning sign for type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome.<Cite id="bonora-2007-homa-cvd" /><Cite id="bonora-1998-bruneck-insulin-resistance" /> And most physicians do not routinely test it.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis" />

## Why fasting insulin matters more than you think

Here is the key insight: your body will fight to keep blood glucose in a narrow range. It does this by producing **more insulin**. So in the early stages of insulin resistance, your glucose looks fine — because your pancreas is compensating by pumping out extra insulin.<Cite id="defronzo-2009-banting" /> If you do not have a fasting insulin result, the [TyG index calculator](/tools/tyg-index-calculator) estimates insulin resistance from triglycerides and glucose alone.

This is called **compensatory hyperinsulinemia**. Your glucose is 90 mg/dL. Everything looks great. But your insulin is 20 µIU/mL — your pancreas is working overtime to achieve that result.

Eventually, the pancreas cannot keep up. Glucose starts rising. First to [100 mg/dL](/blog/fasting-glucose-100-borderline), then [110 mg/dL](/blog/fasting-glucose-100-borderline#fasting-glucose-110-mg-dl), then 126 mg/dL (the diabetes threshold). But by that point, you have been insulin resistant for **years**.<Cite id="tabak-2009-whitehall-trajectories" />

Fasting insulin catches the problem at the beginning, not the end.

## Fasting insulin levels chart

Here is how to interpret your fasting insulin level:

| Fasting insulin (µIU/mL) | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Below 3** | Very insulin sensitive — typical of lean, active individuals |
| **3–5** | Optimal — the target range for metabolic health and longevity |
| **5–8** | Normal — within healthy range for most adults |
| **8–12** | Borderline — early insulin resistance may be developing |
| **12–20** | Elevated — significant insulin resistance likely present |
| **Above 20** | High — strong indicator of metabolic syndrome or pre-diabetes |
| **Above 25** | Very high — almost always associated with metabolic dysfunction |

### Important context

These bands are a rough guide, not a clinical standard: no guideline defines an optimal fasting insulin, and insulin assays are not standardized, so the same sample can read quite differently from one lab method to another.<Cite id="marcovina-2007-insulin-assays" /> Population studies have instead used percentiles — in one study of healthy adults, the top quarter started at a fasting insulin of 12 µIU/mL.<Cite id="ascaso-2003-insulin-resistance-cutoffs" />

Standard lab reference ranges will call anything from about 2.6 to 24.9 µIU/mL "normal."<Cite id="labcorp-insulin-test" /> This is misleading. Lab ranges reflect the tested population, and in the United States, only about 12% of adults have optimal levels across five basic metabolic markers (according to a 2019 UNC Chapel Hill study).<Cite id="araujo-2019-metabolic-health" /> The "normal" range is skewed by a metabolically unhealthy population.

An optimal fasting insulin is not just below the upper lab limit — it is **well below** it.

## Fasting insulin by age and sex

Fasting insulin tends to increase with age, driven by declining muscle mass, increasing visceral fat, reduced physical activity, and hormonal changes. But this increase is not inevitable — it is modifiable: in a clamp study, the drop in insulin sensitivity with age was explained by rising body fat rather than age itself.<Cite id="karakelides-2010-age-insulin-sensitivity" /> The ranges below are illustrative, not lab reference intervals.

| Age group | Typical range (µIU/mL) | Optimal target |
|---|---|---|
| **18–30** | 3–10 | Below 6 |
| **30–40** | 4–12 | Below 7 |
| **40–50** | 5–15 | Below 8 |
| **50–60** | 5–18 | Below 8 |
| **60+** | 5–20 | Below 10 |

Women may see minor fluctuations across the menstrual cycle, with insulin resistance highest in the luteal phase and tracking estradiol and progesterone.<Cite id="yeung-2010-biocycle" /> Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common causes of elevated fasting insulin in premenopausal women: it is common and carries intrinsic insulin resistance, independent of weight.<Cite id="cassar-2016-pcos-insulin-resistance" />

## HOMA-IR: combining insulin and glucose

Fasting insulin is most powerful when paired with fasting glucose to calculate **HOMA-IR** (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance):

**HOMA-IR = (Fasting Glucose mg/dL × Fasting Insulin µIU/mL) ÷ 405**

That is the standard approximation of the 1985 model (÷ 22.5 when glucose is in mmol/L), which defines 1.0 as normal.<Cite id="matthews-1985-homa" /><Cite id="wallace-2004-homa" />

| HOMA-IR | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Below 1.0** | Excellent insulin sensitivity |
| **1.0–1.5** | Optimal |
| **1.5–2.0** | Normal but worth monitoring |
| **2.0–2.9** | Early insulin resistance |
| **3.0+** | Significant insulin resistance |

Research thresholds for [insulin resistance](/blog/homa-ir-insulin-resistance) vary with age, sex and ethnicity: population studies have proposed cut-offs from under 2 to about 3.5, so a single "2.5" rule is a simplification.<Cite id="gayoso-diz-2013-homa-cutoffs" /><Cite id="tahapary-2022-homa-cutoffs" /> Many longevity physicians prefer to see it below 1.5. You can run your two fasting numbers through the [HOMA-IR calculator](/tools/homa-ir-calculator) to get your score instantly.

## Why your doctor probably does not test it

Fasting insulin is not included in the standard basic metabolic panel (BMP) or comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP).<Cite id="medlineplus-cmp" /> The current screening guidelines from the ADA (American Diabetes Association) focus on fasting glucose and HbA1c (plus the oral glucose tolerance test) — all of which detect problems at a later stage.<Cite id="ada-2025-diagnosis" />

The reasons fasting insulin is not standard:

- **Guidelines lag behind research** — clinical guidelines take years to update, and fasting insulin's utility is still debated in traditional endocrinology
- **Cost sensitivity** — adding tests increases healthcare costs, and insurers push for the minimum
- **Treatment threshold uncertainty** — there is no universally agreed cut-off for fasting insulin, partly because insulin assays are not standardized (unlike HbA1c ≥ 6.5% = diabetes)<Cite id="marcovina-2007-insulin-assays" />
- **Variability** — insulin levels can fluctuate based on recent meals, stress, sleep, and time of day, which is why averaging repeat samples is advised for individuals<Cite id="wallace-2004-homa" />

None of these are reasons to ignore it. They are reasons the system has been slow to adopt it.

## The metabolic progression timeline

Understanding the typical progression from healthy to diabetic makes the case for early testing. Timing varies from person to person — in the Whitehall II cohort, insulin sensitivity fell steeply over the 5 years before diagnosis, while fasting glucose climbed sharply only in the last 3<Cite id="tabak-2009-whitehall-trajectories" /> — so treat the years below as a rough sketch:

1. **Years 0–5**: Insulin rises. Glucose stays normal. Standard tests show nothing wrong.
2. **Years 5–10**: Fasting glucose creeps to 100–110 mg/dL. [HbA1c reaches 5.7%](/blog/hba1c-5-7-pre-diabetic). Now labeled "pre-diabetic."
3. **Years 10–15**: Pancreatic beta cells begin to fail. Glucose crosses 126 mg/dL. Diabetes is diagnosed.

Fasting insulin detects the problem at stage 1. Fasting glucose detects it at stage 2. HbA1c confirms it at stage 2–3.

By the time you are diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, you may already have lost **much of your beta cell function** — over 80%, by one leading estimate.<Cite id="defronzo-2009-banting" /> This is not a sudden event. It is a slow decline that fasting insulin could have flagged a decade earlier.<Cite id="dankner-2009-hyperinsulinemia" />

## How to lower fasting insulin

### Exercise — the most powerful tool

Exercise is one of the most effective ways to improve insulin sensitivity:<Cite id="richter-2013-exercise-glut4" />

- **Resistance training** builds muscle mass, and muscle is the primary tissue for insulin-driven glucose disposal.<Cite id="defronzo-2009-banting" /> More muscle means more capacity to absorb glucose without needing high insulin.<Cite id="srikanthan-2011-muscle-mass" />
- **Aerobic exercise** (especially zone 2 training at 60–70% max heart rate) improves mitochondrial function and fat oxidation.<Cite id="toledo-2007-exercise-mitochondria" />
- **High-intensity interval training (HIIT)** acutely improves GLUT4 transporter activity on muscle cells.<Cite id="richter-2013-exercise-glut4" />

Even a single session of exercise improves insulin sensitivity for up to 48 hours.<Cite id="mikines-1988-exercise-insulin-action" /> Consistency compounds the effect.

### Dietary strategies

- **Reduce refined carbohydrates and sugar** — these cause the largest insulin spikes<Cite id="holt-1997-insulin-index" />
- **Prioritize protein and healthy fats** — both raise glucose less than carbohydrate-dominant meals, though protein-rich foods still trigger a sizable insulin response<Cite id="holt-1997-insulin-index" />
- **Increase fiber intake** — soluble (viscous) fiber slows glucose absorption; in trials in type 2 diabetes it lowered HOMA-IR<Cite id="jovanovski-2019-viscous-fiber" />
- **Consider time-restricted eating** — in a small controlled trial, an early 6-hour eating window improved insulin sensitivity in men with prediabetes, even without weight loss<Cite id="sutton-2018-etrf" />
- **Limit fructose** — particularly from sugary beverages, which raised liver fat production and lowered insulin sensitivity in a 10-week trial<Cite id="stanhope-2009-fructose" />

### Sleep and stress

- **Poor sleep** (under 6 hours or fragmented) increases insulin resistance within days.<Cite id="buxton-2010-sleep-restriction" /> One study showed a single night of sleep restriction to 4 hours reduced insulin sensitivity by 25%.<Cite id="donga-2010-sleep-insulin" />
- **Chronic stress** elevates cortisol, which directly antagonizes insulin action. Cortisol tells the liver to release glucose, requiring more insulin to compensate.<Cite id="rizza-1982-cortisol" />

### Body composition

Visceral fat (the fat around your organs) is uniquely inflammatory and insulin-resistance-promoting.<Cite id="neeland-2019-visceral-fat" /><Cite id="kahn-2006-obesity-insulin-resistance" /> Losing even 5–10% of body weight in people with excess visceral fat can markedly improve insulin sensitivity in liver, muscle and fat tissue.<Cite id="magkos-2016-weight-loss" />

## When to test fasting insulin

Consider asking your doctor for a fasting insulin test if you have:

- A family history of type 2 diabetes
- A waist-to-hip ratio above 0.85 (women) or 0.90 (men)
- Elevated triglycerides or low HDL-C
- PCOS or irregular menstrual cycles
- Difficulty losing weight despite caloric restriction
- Fatigue after meals or frequent sugar cravings
- An HbA1c between 5.4–5.6% (high-normal)

Or simply if you want an earlier signal of metabolic health than fasting glucose alone.

In the US, you can [order a fasting insulin test directly](/blog/blood-test-without-doctor) through services like Quest Diagnostics or LabCorp without a physician order.
